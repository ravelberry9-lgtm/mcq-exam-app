Lesson 2 — Non-Renewable Energy: Sources, Capacities and Energy Security

APPSC Group-II Mains · Paper-II Section-B · Sub-topic 2 — Energy Management · MASTER PLAN ST2, Lesson 2 of 6 · English draft — bilingual (Telugu/Tenglish) pass to follow · current-affairs references, where any appear, must be re-verified before the exam

Lesson 1 established that just under half of India's installed generation capacity is thermal, and that "installed capacity," "demand met," and "generation" are three different numbers that must never be used interchangeably. This lesson opens that thermal fleet up and asks what it is actually made of — coal overwhelmingly, with lignite, gas, and a separate nuclear track alongside it — and then turns to the harder question the syllabus groups under the same clause: how secure is India's non-renewable energy supply, and what does the state actually plan to do about the biggest single source of India's carbon emissions, coal itself? Four institutions anchor this lesson — Coal India Limited, NLC India Limited, NPCIL, and the NITI Aayog Just Transition Committee — and every capacity or percentage figure below carries the date it was pulled from, its source, and — where sourcing is anything less than a direct primary read — an explicit tier label, because in this topic more than almost any other, a number without a date and a known source tier is not a fact worth remembering.
2A — Coal: Institutions, Mining Reform, Reserves and Installed Capacity
Four separate numbers get discussed under "coal," and APPSC-style objective questions are built precisely to test whether a candidate can keep them apart: how much coal is in the ground (reserves), how much is dug up each year (production), who mines it and under what legal arrangement (institutional structure), and how much of India's electricity-generating machinery runs on it (installed thermal capacity). These are four different quantities, measured in different units, that happen to share the word "coal."
Coal India Limited (CIL), headquartered at Kolkata, is a Maharatna central public sector enterprise under the Ministry of Coal and by far the dominant producer of raw coal in India. Its operations are carried out through subsidiary coalfield companies (among them Bharat Coking Coal Ltd, Central Coalfields, Eastern Coalfields, Mahanadi Coalfields, Northern Coalfields, South Eastern Coalfields, and Western Coalfields), plus North Eastern Coalfields and the Central Mine Planning and Design Institute (CMPDI) for planning and exploration support.

Why did the government open coal mining to private, commercial players in 2020?
Coal mining in India had been a near-total public-sector preserve since the Coal Mines Nationalisation Act era of the early 1970s; private companies could mine coal only for captive use — consumption by their own linked plant (a cement, steel, or power company mining coal solely to feed its own factory, never to sell on the open market). In 2020, as part of the Atmanirbhar Bharat reform package, the government launched commercial coal mining auctions, opening coal mining to private players on a fully commercial basis — meaning a private company could, for the first time since nationalisation, mine coal and sell it freely in the open market rather than being restricted to feeding one captive end-use plant. This was presented as ending coal mining's roughly five-decade public-sector monopoly at the mining stage (distinct from CIL's continuing dominance in actual production volume, which persists regardless of the legal reform).
TRAP — captive vs commercial (defined above) is a different question from "was Coal India Limited itself privatised?"
Having distinguished captive mining from commercial mining above, do not let a third, separate claim quietly ride in alongside them: neither reform privatised Coal India Limited. CIL remains a public-sector Maharatna and still produces roughly three-fourths of India's coal (§ above, the CIL production-share CURRENT box); the 2020 commercial-mining reform added new private bidders competing for fresh blocks, it did not transfer any part of CIL's own existing mines or operations to private ownership. An exam option reading "the 2020 reforms privatised Coal India Limited" is testing exactly this distinction: captive-vs-commercial is a question about what a private allottee is permitted to do with the coal it mines, while "was CIL privatised" is a question about who owns an existing PSU's assets — a correct answer to the first says nothing about the second, and conflating the two is the trap.

2A(i) — Lignite: NLC India Limited
Lignite ("brown coal") is a lower-rank, lower-calorific-value coal, geologically younger and softer than the bituminous coal CIL mostly mines, and concentrated in a much smaller number of Indian states.
NLC India Limited (formerly Neyveli Lignite Corporation), headquartered at Neyveli in Tamil Nadu, is a Navratna central public sector enterprise under the Ministry of Coal and India's principal lignite-mining and lignite-based power generation company. India's lignite deposits are geographically concentrated rather than widely spread: Tamil Nadu (the Neyveli/Cauvery basin fields, historically the largest single concentration and the seat of NLC India's original operations), Gujarat (Kutch and South Gujarat basins), and Rajasthan (Barmer and the Bikaner–Nagaur basin) between them account for the great majority of India's known lignite reserves, with smaller deposits in Puducherry, Jammu & Kashmir, and West Bengal. This state-concentration pattern — unlike coal, which is spread across a wider central-eastern belt (Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, West Bengal, Madhya Pradesh) — is itself a recurring "match the resource to the state" objective-question pattern.

2B — Natural Gas and Petroleum
Gas and petroleum are grouped together here because both illustrate the same underlying vulnerability — India's non-renewable energy supply for both is substantially import-dependent — but they show up in the power system in very different ways: gas is a (small, and importantly underused) share of installed electricity capacity, while petroleum barely features in electricity generation at all and instead dominates the transport-fuel economy.
CURRENT — Gas-based installed capacity
FACT: India's gas-based (natural gas) installed electricity-generation capacity. VALUE: 20,122.42 MW — ≈3.6% of India's entire installed generation capacity (all sources combined). Gas reaches non-power consumers mainly through the City Gas Distribution (CGD) network — the system of pipelines and retail outlets that supplies PNG (Piped Natural Gas, for domestic and commercial/industrial use) and CNG (Compressed Natural Gas, for vehicles) directly to end users in a licensed geographical area. CGD networks are licensed and regulated by the Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board (PNGRB), which conducts periodic competitive bidding rounds authorising companies to build and operate CGD networks in specific "geographical areas" (GAs) across the country — the network has expanded considerably in coverage over successive bidding rounds, though exact current GA-count figures move with each new round and should be re-verified against PNGRB's own bidding-round data before being quoted as a fact.
A generator you built but rarely switch on
Imagine buying a large diesel generator for your house, capable of powering the whole building — that is its installed capacity. If diesel then becomes too expensive or too hard to source, you might run that generator only a few hours a month instead of continuously. The generator's capability has not shrunk — it is still rated for the same output — but its actual use has collapsed. India's gas-based power fleet went through almost exactly this, at national scale, once domestic gas supply fell short of what the plants were built to consume.
Installed capacity ≠ utilized capacity — the Plant Load Factor (PLF) concept
Plant Load Factor (PLF) is the ratio of the electricity a plant actually generates over a period to the electricity it could have generated if it had run at its full rated (installed) capacity for that entire period, expressed as a percentage. A plant can have a large, perfectly healthy installed capacity and still run at a very low PLF if it lacks fuel, lacks buyers for its power, or is simply not economically competitive enough to be dispatched — installed capacity is a statement about what a plant can do; PLF is a statement about what it actually did.
TRAP — the gas-based "stranded capacity" figures below are a HISTORICAL ILLUSTRATION, not a current fact
A Parliamentary Standing Committee report on stressed/non-performing assets in gas-based power plants (submitted 4 January 2019, reporting on conditions largely through 2017-18) found that of India's then roughly 24.9 GW of gas-based installed capacity (about 7% of the country's then-total installed capacity of roughly 345 GW), some 14.30 GW — about 57% of all gas-based capacity — was "stranded": built, connected to the grid, and yet sitting largely idle for want of adequate, affordable domestic gas supply. Over the same broad period, the fleet's average Plant Load Factor fell from about 67% (2009-10) to about 24% at the time of the report. These figures are 2017-18 vintage and are taught here purely as the clearest available worked illustration of "installed capacity ≠ utilized capacity" — they must NOT be quoted as today's gas-fleet PLF or stranding figure without re-verifying a current CEA PLF report first.

2C — Nuclear Power
Nuclear power occupies a small share of India's installed capacity by MW, but a disproportionately large share of the syllabus's attention, because it comes bundled with a genuinely distinctive piece of Indian science policy: a three-stage strategy conceived specifically to convert India's uranium scarcity and thorium abundance into a long-term advantage rather than a permanent constraint.
Nuclear Power Corporation of India Limited (NPCIL), a public sector enterprise under the Department of Atomic Energy (DAE), remains India's dominant operator of nuclear power plants and the entity that actually builds, owns, and operates every commercial nuclear reactor generating electricity in the country today (a distinct, narrower role from DAE itself, which is the parent government department, or from research/prototype entities such as BHAVINI, discussed below). Until December 2025, this was also NPCIL's exclusive legal position — the only entity permitted by law to hold that role at all. That legal monopoly has since had a statutory opening created against it by Parliament passing the SHANTI Act, 2025, though the effective commencement date of its provisions should still be verified from the Gazette; see the SHANTI Act, 2025 box immediately below for exactly what has changed in law, and what has (and has not) changed on the ground.
CURRENT — the SHANTI Act, 2025 creates the statutory opening for private/JV nuclear operation (commencement details to verify)
FACT: Legal framework governing who may build, own, and operate nuclear power plants in India. VALUE: Parliament has enacted the SHANTI Act, 2025, to repeal and replace both the Atomic Energy Act, 1962 and the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage (CLND) Act, 2010 with a single consolidated law — "Sustainable Harnessing and Advancement of Nuclear Energy for Transforming India." For the first time since Independence, it creates the statutory framework under which Indian-incorporated private companies and government-private joint ventures may be licensed to build, own, and operate nuclear power reactors — subject to commencement of the relevant provisions and subordinate licensing rules — and separately provides for the Atomic Energy Regulatory Board (AERB) to hold independent statutory status under the parent Act (previously AERB existed only as a body created by executive/DAE order, not under its own Act); verify the commencement provision before describing either change as already operative on the ground. Strategic activities — fissile-material/nuclear-fuel production, heavy-water manufacture, and spent-fuel/radioactive-waste custody — remain under exclusive government control even under the new Act. MEASURE/DEFINITION: a change in the legal licensing framework, not, by itself, a change in who is actually operating a plant on the ground — no private- or JV-operated reactor yet exists; NPCIL continues, in practice, to build, own, and operate every commercial reactor currently generating power (§ above — 8,780 MW installed, 20 of 24 reactors operational). JURISDICTION SCOPE: All-India, civil nuclear power sector. STATUS DATE: passed Lok Sabha 17.12.2025; passed Rajya Sabha 18.12.2025. Why India could not simply "buy more uranium" and stop there
India holds only modest, low-grade domestic uranium reserves but one of the largest thorium-bearing mineral endowments in the world. Homi J. Bhabha, framing India's nuclear strategy in the 1950s, treated this asymmetry not as a dead end but as a design problem: build a fuel cycle that starts on the uranium India does have, and steadily bootstraps its way toward the thorium it has in abundance. That bootstrapping plan is the three-stage nuclear programme.
The Three-Stage Nuclear Programme (Homi Bhabha's strategy)
Stage 1 — Pressurised Heavy Water Reactors (PHWRs): fuelled by natural (unenriched) uranium, using heavy water as both moderator and coolant — a technology choice suited to a country with limited uranium-enrichment capability at the time the strategy was conceived. This is the workhorse of India's current operating reactor fleet. Stage 2 — Fast Breeder Reactors (FBRs): fuelled by plutonium recovered from Stage-1 spent fuel; breeding inside the reactor relies substantially on fertile Uranium-238 in the blanket/core to produce additional plutonium, while thorium can also be irradiated in the blanket to progressively breed fissile Uranium-233 — the fuel bridge toward Stage 3. Stage 3 — Thorium/U-233-based reactors: advanced reactors designed to run on the U-233 bred during Stage 2, finally putting India's large thorium reserves to direct use as reactor fuel rather than merely as a breeding blanket. The strategy is explicitly sequential — each stage's output becomes the next stage's input — which is precisely why it is taught as a "programme," not three unrelated reactor types.
Is Stage 2 still just a plan on paper?
No — India's Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR) at Kalpakkam, Tamil Nadu, built and operated by BHAVINI (Bharatiya Nabhikiya Vidyut Nigam Limited, a DAE public sector enterprise) with design and technology development by the Indira Gandhi Centre for Atomic Research (IGCAR), attained first criticality on 6 April 2026, per a Department of Atomic Energy press release. DAE itself described the milestone as moving India "closer to realising the full potential of its three-stage nuclear power programme," with fast-breeder technology serving as "the vital bridge between the current fleet of pressurised heavy water reactors and the future deployment of thorium-based reactors." This is a live, dated milestone in an otherwise decades-old strategy — treat "PFBR has achieved first criticality" as itself a current-affairs fact requiring its own re-verification of status (from criticality to full commercial power operation is a further, separate step) before the exam.
CAUTION — do NOT memorise a % figure for India's share of world thorium reserves
India is widely and correctly described as holding one of the world's largest thorium reserves, concentrated chiefly in monazite-bearing beach-sand placer deposits along the Kerala coast (with further such deposits along other coastal stretches, including parts of Tamil Nadu, Odisha, and Andhra Pradesh) — monazite being a rare-earth-and-thorium-bearing mineral sand. This qualitative fact — large reserves, Kerala-coast geography, monazite as the host mineral, and its role as the rationale for Stage 3 of the nuclear programme — is stable and safe to state. A specific percentage of world thorium reserves, however, is genuinely disputed across sources: coaching material, news reports, and even some government-adjacent sources quote conflicting figures for this share, and no single authoritative DAE/IAEA primary figure could be confirmed. This book deliberately does not state one. If an MCQ option presents a specific world-share percentage for Indian thorium as a statement to be judged true or false, treat it as unverifiable trivia rather than a figure to have memorised — the safer, exam-tested ground is the qualitative rationale (uranium-scarce, thorium-rich, hence the three-stage strategy), not a number.
Why does a nuclear capacity "target" go stale faster than a coal capacity figure?
Because a target is usually phrased relative to a stated baseline ("from X MW today to Y MW by year Z"), and if the baseline itself moves before the target is reissued, the target quietly becomes internally inconsistent — a trap nuclear-capacity targets are particularly prone to. In a written reply to Rajya Sabha dated 28 November 2024, Dr Jitendra Singh (MoS, PMO/DAE) stated a plan to raise nuclear capacity "to 22,480 MW by 2031-32 from the present installed capacity of 8,180 MW." But by December 2025 — little over a year later — actual installed capacity had already reached 8,780 MW (§ above), 600 MW past the baseline the November-2024 target was computed against. The target figure (22,480 MW) itself was not wrong when stated; it is simply already resting on a baseline that current reality has moved past, without the target having been formally restated against the newer baseline. Treat any nuclear-capacity "target" figure in coaching material or news reports as provisional in exactly this way, and always check which baseline year and value it was computed against before repeating it.
A newer, larger, and separately dynamic target: 100 GW by 2047
Distinct from the November-2024 statement above, a considerably larger nuclear-capacity ambition — commonly reported as a target of 100 GW of nuclear capacity by 2047 — has been associated with the government's Nuclear Energy Mission (linked to Union Budget 2025-26 announcements envisaging amendments to the Atomic Energy Act, 1962 and the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010 to enable private-sector and foreign investment in nuclear power). This is flagged here explicitly as an aspirational, actively-evolving policy target rather than a settled installed-capacity fact — its precise wording, legal status, and any funding/legislative follow-through must be re-verified against a primary DAE/PIB/Ministry of Finance source before being quoted as confirmed government policy. A more fully dated, sourced version of this same 100 GW/2047 target, with intermediate waypoints, now exists — see the CURRENT box below.
Bharat Small Modular Reactors (BSMR) give the 100 GW/2047 ambition above a concrete near-term mechanism rather than leaving it as a bare aspiration. Union Budget 2025-26, under the Nuclear Energy Mission, allocated ₹20,000 crore for the research, design, development and deployment of indigenous Small Modular Reactors, with a stated goal of operationalising at least five indigenous SMRs by 2033. Three distinct reactor designs are under DAE/BARC/NPCIL development: the BSMR-200 (a pressurised heavy water reactor — PHWR, not an ordinary light-water PWR — derived from India's existing IPHWR-220 design, rated at 200 MWe, matching its name), a smaller SMR-55 (≈55 MWe; its precise reactor-type/technology base was not independently confirmed in this session and should be verified against DAE/BARC before assuming it is either a PWR or PHWR variant), and a gas-cooled high-temperature reactor (up to 5 MWth) intended as a heat source for hydrogen production via thermochemical cycles rather than for electricity generation.

2D — Energy Security, Coal Phase-Down, and Just Transition
The four sources covered above now combine into the syllabus's real underlying question for this lesson: how secure is India's non-renewable energy supply, and what is the government's stated plan for the one non-renewable fuel it has committed, at the international level, to eventually wind down?
Where exactly is India's energy-security vulnerability concentrated?
Not evenly across all four fuels. Coal is the strong point — India holds among the world's largest domestic coal reserves (§2A) and CIL alone supplies roughly three-quarters of a production base exceeding a billion tonnes a year, giving coal-based electricity a comparatively secure domestic supply chain, even though India — despite being the world's second-largest coal producer — still imports meaningful volumes of coal each year, chiefly high-grade coking coal for steel-making (where domestic reserves and grades are limited) and some thermal coal for coastal or blending needs; exact current import-volume figures should be re-pulled from Ministry of Coal/DGCI&S trade data before being quoted, since no verified current tonnage is given here. Petroleum and natural gas are the weak points: a record ≈88.7% of crude oil (FY2025-26, provisional) is import-dependent, and a rising share of natural gas consumption is import-dependent too — reported at roughly 46% (PRS, up from 28% a decade earlier), though PPAC's own differently-defined figure and a Lok Sabha Estimates Committee report both put the current level higher (see the CURRENT box above for the full source conflict); the exact percentage is contested but the underlying trend and direction are not. This asymmetry — secure in coal, exposed in oil and gas — is the single most useful one-line "energy security" answer this lesson supports, and it is exactly the kind of statement-based contrast APPSC-style objective questions are built to test.
Why import dependence is a strategic vulnerability, not just an economics footnote
A high import-dependence ratio exposes the economy to global crude/LNG price volatility (directly affecting the trade deficit and the rupee's exchange rate), to shipping-route and chokepoint risk (the Strait of Hormuz being the most cited example for crude), and to the diplomatic leverage of supplier and transit states. This is the concrete, applied reason "energy security" appears as its own named thread across this book's Lesson 3 (National Energy Policy) rather than being treated as a purely descriptive statistic here.
TRAP — coal "phase-DOWN" vs "phase-OUT" (Glasgow Climate Pact, COP26)
At COP26 in Glasgow, the final consensus text — the Glasgow Climate Pact, adopted on 13 November 2021 (the conference's last day, itself extended by a day to reach agreement) — was, by wide reporting, the first COP outcome document ever to explicitly name "coal" at all. Its draft language had called for parties to accelerate the "phase-out" of unabated coal power and inefficient fossil-fuel subsidies. In a last-minute floor amendment, India — supported by China and a number of other developing, coal-dependent countries — moved to soften this to a "phase-down" of unabated coal power (with "phase-out" retained only for the fossil-fuel-subsidies clause, itself qualified as "inefficient" subsidies). Because COP decisions require consensus, the amendment was accepted rather than blocking the whole agreement, over the visible disappointment of several delegations, including small island states and the EU/UK bloc, who had pushed for the stronger "phase-out" language. "Phase-down" = gradual, managed reduction in reliance on unabated coal power over time, without a committed end-date or total elimination. "Phase-out" = a committed, eventual complete elimination. India's position was never a rejection of climate action — it reflected a developing-economy argument about equitable transition timelines given continuing energy-access and development needs, not a claim that coal use should not fall at all.
The NITI Aayog Inter-Ministerial Committee on Just Transition from Coal was constituted in July 2021 — a 14-member committee headed by the Joint Secretary, Ministry of Coal, set up under the Sustainable Growth pillar of the India–US Strategic Clean Energy Partnership. "Just transition", as a term, refers to managing the shift away from coal in a way that protects the workers, communities, and regional economies that currently depend on coal mining and coal-based industry — not simply closing mines and plants and leaving the resulting economic and social disruption unaddressed.
The Committee's institutional framework and findings
The Committee's report recommended a three-tier institutional structure of task forces (spanning national, state, and local/district-coalfield levels) to plan and oversee coal-mine closure activities and the post-closure redevelopment of affected land and communities, rather than leaving closure decisions to a single centralised authority. It also proposed a dedicated, non-lapsable "Green Energy Transition India Fund" to finance the transition in coal-bearing regions, suggested to be capitalised through instruments such as bond issuance, special levies or cesses, fines collected from polluters, and the existing clean energy cess. On the underlying demand question, the report/associated analysis projected that India's coal consumption is likely to peak somewhere between 2035 and 2040 before beginning a gradual decline — a projection, not a fixed date, and one that should be treated as illustrative of the general "coal demand peaks before it declines" shape rather than a number to be quoted as certain.
Managed decline, not a sudden switch-off
Think of "just transition" as the difference between abruptly turning off a factory's power supply mid-shift and instead planning, months in advance, how every worker will be redeployed, retrained, or supported before the switch is finally thrown. Coal phase-down and Just Transition are two halves of the same policy problem: phase-down is the "how much, how fast" question at the national/global level; Just Transition is the "and what happens to the people and places left behind" question at the regional/local level.
2E — Market-Based Instruments for Energy Transition: PAT and the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS)
Beyond the physical fuel-security picture built up in §2A–2D, the state also steers India's energy transition through two linked market-based instruments rather than command-and-control regulation alone. The older of the two, Perform, Achieve and Trade (PAT), turned industrial energy efficiency into a tradable commodity from 2012 onward; the newer, the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS), creates a broader national carbon-market framework capable of extending beyond PAT's narrower energy-efficiency sectors — current coverage is limited to notified sectors/entities, not yet economy-wide — and is now gradually absorbing PAT's most energy-intensive sectors into its own compliance mechanism. Both sit on the same statutory foundation — the Energy Conservation Act, 2001, as substantially amended by the Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022 — and both are administered, in the first instance, by the Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE) under the Ministry of Power.
Perform, Achieve and Trade (PAT) is the flagship scheme of the National Mission for Enhanced Energy Efficiency (NMEEE) — one of the eight missions under the National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC) — administered by BEE. Large energy-intensive facilities above a notified energy-consumption threshold are declared Designated Consumers (DCs) and assigned a mandatory Specific Energy Consumption (energy-per-unit-of-output) reduction target for each multi-year "cycle." A DC that beats its target earns tradable Energy Saving Certificates (ESCerts), which it can sell on the power exchanges (IEX, PXIL — regulated by the CERC); a DC that falls short must buy enough ESCerts to cover the gap, or face a penalty under the Energy Conservation Act. The scheme has run in successive multi-year cycles since 2012, expanding in both sector coverage and Designated-Consumer count with each cycle.

Why did PAT need a bigger sibling scheme at all?
PAT measures and trades energy savings relative to a plant's own past efficiency — a useful lever, but not, by itself, an economy-wide price signal on carbon. The Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022 (Act No. 19 of 2022) inserted an explicit statutory basis for the Central Government to establish a carbon credit trading scheme, defined a "Carbon Credit Certificate," and — critically — empowered the government to bring any person, not only PAT's Designated Consumers, within such a scheme. This is the direct legislative bridge from a narrower industrial-efficiency-trading mechanism (PAT) to a broader, GHG-emission-based trading mechanism (CCTS).
Acting on this amended statutory basis, the Ministry of Power notified the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme, 2023 on 28 June 2023. CCTS runs on two parallel tracks: a Compliance Mechanism, under which energy-intensive industries designated as Obligated Entities are assigned mandatory Greenhouse Gas Emission Intensity (GEI) targets (emissions per unit of output) — entities that beat their target earn tradable Carbon Credit Certificates (CCCs), entities that miss it must buy enough CCCs to cover the shortfall — and a voluntary Offset Mechanism, open to any non-obligated entity (including renewable-energy project developers) that registers an approved emission-reduction activity for credit. Overall governance sits with the National Steering Committee for the Indian Carbon Market, co-chaired by the Secretaries of the Ministry of Power and the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC); MoEFCC formally notifies each sector's GEI targets under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, on the Ministry of Power's recommendation; BEE is the scheme's administrator, running certification and IT infrastructure; the Grid Controller of India Limited (Grid-India) operates the national registry on which every Obligated Entity and voluntary participant must register; and the CERC regulates trading of CCCs on the power exchanges, mirroring its existing role for PAT's ESCerts.
TRAP — PAT is converting INTO CCTS's compliance mechanism, sector by sector — it has not been abolished, and not every PAT sector has moved yet
CCTS's Compliance Mechanism is designed to replace PAT, sector by sector, for facilities that move across — not to run as a wholly separate, unrelated scheme alongside PAT forever. As of the Ministry of Environment's own notifications (below), only some PAT sectors and sub-sectors had been brought under CCTS's compliance obligations: Aluminium, Cement, Chlor-Alkali and Pulp & Paper (moved first, by October 2025) and Petroleum Refineries, Petrochemicals, Textiles and Secondary Aluminium (added January 2026). Thermal power plants — one of PAT's largest and oldest-covered sectors — remain under PAT, not CCTS. An exam option that assumes "PAT has been replaced by CCTS" or "all PAT sectors are now under CCTS" is therefore wrong on both counts: the transition is real, government-confirmed, and sector-by-sector, but it is neither complete nor, on current evidence, extended to every historically PAT-covered sector.

Two certificates, one underlying idea
Both ESCerts (PAT) and CCCs (CCTS) work on the identical carrot-and-stick logic of a tradable permit: beat your target and you get something valuable to sell; miss it and you must buy that same something from someone who beat theirs. The difference is what is being measured and traded — PAT prices energy saved relative to a plant's own past consumption; CCTS prices greenhouse-gas emissions avoided relative to a sector-wide intensity benchmark — which is exactly why CCTS, not PAT, is India's real entry into carbon-market territory, even though PAT quietly built the institutional muscle (Designated Consumers, tradable certificates, power-exchange trading, CERC oversight) that CCTS now reuses.

2F — The National Coal Gasification Mission: Coal as an Import-Substitution Lever
Coal gasification converts coal or lignite into syngas — a mixture chiefly of carbon monoxide and hydrogen — which can then be refined into substitutes for products India currently imports in large volumes: liquefied natural gas (LNG), urea and other nitrogenous fertilisers, ammonia, methanol, and dimethyl ether (DME). The Ministry of Coal launched the National Coal Gasification Mission on 21 September 2021 with a headline target of 100 million tonnes per annum (MTPA) of coal/lignite gasification capacity by 2030 — the same "reduce import dependence on a strategic energy input" logic already taught in §2B for crude oil and natural gas, now applied to coal's downstream chemistry rather than to coal itself (which, as §2A and §2D established, is not an import-dependence problem for India the way oil and gas are).

Why does an energy-security lesson about coal end up talking about LNG and fertiliser imports?
Because coal gasification is the one lever in this lesson that actually starts to move the "secure in coal, exposed in oil and gas" asymmetry (§2D), rather than merely sitting on one side of it. Everywhere else in this lesson, that asymmetry is a fixed backdrop — coal stays the strong point, oil and gas stay the weak points, and nothing changes that pairing. Coal gasification is different: it takes the strength (§2A's coal reserves) and uses it to manufacture direct substitutes for specific imported products — LNG, urea and other nitrogenous fertilisers, ammonia, methanol, DME — so a successful scale-up would, product by product, chip away at exactly the import bill §2B and §2D flag as the vulnerable side of the equation. Two limits keep this from being a full fix rather than a partial one: first, it only ever substitutes for the refined-product imports listed above, not for crude oil itself, which India burns overwhelmingly as a transport fuel with no coal-gasification substitute in this pipeline — the crude-import exposure is untouched. Second, at ≈22.6 MTPA against a 100 MTPA-by-2030 mission target (the CURRENT box above), the mechanism is still a small fraction of its own eventual scale, let alone of the ≈₹2.77 lakh crore import bill it is aimed at. So the asymmetry is not disappearing — it is being worked on, unevenly, from the coal side, and only for the slice of oil-and-gas-linked imports that coal chemistry can actually replace.

★ Quick Facts
  1. Coal-based installed thermal capacity: 224,157.50 MW ≈ 40.6% of all-India installed capacity (CEA, 31.07.2026) — India's single largest source by a wide margin, and now the same as-of-date snapshot Lesson 1 uses.
  2. Coal India Limited (CIL) produced 781.056 MT of India's 1,047.523 MT total coal production in FY2024-25 — ≈74.5%, commonly rounded to "roughly three-fourths."
  3. India's total coal reserve estimate: 400.715 billion tonnes as of 01.04.2025 (GSI/CMPDI National Coal Inventory) — reserves, production, and installed capacity are three different quantities; never conflate them.
  4. 2020 commercial coal-mining reform opened coal mining to private players on a fully commercial (not merely captive) basis for the first time since the nationalisation era — CIL was not privatised; new producers were added alongside it.
  5. Captive mining = private mining restricted to the allottee's own end-use plant; commercial mining (2020) = mining for open-market sale — two distinct reforms, not synonyms.
  6. Lignite: NLC India Limited (Navratna, HQ Neyveli, Tamil Nadu); India's lignite is concentrated chiefly in Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, and Rajasthan. Lignite installed capacity: 6,620 MW ≈ 1.2% (31.07.2026).
  7. Gas-based installed capacity: 20,122.42 MW ≈ 3.6% (31.07.2026) — small in share, and historically prone to severe underutilisation.
  8. City Gas Distribution (CGD) — PNG (domestic/commercial) and CNG (transport) — is licensed and regulated by the PNGRB, not by CEA or the Ministry of Power.
  9. PLF (Plant Load Factor) = actual generation ÷ generation possible at full rated capacity. Installed capacity can be large while PLF is very low — the two are not proxies for each other.
  10. Gas-fleet "stranded capacity" (≈57% of gas capacity, PLF fallen to ≈24%) is a 2017-18-vintage Parliamentary Standing Committee finding — teach it only as a historical illustration of the installed-vs-utilized trap, never as today's figure.
  11. Crude oil import dependence: a record ≈88.7% (FY2025-26, provisional; Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas, Rajya Sabha reply 27.07.2026) — up from 85.5% in FY2021-22.
  12. Natural gas import dependence rose from 28% (2011-12) to 46% (2023-24) — nearly doubling as a share of consumption.
  13. NPCIL remains India's dominant operator of nuclear power plants in practice (a DAE public sector enterprise) — but is no longer the sole entity legally permitted to be one: the SHANTI Act, 2025 (Lok Sabha 17.12.2025, Rajya Sabha 18.12.2025) repealed the Atomic Energy Act 1962 and the CLND Act 2010, created the statutory opening to end that legal monopoly (commencement to verify), opened licensing to private Indian companies and government-private joint ventures, and gave AERB independent statutory status.
  14. Nuclear installed capacity: 8,780 MW; 20 of 24 reactors operational (Dec 2025 Lok Sabha statement; cross-checked against CEA's own 31.07.2026 figure — both show 8,780 MW).
  15. Three-stage nuclear programme (Homi Bhabha): Stage 1 PHWR (natural uranium) → Stage 2 FBR (plutonium-fuelled; U-238 blanket breeds more plutonium, thorium blanket breeds U-233) → Stage 3 thorium/U-233 reactors.
  16. PFBR at Kalpakkam (BHAVINI/IGCAR) attained first criticality on 06.04.2026 — DAE calls it the "vital bridge" between Stage 1 and Stage 3.
  17. Do NOT quote a % figure for India's world thorium-reserve share — genuinely disputed across every source; teach only the qualitative fact (large reserves, Kerala-coast monazite sands).
  18. Coal "phase-down" (not "phase-out") of unabated coal power is the actual language of the Glasgow Climate Pact, adopted 13.11.2021 at COP26, after a last-minute India(+China)-led amendment.
  19. NITI Aayog's Inter-Ministerial Committee on Just Transition from Coal was constituted July 2021; recommended a three-tier (national/state/local) institutional framework and a dedicated Green Energy Transition India Fund; coal demand is projected to peak roughly 2035-2040 before declining.
  20. PAT (Perform, Achieve and Trade), BEE's flagship industrial energy-efficiency trading scheme since 2012: 1,333 Designated Consumers across 13 sectors, ≈55% of India's industrial energy consumption, ≈25.78 Mtoe cumulative verified savings. Beating-target DCs earn tradable Energy Saving Certificates (ESCerts); shortfall DCs must buy them.
  21. CCTS (Carbon Credit Trading Scheme), 2023, notified 28.06.2023 under the Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022, runs a mandatory Compliance Mechanism (Obligated Entities, Greenhouse Gas Emission Intensity targets, tradable Carbon Credit Certificates) alongside a voluntary Offset Mechanism; 490 Obligated Entities notified as of 13.01.2026.
  22. PAT is converting INTO CCTS's compliance mechanism, sector by sector, not being abolished: Aluminium, Cement, Chlor-Alkali and Pulp & Paper moved by October 2025; Petroleum Refineries, Petrochemicals, Textiles and Secondary Aluminium added January 2026 — but thermal power plants, one of PAT's largest sectors, remain under PAT.
  23. National Coal Gasification Mission (Ministry of Coal, launched 21.09.2021): target 100 MTPA of coal/lignite gasification capacity by 2030; ≈22.6 MTPA operational/under implementation as of the latest reply; a new ₹37,500 crore "Scheme for Promotion of Surface Coal/Lignite Gasification Projects" (Cabinet-approved 13.05.2026) succeeds an earlier ₹8,500 crore (Jan 2024) scheme — an import-substitution lever for LNG, urea, ammonia, methanol and DME.
  24. Bharat Small Modular Reactors (BSMR): ₹20,000 crore R&D-and-deployment outlay under the Nuclear Energy Mission (Union Budget 2025-26), targeting at least 5 indigenous SMRs operational by 2033, across three designs — BSMR-200 (200 MWe PHWR, derived from the existing IPHWR-220 design), SMR-55 (≈55 MWe; reactor-type not independently confirmed), and a gas-cooled high-temperature reactor (≤5 MWth, for hydrogen production) — none operational yet.

TRAP — where an examiner twists it
Looks like thisActually this
"Installed capacity tells you how much electricity a source actually supplies"Installed capacity is only the rated ceiling; actual supply depends on PLF/utilization — the gas-fleet stranded-capacity episode (57% stranded, PLF to ≈24% in 2017-18) is the classic, if now historical, illustration
"The Glasgow Climate Pact commits the world to phasing OUT coal power"The adopted text says "phase-DOWN" of unabated coal power (India+China-led amendment, 13.11.2021); "phase-out" survives only for "inefficient" fossil-fuel subsidies, a separate clause
"CIL's ≈74.5% production share means coal supplies ≈74.5% of India's electricity"Two different denominators: CIL's share is of national coal production (tonnes); coal's 40.6% figure is its share of installed electricity capacity (MW) — do not swap one percentage for the other
"2020 reforms privatised Coal India Limited"The 2020 reform opened commercial mining to new private players; CIL itself remained a public-sector Maharatna and still produces roughly three-fourths of national output
"Captive mining and commercial mining are the same policy"Captive mining restricts output to the allottee's own linked plant; commercial mining (2020) permits open-market sale — a genuine, distinct reform, not a renaming
"India's share of world thorium reserves is [any specific %]"Genuinely disputed across every available source; this book states only the qualitative fact (large reserves, Kerala-coast monazite) and no percentage — treat any quoted % as unverified trivia
"20 of 24 reactors operational means 4 reactors are permanently shut"The gap typically reflects reactors temporarily offline for scheduled refuelling/maintenance or life-extension work at the stated date, not a permanent closure — re-verify the current ratio before citing it as static
"A nuclear capacity target, once announced, stays valid until its target year"A target is only as good as its stated baseline; the Nov-2024 statement's own 8,180 MW baseline was already 600 MW stale by Dec-2025's actual 8,780 MW — always check the baseline date, not just the target figure
"India is self-sufficient in coal and therefore never imports it"Despite being the world's second-largest coal producer with large domestic reserves, India still imports meaningful volumes annually — chiefly high-grade coking coal for steel-making, where domestic reserves/grades are limited
"Rising renewable capacity means India's overall energy import-dependence is falling across the board"Electricity-sector import dependence is falling (more domestic solar/wind), but natural gas import dependence is simultaneously rising sharply (28%→46%) — the two trends run in opposite directions and must not be merged into one storyline
"NPCIL is, by law, the only entity that can ever operate a nuclear power plant in India"True only up to December 2025 — the SHANTI Act, 2025 (Lok Sabha 17.12.2025, Rajya Sabha 18.12.2025) repealed the Atomic Energy Act 1962 and the CLND Act 2010 and opened licensing to private Indian companies and government-private JVs, with AERB gaining independent statutory status; NPCIL still operates every reactor actually running today, but the legal monopoly itself has ended
"CCTS has replaced PAT" / "all PAT sectors are now under CCTS"CCTS's Compliance Mechanism is absorbing PAT sector by sector (Aluminium/Cement/Chlor-Alkali/Pulp & Paper by Oct 2025; Petroleum Refineries/Petrochemicals/Textiles/Secondary Aluminium by Jan 2026), but thermal power plants — one of PAT's largest, oldest-covered sectors — remain under PAT, not CCTS; the transition is real but neither complete nor universal
"The National Coal Gasification Mission's 100 MTPA target has already been met"100 MTPA by 2030 is the mission's target, not its current status; only ≈22.6 MTPA of gasification capacity was operational or under implementation as of the latest Rajya Sabha reply — a large gap remains between target and progress
"The SHANTI Act, 2025 means a private or JV company is now operating a nuclear reactor in India"The SHANTI Act (Dec 2025) opened the legal licensing framework to private Indian companies and government-private JVs, but no private- or JV-operated reactor had yet been licensed, financed, or begun construction — "licensing is now legally possible" and "a private/JV reactor is operating" are two different, not-yet-equal claims, the same trap the Bharat Small Modular Reactor programme's 2033 target illustrates from the other direction (funded and designed, but none of the three BSMR designs is operational yet)
Why this lesson matters
Every later policy lesson in this book assumes the reader can already tell reserves from production, installed capacity from utilized capacity, and an announced target from a currently-achieved figure — because almost every subsequent lesson's "energy security" and "policy achievement" claims rest on exactly these distinctions being kept straight. The coal phase-down/phase-out and thorium-percentage traps built here are also two of this lesson's most well-established, high-value examiner traps: the Glasgow Climate Pact's adopted wording commits parties only to a "phase-down" — never a "phase-out" — of unabated coal power (with "phase-out" reserved solely for "inefficient" fossil-fuel subsidies), and no specific percentage of world thorium reserves attributed to India should ever be memorised or quoted, since that figure is genuinely disputed across every available source and this book deliberately states none. Get both of those two points right, and a large share of this lesson's exam value is already secured.
