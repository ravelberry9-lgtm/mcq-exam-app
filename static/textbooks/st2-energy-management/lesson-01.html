Lesson 1 — India's Energy Landscape: Capacity, Demand and Classification

APPSC Group-II Mains · Paper-II Section-B · Sub-topic 2 — Energy Management · MASTER PLAN ST2, Lesson 1 of 6 · English draft — bilingual (Telugu/Tenglish) pass to follow · current-affairs references, where any appear, must be re-verified before the exam

The syllabus clause "Installed Energy Capacities and Demand in India" sounds like a simple numbers-memorisation exercise, but it is actually the single most trap-dense corner of this entire book. Four different quantities — installed capacity, generation, demand (energy requirement/supplied), and peak demand — are routinely collapsed into one in casual reading, and examiners know it. A second, quieter trap sits inside the numbers themselves: the Central Electricity Authority's (CEA) own category "RES" excludes large hydro even though large hydro is physically renewable; CEA's broader "non-fossil" category then adds both large hydro AND nuclear (which is not renewable, but is also not a fossil fuel) back in. This lesson builds the vocabulary and the worked numeric examples that every later lesson in this book — coal and gas capacity (Lesson 2), the two similarly-named policy documents (Lesson 3), and the renewable-capacity lessons (Lessons 5–6) — will assume you already have exactly right.
1A — Conventional/Non-Conventional and Renewable/Non-Renewable: Two Different Axes
Energy sources are classified along two separate axes, and the single most common classification error is to treat them as one axis with two names. The first axis — conventional vs non-conventional — is about how long a source has been in large-scale commercial use: coal, oil, natural gas, large hydro, and nuclear power are "conventional" simply because they have powered grids for decades; solar, wind, small hydro, biomass, tidal and geothermal are "non-conventional" because large-scale commercial use is comparatively recent. The second axis — renewable vs non-renewable — is about whether nature replenishes the resource on a human timescale: solar, wind, biomass, and moving water are renewable because the sun, wind, and hydrological cycle keep resupplying them; coal, oil, natural gas, and nuclear fuel (uranium/thorium) are non-renewable because a finite geological stock is drawn down and not meaningfully replaced within a human lifetime.
This NCERT Class 10 ("Sources of Energy") foundation is tested less for its own sake than for the classification traps it sets up. Large hydro power is conventional (long-established, grid-scale since the early 20th century) yet renewable (the hydrological cycle refills the reservoir). Nuclear power is conventional (established since the 1950s–60s) yet non-renewable (uranium/thorium are finite mined resources). Meanwhile solar and wind are both non-conventional and renewable — the combination most people default to as "the" definition of renewable energy, forgetting that renewability and conventional-ness are independent properties. Worth flagging honestly: "conventional/non-conventional" is a widely-used Indian administrative and school-textbook classification (NCERT-style), not a universal scientific taxonomy — the 2×2 grid below is still worth learning exactly as India's exam-setters use it.

Why does this 2×2 grid matter more than it looks?
Because it is the conceptual seed of the single biggest trap in this lesson, coming up next in §1B: the Central Electricity Authority classifies large hydro and nuclear as "conventional," not as "RES," even though large hydro is renewable in the strict physical sense. A student who has only memorised "renewable = solar+wind+hydro" will misread every CEA capacity table that follows.
One further terminology note worth flagging early: India's own nodal ministry has gone through three distinct stages, not one, and coaching material frequently collapses all three into a single wrong date. The Department of Non-Conventional Energy Sources (DNES) was created in 1982; it was upgraded from Department to full Ministry status — becoming the Ministry of Non-Conventional Energy Sources — in 1992; and it was renamed the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE), the name it carries today, in October 2006. The 1992 event is a status upgrade (Department → Ministry), not the ministry's founding — a reminder that "non-conventional energy" and "renewable energy" have been used near-interchangeably in Indian official usage even though the two words strictly describe different axes above. This terminology drift, and the deeper installed-capacity-vs-generation-share trap that follows from it, is built out fully in Lesson 5 once the full renewable-capacity tables are on the table; here it is enough to recognise that the two phrases are not always used with technical precision even in government documents.
1B — Total Installed Capacity, the Source-Wise Mix, and CEA's "RES" vs "Non-Fossil" Boundary
"Installed capacity" is the nameplate maximum a power plant is rated to produce under specified/nameplate conditions — a ceiling, not a promise of how much electricity actually flows, and not a claim that this output is sustained continuously (a solar or wind plant's nameplate MW, in particular, is reached only intermittently, when sunlight/wind conditions allow). Every month, the Central Electricity Authority (CEA) — the technical arm of the Ministry of Power that maintains India's official capacity and generation statistics — publishes an all-India installed-capacity report, broken down by fuel/source. This is the anchor table for the entire Energy Management topic, and it is also the single most volatile set of figures in this book.

Think of installed capacity as the number of seats in a stadium
Installed capacity is like the total seating capacity printed on a stadium's certificate — a fixed ceiling that does not change match to match. How many seats are actually filled on a given evening (generation), how many tickets fans wanted (demand/energy requirement), and how full the stadium was at its single most-crowded moment of the night (peak demand) are three completely different numbers that all depend on the seating-capacity ceiling without ever equalling it. §1C builds this out with real CEA figures.

TRAP — CEA's "RES" deliberately excludes large hydro and nuclear
In CEA's own classification, "RES" (Renewable Energy Sources) = small hydro + wind + solar + biomass/bio-power + other new renewables — but explicitly EXCLUDES large hydro power (conventionally, hydro projects above 25 MW) and nuclear power. CEA instead reports a separate, broader category, "non-fossil," defined as RES + large hydro + nuclear. So: RES ⊂ non-fossil, and the difference between the two percentages is made up entirely of large-hydro and nuclear capacity — sources that are perfectly genuine parts of India's clean-energy story but are, by CEA's own bookkeeping convention, not counted inside "RES." A statement like "renewable energy is 54% of India's installed capacity" is therefore a conflation error dressed up as a fact — 54.44% is the non-fossil figure, not the RES figure. CEA's own summary cover-sheet occasionally headlines a broader "RES (including Hydro)" rollup (~52-53% of capacity); the detailed regional/ownership tables' "RES*(MNRE)" column — the one this lesson's 43.42% figure is drawn from — is the one that excludes large hydro, and is the convention PIB/MNRE releases also follow.
Working the July-2026 figures through gives the worked numeric example this exact trap needs. Non-fossil capacity ≈ 54.44% of 551,994.76 MW ≈ 300,506 MW — a direct back-calculation from CEA's published non-fossil percentage. Large hydro (52,064.66 MW) plus nuclear (8,780.00 MW) — both directly CEA-published figures, detailed in the box just below, not back-calculated — sum to ≈60,844.66 MW; subtracting this directly-published sum from the back-calculated non-fossil total gives RES capacity ≈ 300,506 − 60,845 ≈ 239,661 MW. This 239,661 MW figure is obtained by subtraction against the directly-published large-hydro-plus-nuclear total, not by multiplying CEA's own rounded 43.42% RES percentage directly against 551,994.76 MW — that direct multiplication instead gives ≈239,676 MW, about 15 MW higher, the small gap explained by 43.42% itself being CEA's own rounded percentage rather than an exact figure. Both routes agree to within rounding; this lesson uses the subtraction route specifically because it stays internally consistent with the directly-published large-hydro/nuclear figures below, where the ≈60,845 MW gap is independently corroborated by two figures CEA publishes directly, not as a back-calculated residual.

1C — The Core Trap: Installed Capacity ≠ Demand Met ≠ Peak Demand ≠ Generation
This is the definitional distinction the entire lesson is built around, and it is worth stating as cleanly as possible before any numbers appear. Four different quantities describe four different things about the same power system, and no two of them are interchangeable.
Installed capacity (MW) — the maximum the system could produce if every plant ran at full rated output simultaneously; a stock/ceiling, not a rate that is ever actually sustained. Generation (MU — million units; 1 MU = 1 GWh = 10 lakh kWh) — the electricity actually produced over a period (a day, a month, a year); a flow, measured over time, not an instant. Energy requirement / demand (MU, over a period) — how much electricity the system's consumers wanted over that period; energy supplied / demand met (MU) is how much of that was actually delivered, and the gap between the two is the energy shortage, usually expressed as a percentage. Peak demand (MW) — the single highest instantaneous demand recorded at any moment within a period (typically the evening peak on the hottest day of the year); peak demand met (MW) is how much of that instantaneous peak was actually supplied, and the gap is the peaking shortage. Peak demand and peak demand met are measured in MW (an instant), never confused with the MU figures above (a cumulative total) — this unit difference is itself a quick way to catch a conflation in an exam option.
A water-supply analogy for all four terms at once
Picture a city's water system. Installed capacity is the maximum flow rate every pump and pipe in the network could physically deliver at once if fully opened — a fixed engineering ceiling. Generation is the actual total volume of water that flowed through the network over the whole day. Energy requirement/demand is how much water residents wanted that day; demand met is how much they actually got, and any gap is the day's water shortage. Peak demand is the single busiest minute of the day — say, 7 a.m. when everyone showers at once — and peak demand met is whether the system kept up at that exact minute. A system can meet 100% of the day's total water demand while still running dry for five minutes at 7 a.m. — which is exactly why "demand met" and "peak demand met" must be tracked as two separate numbers, not one.

Re-pull a fresher month's Executive Summary before the exam if a more recent one better illustrates the point.
If February 2026 shows a near-zero shortage, why does installed capacity (551,994.76 MW) look so much larger than peak demand met (244,206 MW) the same year?
Because installed capacity is a ceiling built to cover the worst-case coincidence of demand across the entire year (a summer evening peak, not a mild February evening) plus a reserve margin for maintenance outages, transmission losses, and the fact that solar and wind capacity does not generate at its full nameplate rating most of the time (a low average capacity-utilisation factor relative to thermal or nuclear plants of the same nameplate MW). A system is not "wasting" capacity by having installed MW far above a given month's peak demand met — it is exactly this gap that lets the same system also meet a much higher demand on the hottest day of the year, discussed next in §1D. The precise mechanics of why renewable nameplate capacity needs to be so much larger than the peak MW it displaces is developed fully in Lesson 5's installed-capacity-vs-generation-share trap.
TRAP — a national zero-shortage figure does not mean zero shortage everywhere, every hour
February 2026's near-zero all-India energy and peaking shortage is an aggregate, national-level figure. It does not, by itself, establish that every state, every distribution utility, or every hour of that month was shortage-free — local transmission-and-distribution (T&D) bottlenecks, feeder-level constraints, or a single state's temporary deficit can sit underneath a healthy national aggregate without appearing in it. Treat "0% all-India shortage" as a statement about the national balance sheet, not a guarantee about every corner of the grid.
1D — Peak Demand: Trend and the Latest Confirmed Record
Peak demand — the single highest instantaneous MW figure recorded across a year — climbs steadily as summer cooling load, agricultural pumping, industrial growth, and rising per-capita consumption (§1F) all stack together, typically peaking on the hottest pre-monsoon evenings of April–June. Each year's new maximum is widely reported, but this is exactly the kind of figure that can move across sources within weeks of each other — so the sourcing discipline here matters more than the number itself.

TRAP — an unconfirmed higher figure (270.8 GW) must not be used as the headline fact
A figure of 270.8 GW, dated 21 May 2026, has been reported via multiple news outlets attributing it to the Ministry of Power — this is government-sourced via wire/press reporting during that month's heatwave, not a trade-press aggregator's own invention — but no standalone PIB or CEA press release (PRID) for this specific number could be located to independently confirm it. Per this book's sourcing discipline, only the PIB-confirmed 256.1 GW (25.04.2026) figure should be used as the exam-ready headline record. The 270.8 GW figure may be mentioned as "a higher figure, attributed to the Ministry of Power, has been widely reported in the press but has no standalone PIB/CEA release confirming it," but must never be presented as the confirmed record unless a genuine CEA/PIB primary source for it is independently located and dated.
Set against §1C's February 2026 peak-demand-met figure of 244,206 MW (≈244.2 GW — a non-summer, "shoulder season" month), the April 2026 record of 256.1 GW illustrates the strong seasonality built into India's peak-demand curve: the same grid's peak load moves by roughly 12 GW between an ordinary February evening and the hottest evening of the pre-monsoon summer, and the gap widens further in a genuinely severe heatwave year. This seasonality, not a steady month-on-month climb, is the shape to remember.

Why does installed capacity more than double while peak demand grows by a smaller multiple?
Because a growing share of the added capacity is renewable (solar/wind), and renewable nameplate capacity generates, on average, only a fraction of its rated MW across the year (its output depends on sunlight/wind availability) — so far more renewable nameplate GW must be built than the peak-demand GW it is meant to help cover, with battery storage and pumped-hydro capacity added on top purely to shift that output to the hours when it is actually needed. Capacity growing faster than demand is therefore not inefficiency; it is the physical cost of shifting the generation mix toward variable renewable sources while still keeping the lights on at the peak hour — the same installed-capacity-vs-generation-share idea flagged in §1C, built out fully with full source-wise renewable figures in Lesson 5.

TRAP — three different documents, all liable to be called "NEP"
This syllabus clause's reading list collides three separate documents onto the same three-letter abbreviation: the National Energy Policy, 2017 (NITI Aayog, an unfinalised whole-of-energy "umbrella" draft — see Lesson 3), the National Electricity Plan, 2022-32 (CEA, a statutory generation-planning document notified under the Electricity Act, 2003 — this box), and the Draft National Electricity Policy, 2026 (Ministry of Power, released for consultation 21.01.2026 — also Lesson 3). This book deliberately avoids the bare abbreviation "NEP" wherever ambiguity could arise, and an examiner's question stem that uses "NEP" alone without naming the issuing body should be read as a trap, not a shortcut — always match it to author, legal status, and year before answering.

TRAP — per-capita electricity consumption is NOT the same metric as per-capita energy consumption
"Per-capita electricity consumption" (measured in kWh/person/year, as above) covers only grid electricity. "Per-capita energy consumption" (a broader metric, typically expressed in MJ, kgoe, or similar all-energy units) additionally includes non-electric energy use — transport fuel, cooking fuel (LPG/biomass), and direct industrial/thermal fuel use that never passes through the electricity grid at all. Because electricity is only one slice of a country's total energy use, per-capita energy consumption figures are structurally larger and answer a different question than per-capita electricity figures — the two must never be quoted as if interchangeable, and a specific per-capita-energy figure is deliberately not frozen into this lesson, since no single reliable same-dated figure for it could be located; treat the distinction itself, not a number, as the examinable point here.

Recognition-level footnote — sector-wise ownership. Installed capacity in India is owned across three broad sectors — Central (central public-sector generating companies), State (state generating utilities), and Private (private power producers) — with the private sector's share having grown to be a very large part of the mix in recent years. Exact current percentages are not frozen into this lesson and must be re-verified from a current CEA source before being cited as fact.
Recognition-level footnote — India's global rank claims. India is commonly described in commentary as being among the world's largest producers and consumers of electricity by absolute volume (its large population means its per-capita figures in §1F remain well below many developed economies even where its absolute rank is high). Any specific ordinal rank ("3rd largest," etc.) must be re-verified against a current, dated primary source immediately before the exam — global-rank claims move as other countries' figures update too, and no single rank claim is frozen into this lesson as a stated fact.

★ Quick Facts
  1. Two classification axes, not one: conventional/non-conventional (how long a source has been in large-scale use) is independent of renewable/non-renewable (whether nature replenishes it). Large hydro = conventional + renewable; nuclear = conventional + non-renewable.
  2. MNRE traces through three stages: Department of Non-Conventional Energy Sources (created 1982) → upgraded to full Ministry status as the Ministry of Non-Conventional Energy Sources (1992) → renamed Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (October 2006) — a reminder that "non-conventional" and "renewable" have long been used loosely as synonyms in Indian official usage.
  3. Total installed capacity (all-India): 551,994.76 MW as of 31.07.2026 (CEA Monthly Installed Capacity Report) — a dynamic, monthly-moving figure.
  4. CEA's "RES" (43.42%, 31.07.2026) EXCLUDES large hydro and nuclear; "non-fossil" (54.44%, same date) = RES + large hydro + nuclear. The ≈11-point gap ≈ 60.8 GW is made up of large hydro (52,064.66 MW) and nuclear (8,780.00 MW), both directly CEA-published at the same 31.07.2026 date.
  5. Coal-based thermal capacity: 224,157.50 MW (≈40.6% of total), as of 31.07.2026 — the same CEA snapshot as the headline total-capacity figure above (this date now also matches Lesson 2's coal figure, reconciling the earlier cross-lesson date mismatch).
  6. Installed capacity (MW, a ceiling) ≠ generation (MU, actual output) ≠ demand/energy requirement (MU wanted) ≠ demand met/energy supplied (MU delivered) ≠ peak demand (MW, one instant) ≠ peak demand met (MW delivered at that instant).
  7. February 2026 worked example (CEA Executive Summary): energy requirement 132,501 MU vs supplied 132,474 MU (shortage 27 MU, ≈0.02%); peak demand 244,206 MW vs peak met 244,206 MW (peaking shortage 0 MW, 0%).
  8. A national zero-shortage figure is an aggregate — it does not guarantee zero shortage in every state, feeder, or hour.
  9. Latest PIB-confirmed all-time peak demand: 256.1 GW, recorded 25 April 2026 (PIB PRID 2256313, released 28.04.2026) — the only headline figure to cite.
  10. A higher figure of 270.8 GW (21.05.2026) is reported via multiple news outlets attributing it to the Ministry of Power, but with no standalone PIB/CEA press release located to independently confirm it — do not cite it as the confirmed record.
  11. CEA Long-Term National Resource Adequacy Plan (2026-27 to 2035-36, March 2026): capacity rises from 520.5 GW (31.01.2026) to ≈1,121 GW (2035-36) — more than doubling; non-fossil share rises from ≈52% to ≈70%; peak demand rises from ≈289 GW (2026-27 estimate) to ≈459 GW (2035-36).
  12. Capacity grows faster than demand in these projections because variable renewable (solar/wind) nameplate MW must be over-built relative to the peak MW it displaces, given its lower average output relative to rated capacity.
  13. National Electricity Plan (NEP), Volume I: Generation, 2022-32 — CEA's statutory plan under Section 3(4) of the Electricity Act, 2003 (notified 31.05.2023, PIB PRID 1928750): projects 2031-32 peak demand of 366.4 GW, installed capacity ≈900,422 MW, non-fossil share ≈68.4% (2026-27 milestone: ≈277.2 GW peak demand, ≈609,591 MW capacity, ≈57.4% non-fossil) — a different, separately-dated CEA document from both the Long-Term Resource Adequacy Plan above and Lesson 3's National Energy Policy, 2017, despite the shared "NEP" abbreviation.
  14. Per-capita electricity consumption: 957 kWh (2013-14) → 1,460 kWh (2024-25), per PIB Year End Review 2025 (PRID 2215187, posted 16.01.2026) — a 52.6% rise in a decade.
  15. Per-capita electricity ≠ per-capita energy — the latter is a broader, all-energy metric (including transport and cooking fuel) that is structurally larger and answers a different question; no specific figure is frozen here.
  16. Capacity roughly doubled, 2014 (249 GW) → 2025 (over 510 GW) — Union Power Minister's statement, 16.01.2026, Bharat Electricity Summit curtain-raiser.
  17. Sector-wise ownership (Central/State/Private) and India's specific global rank claims are recognition-level footnotes only — re-verify exact current figures before the exam.
  18. Never assemble a capacity/demand breakdown by mixing two CEA/MNRE reports with different as-of dates — always use one same-dated snapshot per breakdown.

"270.8 GW is India's confirmed all-time peak demand record"Only 256.1 GW (25.04.2026, PIB PRID 2256313) is primarily confirmed; 270.8 GW (21.05.2026) is reported via multiple news outlets attributing it to the Ministry of Power, but with no standalone PIB/CEA press release located to independently confirm it — must not be cited as the headline record
"Energy shortage % and peaking shortage % always move together"They are separate measures (cumulative MU vs instantaneous MW) that can diverge — a system can be fine on average across a month while still straining at the single peak hour, or vice versa
"A 0% national shortage means there was no shortage anywhere that month"The national aggregate can mask local state, feeder, or hourly shortfalls sitting underneath a healthy all-India number
"Per-capita electricity consumption and per-capita energy consumption are the same figure"Per-capita electricity (kWh) covers grid electricity only; per-capita energy (MJ/kgoe-type units) is broader, adding transport and cooking-fuel use — structurally larger, a different question
"Large hydro power counts as one of CEA's renewable energy sources (RES)"Large hydro is conventional AND renewable in the strict physical sense, but CEA's own "RES" bookkeeping category excludes it — it sits inside "non-fossil" instead, alongside nuclear
"Nuclear power is a renewable, non-conventional source since it doesn't burn fossil fuel"Nuclear is conventional (decades-established) and non-renewable (finite mined uranium/thorium fuel) — "does not burn fossil fuel" is not the same test as "renewable"
