6A — Household and Agricultural Solar: PM Surya Ghar and PM-KUSUM
India's renewable-energy scheme landscape splits cleanly along who the electricity is for. Two flagship schemes anchor the two ends of that split: one puts solar panels on the roofs of ordinary households, the other puts solar power (and solar-powered pumps) into the hands of farmers. Both are Central Sector Schemes administered principally through the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE), and both are exactly the kind of scheme-name-plus-parameter combination APPSC's objective format tests directly.
PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana ("Surya Ghar" — literally "Sun (in the) Home"; "Muft Bijli" — "free electricity") is the Union Government's flagship residential rooftop solar scheme, aimed at making grid-connected rooftop solar affordable enough for ordinary households to adopt at scale, with net-metering allowing surplus power generated during the day to be exported back to the DISCOM and credited against the household's bill.
CURRENT — PM Surya Ghar: approval, outlay and target
FACT: PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana's Cabinet approval, total outlay, and rollout target. VALUE: The scheme received Cabinet approval on 29 February 2024, with a total outlay of ₹75,021 crore — one of the largest single outlays of any scheme in this book. Its stated target is twofold and the two halves are two complementary, related target metrics for the same underlying rollout (not two separate or rival figures) — though converting cleanly between them assumes a given average system size per household, so they are not strictly interchangeable by unit conversion alone: 1 crore (10 million) households are to be covered with rooftop solar, aggregating to 30 GW of rooftop solar capacity by the scheme's own design horizon. Think of net metering as a two-way ledger, not literally a meter spinning backward
A conventional meter only counts electricity flowing one way — into the house. Modern net-metering installations instead use a bidirectional meter that separately records the electricity drawn from the grid and the electricity exported to it: whenever the rooftop panels generate more than the household is using at that moment, the surplus flows out to the grid and is credited; at night, when the panels produce nothing, the household draws from the grid as before. The bill then nets the two recorded totals out under the applicable state regulations — which in some states means pure net metering, and in others a net-billing or gross-metering variant with different settlement rules — which is how some beneficiaries end up with a "zero" bill, not because they use no grid electricity at all, but because their exports offset their imports over the billing cycle.
TRAP — "1 crore households" and "30 GW" are two different units of the same target, not two competing figures
A careless reading can make it look as if the scheme has "two targets" that might conflict. They do not conflict — they are two complementary, related metrics for the same rollout (not strictly interchangeable by unit conversion, since that would assume a fixed average system size per household): 1 crore counts beneficiary households; 30 GW counts the aggregate installed capacity those households' rooftops are expected to add up to. Neither figure is the scheme's outlay (₹75,021 crore, a rupee figure) and neither is its achievement so far (a separate, dated, moving figure — see below). A single MCQ can test all three as separate blanks in one statement.

Why does APPSC care about a household electricity scheme at all?
Because it sits exactly at the intersection the syllabus clause names: it is simultaneously a renewable-capacity-addition instrument (contributing rooftop GW toward the 500 GW non-fossil target taught in Lesson 5), a household-welfare instrument (bill relief), and an industrial-policy instrument — the Economic Survey 2023-24 (tabled in Parliament by the Finance Minister on 22 July 2024) itself states the scheme is expected to be "creating around 17 lakh direct jobs across the solar value chain," a Tier-1 government-document citation rather than merely the scheme's own promotional materials. A single scheme touching energy, welfare, and industry policy at once is precisely the sort of "new initiative" the clause is asking students to be able to describe.
The agricultural counterpart to PM Surya Ghar is PM-KUSUM — the Pradhan Mantri Kisan Urja Suraksha evam Utthaan Mahabhiyan ("Prime Minister's Farmer Energy Security and Upliftment Campaign"). Where PM Surya Ghar's beneficiary is a household electricity consumer, PM-KUSUM's beneficiary is a farmer, and its unit of work is as much the irrigation pump as the rooftop panel. MNRE's own official account dates the scheme's launch to 8 March 2019 — the source for the "2019 launch" year that recurs in the scheme-matrix table later in this lesson — under an original Cabinet-approved outlay of ₹34,035 crore, since revised to the ₹34,422 crore CFA figure given in the CURRENT box below. T1 — MNRE (@mnreindia), official scheme-anniversary statement, 08.03.2025
PM-KUSUM is built from three components, and knowing which component does what is the most commonly tested part of the scheme. Component A — setting up small (0.5–2 MW), decentralised, ground-mounted solar/renewable power plants on barren or agricultural land, with the power sold to the local DISCOM, giving landowners (including farmers) a new income stream from otherwise unused or marginal land. Component B — installation of new standalone solar-powered agricultural pumps, replacing diesel pumps in areas without reliable grid access. Component C — solarisation of existing grid-connected agricultural pumps, under two distinct sub-models. Under individual-pump solarisation, a farmer's own grid-connected pump gets dedicated solar capacity, and that farmer may individually export/sell surplus power under the applicable arrangement. Under feeder-level solarisation, a dedicated agriculture feeder is instead supplied by solar generation built at a utility/project level — the surplus-sale relationship there runs between the solar generator and the DISCOM at feeder scale, not simply each individual farmer selling personal surplus.

TRAP — Component A is not "solarising a farmer's pump"
Component A is a small power-generation project sold wholesale to the DISCOM — its beneficiary relationship to the land owner is closer to a lease/power-purchase arrangement than to irrigation. Component C, by contrast, is specifically about pumps a farmer already uses for irrigation, now solarised. An MCQ describing "a farmer earning income by leasing barren land for a small solar plant" is testing Component A; one describing "a farmer's existing electric tubewell converted to run on solar power" is testing Component C — do not swap the two.

Why is PM-KUSUM's timing (daytime solar, daytime irrigation) not a coincidence?
Because it solves two problems with one design choice. Grid-connected irrigation pumps are traditionally a major source of subsidised, often night-shifted or erratic-hours DISCOM electricity supply to agriculture — a long-standing fiscal and grid-management headache for state utilities. Solar generation, meanwhile, peaks exactly during daylight hours, which is also when most irrigation happens. Solarising a pump therefore aligns the electricity source with the actual demand pattern, cutting both diesel dependence and the DISCOM subsidy burden on agricultural feeders at the same time — the same "installed capacity is not useful unless it is usable when needed" theme that Lesson 2 raised for gas-based stranded capacity reappears here in a renewable, farm-level form.
6B — Green Hydrogen and Legacy Solar Missions
This section covers four distinct threads: the newest major renewable initiative in the book (green hydrogen), the oldest named solar scheme still worth knowing (JNNSM, taught here purely as dated history), the scheme responsible for most of India's utility-scale solar land (the Solar Park Scheme), and the policy pair behind how much of that solar hardware is actually made in India — the PLI scheme for solar manufacturing and the ALMM compulsory-registration list.
Green hydrogen is hydrogen gas produced by splitting water via electrolysis, using electricity drawn from renewable sources — as distinct from "grey" hydrogen (produced from natural gas via steam-methane reforming, the dominant global method today) and "blue" hydrogen (grey hydrogen's process, but paired with carbon capture and storage to reduce its net emissions). Because electrolysis is powered by clean electricity rather than fossil fuel, green hydrogen carries a far smaller carbon footprint across its production life-cycle — the entire rationale for building a dedicated national mission around it.
CURRENT — three different things share the name "Green Hydrogen": Mission ≠ Standard ≠ Certification Scheme
FACT: India runs three separate, differently-purposed instruments under the "green hydrogen" umbrella, and APPSC-style questions frequently test whether a candidate can tell them apart. VALUE: (1) the National Green Hydrogen Mission is the funding-and-target policy vehicle — Cabinet-approved, ₹19,744 crore outlay, 5 MMT/year-by-2030 target (detailed in the box immediately below); (2) the Green Hydrogen Standard for India is a separate technical definition notified by MNRE — it sets the emissions ceiling a kilogram of hydrogen must meet to actually be called "green": a well-to-gate emission limit of ≤2 kg of CO₂-equivalent per kilogram of hydrogen produced, averaged over a 12-month period, covering hydrogen made via renewable-electricity electrolysis or via biomass-based routes using renewable energy; (3) the Green Hydrogen Certification Scheme is the verification mechanism that actually checks a given producer's hydrogen against that Standard and issues (or refuses) a "green hydrogen" certificate, with the Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE) designated as the nodal accreditation/certification authority and empanelled agencies conducting the underlying audits. MEASURE/DEFINITION: Mission = outlay/target policy; Standard = an emissions-threshold definition (≤2 kg CO₂e/kg H₂, 12-month average); Certification Scheme = the BEE-administered audit-and-certificate mechanism that applies the Standard to individual producers. None of the three is a substitute term for either of the other two. JURISDICTION SCOPE: All-India; Standard and Certification Scheme both notified/administered by MNRE, with BEE as certifying authority. STATUS DATE: Green Hydrogen Standard notified by MNRE, August 2023. UNDERLYING DOCUMENT NOT DIRECTLY ACCESSED: the ≤2 kg CO₂e/kg H₂ threshold, the 12-month averaging window, and BEE's certifying-authority role are drawn from secondary summary reporting of MNRE's notification (not a direct MNRE/PIB PDF fetch in this session) — primary-lock the exact threshold figure, the averaging methodology, and the notification date against the MNRE Gazette notification itself before treating "2 kg CO₂e/kg" as a verbatim-quotable figure. TRAP — SIGHT is the largest slice of the Mission, not the whole Mission
₹17,490 crore (SIGHT) is easy to mistake for the Mission's total outlay because it is by far the largest named component — but it is roughly 89% of the total, not 100%. The full Mission figure is ₹19,744 crore; SIGHT, Pilot Projects, R&D and other components together make up that total.
SIGHT itself is not one undifferentiated incentive — it splits into two components governing two different points in the green-hydrogen value chain, and telling them apart is the same kind of component-identification test this lesson already built for PM-KUSUM's A/B/C split.

Long before PM Surya Ghar or the Green Hydrogen Mission existed, India's solar push began under a different name entirely — one that is now purely historical vocabulary, but still a favourite chronology item.
The Jawaharlal Nehru National Solar Mission (JNNSM) was launched in January 2010 as one of the eight national missions under the National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC). Its original target, structured across three implementation phases, was 20,000 MW of grid-connected solar power by 2021-22.

Turning ambitious megawatt targets into actual generation required somewhere to physically put thousands of megawatts of panels — which is precisely the gap the Solar Park Scheme was designed to close.
The Solar Park Scheme (formally, the "Scheme for Development of Solar Parks and Ultra Mega Solar Power Projects"), approved by the Cabinet in December 2014, funds the creation of large, government-facilitated solar parks — designated zones with shared land, roads, water, and evacuation (transmission) infrastructure already arranged — so that individual solar developers can set up generation capacity inside the park without separately assembling land and grid connectivity themselves. The scheme originally targeted 20,000 MW of solar capacity through at least 25 such parks (Cabinet approval, December 2014); this was enhanced to a target of 40,000 MW on 21 March 2017. MNRE's own scheme page does not restate an exact revised park count against the 40,000 MW target, so treat "≥25 parks" as tied to the original 20,000 MW target only, not to the current 40,000 MW figure — each park is typically sized at 500 MW or larger.

The Approved List of Models and Manufacturers (ALMM) is MNRE's compulsory-registration regime for solar equipment: a government-maintained list of specific solar-module and solar-cell models and manufacturers, only whose products may be used in specified categories of solar projects — channelling demand created by government money and regulation toward equipment on the government's approved eligibility list of specific models and manufacturers. That is a separate concept from actually requiring the equipment be manufactured in India — ALMM is an eligibility-list regime, not a made-in-India verification regime (see the PLI/ALMM/DCR comparison box below).

★ Must Compare — PLI vs ALMM vs DCR: three different instruments, often confused
InstrumentWhat it actually isQuestion it answers
PLI — Production Linked Incentive (for solar manufacturing)A financial incentive: pays manufacturers a per-watt subsidy for scaling up domestic solar cell/module manufacturing capacity, tied to actual manufacture and sale"Is the government incentivising domestic manufacturing capacity?"
ALMM — Approved List of Models and ManufacturersAn eligibility list: specifies which particular solar-module (List-I) and, from 2026, solar-cell (List-II) models and manufacturers are approved for use in specified categories of projects (government/govt.-scheme/open-access/net-metering/govt.-offtake)"Is this particular model/manufacturer on the approved eligibility list?"
DCR — Domestic Content RequirementA domestic-content condition imposed under specified government schemes, tenders, or programmes (not a blanket market-wide rule): requires that the equipment actually be manufactured (not merely assembled or branded) in India — verification mechanisms vary by programme; NISE-portal certification is used in some implementation contexts, but should not be assumed to be the single universal verification route across every DCR-linked scheme"Was this equipment actually made in India?"
All three push toward the same broad domestic-manufacturing goal, but they are legally and operationally distinct instruments: PLI rewards building manufacturing capacity, ALMM gates which specific models/manufacturers may be used in specified projects, and DCR requires actual Indian manufacture of the equipment itself. An ALMM-listed product is not automatically DCR-compliant — a module can be assembled and branded by an ALMM-listed Indian manufacturer, and still fail DCR, if components further back in its supply chain (such as its cells) were imported rather than domestically made.

6C — Regulatory Mechanisms and Enabling Infrastructure
Not every "new initiative" in this clause is a subsidy scheme with an outlay figure. Two of the most examinable items here are not schemes at all in that sense — one is a regulatory obligation placed on power distribution companies, the other is transmission infrastructure built to carry renewable power to where it is needed. Both matter precisely because capacity and targets, covered in Lesson 5 and above, are useless unless the power can actually be bought, sold, and delivered.
A Renewable Purchase Obligation (RPO) is a statutory obligation, rooted in the Electricity Act, 2003 and given year-wise targets through Ministry of Power and State Electricity Regulatory Commission (SERC) regulations, requiring obligated entities — principally DISCOMs, but also open-access consumers and captive power plant users — to source a minimum percentage of the total consumption of electricity in their area of supply/operation (per Section 86(1)(e) of the Electricity Act, 2003's own statutory language) from renewable energy sources; this is commonly treated as equivalent to electricity procured/purchased for an obligated entity such as a DISCOM, since a DISCOM's own "consumption" in this sense is the electricity it distributes/sells. It is a demand-side lever: rather than the government directly building renewable capacity, RPO compels the entities that buy and distribute power to keep pulling renewable electricity into the mix.
How RPO compliance actually works
An obligated entity can meet its RPO either by directly procuring renewable power (via long-term power-purchase agreements with renewable generators) or, if it cannot procure enough directly, by purchasing Renewable Energy Certificates (RECs) — tradable instruments, bought and sold on power exchanges, that represent renewable generation which happened elsewhere in the grid. This lets an entity in a renewable-poor region meet its obligation through certificate purchase — paying for renewable generation that genuinely occurred at a generator in a renewable-rich region — rather than by directly consuming renewable electricity itself; the generation behind the certificate is real, only the direct physical link between generator and obligated entity is absent.

Why pair a Renewable Purchase Obligation with an Energy Storage Obligation at all?
Because solar and wind are variable — they generate on nature's schedule, not the grid's demand schedule. As the renewable share of the obligation climbs, an obligation on purchase volume alone does not guarantee the power is available exactly when the grid needs it. Bundling in a storage obligation (batteries, pumped hydro, or other storage that an obligated entity must also contract for) is the regulatory tool meant to convert "renewable energy purchased somewhere, sometime" into "renewable-backed energy available when needed" — conceptually the same demand-and-timing problem PM-KUSUM's daytime-pump design solves at the farm level, now solved at the grid level instead.
Even a perfectly sited, perfectly obligated renewable power plant is worthless if there is no wire strong enough to carry its output to where the demand is — which is exactly the gap the Green Energy Corridor exists to close.
The Green Energy Corridor (GEC) is a dedicated transmission-infrastructure programme built specifically to evacuate and integrate renewable power from resource-rich states into the wider grid, reducing curtailment (renewable generation that has to be wasted because the grid cannot carry it away) and easing the grid-balancing task created by variable generation.

Why does "transmission" deserve its own named scheme, rather than being assumed as automatic?
Because generation capacity and evacuation capacity are built by different actors on different timelines, and a mismatch between the two is exactly the "installed vs utilised capacity" trap Lesson 2 taught for gas-based stranded capacity — reappearing here in renewable form. A solar park can be fully commissioned and still generate well below its potential if the surrounding grid cannot carry its output away at the moment the sun is at its peak. The Green Energy Corridor is the government's explicit acknowledgement that hitting a renewable capacity target is necessary but not sufficient — the power has to actually reach consumers.
6D — India's Global Renewable Energy Leadership
The clause's final word — "Achievements" — is not only about domestic megawatts. India's single strongest renewable-energy diplomacy credential is an international body it co-founded, headquartered on Indian soil: the International Solar Alliance.
The International Solar Alliance (ISA) was jointly launched by India and France on 30 November 2015, at Paris, on the sidelines of the COP21 UN climate summit that went on to produce the Paris Agreement. Its founding purpose is to mobilise finance, technology, and capacity for large-scale solar deployment, originally among "sunshine countries" located wholly or partly between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn.
Launch date vs treaty-in-force date — a real and tested distinction
Being "launched" in 2015 is not the same as becoming a formal international treaty body. ISA's Framework Agreement was opened for signature after the 2015 launch and entered into force on 6 December 2017, once the required minimum number of countries had ratified it — the moment ISA became a fully-fledged, treaty-based intergovernmental organisation. ISA is frequently described as the first treaty-based international/intergovernmental organisation headquartered in India. Its headquarters sits at Gurugram, Haryana, on the campus of the National Institute of Solar Energy (NISE).
TRAP — "founded in 2015" and "entered into force in 2017" are both correct, for different questions
A statement-based question may test "ISA was established in 2015" (true — that is its launch date at COP21) alongside "ISA became a legally binding treaty body in 2015" (false — that happened only once the Framework Agreement entered into force on 6 December 2017). Both dates are real and both are tested; the discriminator is whether the question is asking about the political launch or the legal entry-into-force.

A grid that follows the sun around the planet
Picture the Earth as a single circuit: at any moment, the sun is directly over some part of it and has already set over another. OSOWOG's underlying idea, already introduced above, is that connecting solar-rich regions across time zones lets surplus daytime power in one zone cover night-time demand in another. What makes this genuinely harder than PM Surya Ghar's household net metering — the comparison this lesson draws elsewhere — is scale and sovereignty: a rooftop and a DISCOM sit inside one legal and technical system with one grid code, while OSOWOG would have to synchronise voltage, frequency and settlement rules across dozens of national grids and national regulators that answer to no single authority. The household analogy explains the energy-flow logic; the genuinely new problem OSOWOG adds is coordinating that flow across borders nobody controls jointly — closer to negotiating a multilateral treaty than to reading a two-way electricity meter.
6E — Energy Storage and Emerging Obligations
Everything from 6A to 6D assumed that renewable electricity, once generated, is immediately usable. It is not — solar and wind generate on nature's schedule, not the grid's, and 6C's Renewable Purchase Obligation discussion already flagged that a newer trajectory bundles in an Energy Storage Obligation (ESO) whose exact figures were not yet confirmed. This section closes that gap: it covers the two storage technologies now treated as named policy categories in their own right — Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) and Pumped Storage Hydro (PSH) — a concrete, Andhra-Pradesh-sited example that combines solar and wind generation with PSH-based storage at gigawatt scale (not a BESS example — Pinnapuram's storage component is pumped-storage hydro, not batteries), the now-corroborated RPO/ESO trajectory figures, a three-way disambiguation of RPO, ESO and a third obligation (RCO) the book has not yet named, and a short footnote on the critical minerals this entire storage-and-EV build-out depends on.
Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) are large, grid-connected battery installations (typically lithium-ion) that charge when electricity is abundant or cheap — commonly from midday solar surplus — and discharge when demand or prices peak, most often in the evening after solar output falls. Because they respond within seconds, BESS is the fastest-acting of India's named storage categories, and is now supported through a dedicated Central Sector scheme rather than being left to the market alone.

Pumped Storage Hydro (PSH) works like a rechargeable water battery: during hours of surplus generation (typically midday solar, or off-peak demand), electricity is used to pump water uphill into an upper reservoir; when demand peaks — typically evening, once solar output has fallen — that stored water is released back downhill through turbines to generate electricity on demand. Because a fully built PSP can absorb surplus renewable generation and discharge it again within minutes, it is treated as a named storage-policy category in its own right, parallel to (and often paired with) BESS, rather than being folded into ordinary hydropower statistics.

Why three separate obligations, and how do they actually relate to one another?
Because each closes a different gap the other two leave open. RPO alone can be satisfied by buying a certificate (REC) for renewable power that genuinely was generated somewhere else on the grid, without the obligated entity itself directly consuming any renewable electricity — a real generation event, just not one the obligated entity physically drew on. RCO is, first and foremost, a separate statutory obligation in its own right (Energy Conservation Act, 2001, s.14(x), Gazette Notification 20.10.2023) requiring designated consumers to directly consume a minimum non-fossil share — and, as a consequence of that direct-consumption design, it also happens to close a gap RPO leaves open, by counting only energy the designated consumer itself actually consumes, including on-site renewable generation, rather than certificates traded at arm's length. Neither RPO nor RCO says anything about when that renewable energy is available — which is what ESO adds, by requiring a slice of procurement to specifically come through storage, time-shifting some of the renewable electricity procured under RPO/RCO to when the grid actually needs it. A caveat on RCO's mechanics specifically: exactly which entities are obligated, precisely what counts as "consumption," how captive/open-access generation is treated, and whether any certificate-based compliance route exists for RCO are this book's interpretation of the RCO framework's likely operation, not a verified reading of the 20.10.2023 notification's own text — treat this as a provisional interpretation pending direct primary-document verification, not as settled law.

A short critical-minerals footnote to this storage-and-obligations story: much of today's EV and grid-storage deployment depends on lithium-ion batteries, creating strong demand for lithium across chemistries; some major chemistries (such as NMC — nickel-manganese-cobalt) additionally depend on nickel and cobalt, while others (such as LFP — lithium iron phosphate — now widely used) do not use cobalt or nickel at all. Permanent-magnet wind-turbine technologies can separately create demand for rare-earth elements. The clean-energy transition therefore creates new critical-mineral supply-chain vulnerabilities rather than mechanically "replacing crude-oil dependence with lithium-and-cobalt dependence" — the exact mineral exposure depends on which technologies and chemistries are actually deployed, and India's domestic position varies sharply by mineral — lithium and cobalt dependence on imports is especially acute, while the picture for individual rare-earth minerals differs mineral by mineral, so scaling up storage and e-mobility is, in effect, trading one import dependency (crude oil, covered in Lesson 2) for a new, chemistry-dependent one in critical minerals. One important overseas-acquisition vehicle for addressing this is KABIL — Khanij Bidesh India Limited — a joint-venture company incorporated on 8 August 2019 by three government enterprises (National Aluminium Company Ltd., Hindustan Copper Ltd., and Mineral Exploration and Consultancy Ltd.) under the Ministry of Mines, mandated to identify, explore, acquire and develop critical mineral assets overseas — lithium and cobalt foremost.
CURRENT — KABIL's overseas work and the National Critical Mineral Mission (NCMM)
FACT: KABIL's concrete overseas work-in-progress, and the larger NCMM architecture it now sits inside. VALUE: KABIL's most concrete work-in-progress is in Argentina, where it has been pursuing exploration and acquisition of brine lithium blocks in Catamarca province, alongside exploratory interest in Australia and Chile. KABIL's mandate has not disappeared, but it now sits inside a considerably larger architecture: the Union Cabinet approved the National Critical Mineral Mission (NCMM) on 29 January 2025, with a total outlay of ₹34,300 crore over seven years, administered by the Ministry of Mines and spanning the entire critical-mineral value chain — domestic and offshore exploration, mining, beneficiation and processing, recycling and recovery from end-of-life products and tailings, and strategic stockpiling — rather than only the overseas-acquisition role KABIL specialises in. MEASURE/DEFINITION: KABIL = one overseas-acquisition-focused joint-venture company (est. 2019); NCMM = a seven-year, ₹34,300-crore national mission (approved 2025) spanning the full critical-mineral value chain, of which KABIL's overseas work is now one operating instrument, not the whole. The ₹34,300 crore outlay itself is not a pure government-expenditure figure: per the Cabinet approval release, it splits into ₹16,300 crore of direct government budgetary expenditure and ₹18,000 crore of expected investment by PSUs and other entities. KABIL is best read today as one operating instrument inside NCMM's broader architecture, not as India's standalone "principal vehicle" for critical-mineral security. Why this belongs in an energy-management syllabus clause rather than a purely mining one: a storage/EV supply chain dependent on imported critical minerals — lithium above all, and, depending on battery chemistry, nickel and cobalt too — sourced from a handful of overseas producers — much of it processed through a China-concentrated refining chain — carries the same kind of strategic vulnerability Lesson 2 already built for crude-oil import dependence. KABIL's overseas mineral acquisitions are, in that sense, an energy-security instrument as much as an industrial-policy one.
