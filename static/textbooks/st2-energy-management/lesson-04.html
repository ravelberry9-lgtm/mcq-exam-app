Lesson 4 — National Policy on Biofuels and Bharat Stage Norms

APPSC Group-II Mains · Paper-II Section-B · Sub-topic 2 — Energy Management · MASTER PLAN ST2, Lesson 4 of 6 · English draft — bilingual (Telugu/Tenglish) pass to follow · current-affairs references, where any appear, must be re-verified before the exam

This lesson sits at the exact spot where "energy policy" stops being about power plants and starts being about the fuel in a vehicle's tank and the exhaust coming out of it. Two named sub-clauses of the syllabus live here — the National Policy on Biofuels and Bharat Stage Norms — and although they look unrelated at first glance (one is about what goes into an engine, the other about what comes out of it), they are two halves of the same transport-energy story: India trying to blend cleaner fuel in at one end while tightening what tailpipes are allowed to emit at the other. Both halves are unusually numbers-heavy and unusually current-affairs-heavy — this is one of the two most dynamic clusters in the entire book, so every dated figure below is boxed and sourced rather than left loose in prose.
4A — Biofuel Generations and the National Policy on Biofuels, 2018
Before any scheme or target can make sense, the vocabulary of biofuel generations has to be fixed first — almost every objective question in this cluster turns on correctly classifying a feedstock into its generation. One important warning before the classification below: the scientific 1G/2G/3G "generation" system and the National Policy on Biofuels' own Basic/Advanced policy categorisation are two separate classification systems that broadly overlap but are not exact synonyms — this lesson presents them side-by-side for convenience, but a fuel's generation number and its Basic/Advanced policy label should be learned and tested as two related, not identical, facts.
Scientific generation classification (based on feedstock): First Generation (1G): bioethanol produced from sugar- and starch-rich food crops — sugarcane juice, sugarcane molasses (B-heavy and C-heavy), and damaged/surplus foodgrains — and biodiesel produced from non-edible oilseeds (jatropha, karanja), used cooking oil (UCO), and animal fat. Generation terminology is most consistently applied to ethanol feedstocks; for biodiesel, classification of non-edible oils, animal fats, and waste/UCO varies somewhat across technical sources — this book follows the common convention of treating all three as 1G/Basic on the strength of their shared mature, already-commercial processing technology (the same basis the Policy classification below uses), not because the scientific literature is fully unanimous on generation-labelling every biodiesel feedstock this way. Second Generation (2G): made from lignocellulosic biomass and agricultural residue that has no food value once the grain or cane is harvested — rice straw, wheat straw, cotton stalk, bagasse, and similar crop leftovers — using more complex enzymatic/biochemical processing than 1G ethanol requires. Third Generation (3G): primarily algae-based fuel routes; terminology for this tier varies across the scientific literature, and some sources extend "3G" to cover other waste-to-fuel routes as well — treat 3G as the least standardised of the three generation labels. Routes such as bio-CNG (compressed biogas), bio-methanol, bio-hydrogen, and MSW/industrial-waste "drop-in" fuels are NOT reliably classified as "3G" in the scientific literature — they are covered instead by the separate policy classification below, and should not be taught as "scientifically 3G" merely because the National Policy groups them under its Advanced Biofuels category.

National Policy on Biofuels, 2018 classification (a separate, policy-specific system — see the box below for the policy's own details): the Policy splits biofuels into two categories of its own: Basic Biofuels, covering 1G bioethanol and 1G biodiesel; and Advanced Biofuels, covering 2G ethanol, drop-in fuels, and other biofuels such as bio-CNG, bio-methanol, and bio-hydrogen — the Policy's own Basic/Advanced split does not use, and should not be force-mapped onto, the 1G/2G/3G generation numbering above.
Think of it as a feedstock ladder
1G bioethanol is made from things you could otherwise eat or use as food — sugarcane juice, molasses, damaged/surplus foodgrains. 1G/"Basic" biodiesel is different: it is made from non-edible oilseeds, used cooking oil, and animal fat, so it isn't itself food — it shares the "Basic" tier with 1G bioethanol because both rely on mature, already-commercial processing technology, not because both compete with food. 2G biofuels are made from what is left over after the food has already been taken — straw, stalks, husks: agricultural waste that would often just be burnt in the field otherwise. Advanced Biofuels (the policy's highest support tier) go a step further still, drawing on waste streams that are not agricultural at all — municipal garbage, industrial effluent, and (for the narrower scientific 3G tier specifically) algae grown on non-arable land. Each rung up the ladder moves further away from mature, food-linked feedstock and closer to waste-based feedstock processed with costlier technology — which is exactly why 2G ethanol and other Policy-designated Advanced Biofuel routes receive stronger financial support than Basic Biofuels, as the policy discussion just below explains.
The National Policy on Biofuels, 2018 was approved by the Union Cabinet on 16 May 2018 (PIB, Cabinet approval release, 16.05.2018), replacing India's earlier, narrower biofuel policies. Its central structural move was to formally split biofuels into the two categories above — Basic Biofuels (1G bioethanol and biodiesel) and Advanced Biofuels (2G ethanol, drop-in fuels, and other Policy-designated advanced routes such as bio-CNG) — and to attach different incentive structures to each category, since 2G/advanced routes rely on capital-intensive, still-maturing enzymatic and thermochemical refining technology that costs considerably more per unit of output than the mature, already-commercial technology 1G routes use — which is why, as the mechanism below shows, the policy channels dedicated financial support (the Viability Gap Funding mechanism) specifically at the costlier Advanced-tier end and not at 1G.

The 2022 Amendment to the National Policy on Biofuels was approved on 18 May 2022 (PIB, 18.05.2022) and made three changes that matter for the exam. First, and most tested, it advanced the E20 target (20% ethanol blending in petrol) from the original 2030 deadline to Ethanol Supply Year (ESY) 2025-26 — a compression of roughly four years, reflecting how much faster blending had actually scaled up than the 2018 policy assumed. Second, it expanded the permitted feedstock basket for ethanol production (bringing in additional sugar- and grain-based feedstocks) and permitted export of biofuels in specific cases, and allowed the use of surplus food grains for ethanol production when the Food Corporation of India's stocks are in surplus. Third, it expanded NBCC's membership to reflect the wider set of ministries and stakeholders the scaled-up programme now touches.
Why was the E20 target moved up by four years instead of being kept at 2030?
Because ethanol procurement and blending had already outrun the 2018 policy's own assumptions well before 2022 — oil marketing companies were sourcing and blending ethanol faster than the original roadmap anticipated, aided by a bigger sugar-industry surplus and the newly expanded feedstock basket. Rather than let the policy lag behind ground reality, the 2022 amendment simply moved the target to match the trajectory already under way — the blending trend in §4B shows exactly how steep that trajectory was.
TRAP — treating every ethanol/biodiesel route as the same "generation"
A very common error is to assume all fuel-ethanol is "1G" or all biodiesel is "advanced." The generation label depends strictly on feedstock, not on the final fuel type: ethanol itself can be 1G (from molasses/grain) or 2G (from crop residue) depending on what it was made from — "ethanol" and "1G" are not synonyms. Likewise, biodiesel from non-edible oilseeds/UCO is classified as a Basic biofuel under the 2018 policy, not an "advanced" one, even though it is not made from a direct food crop — the Basic/Advanced split in Indian policy tracks the 2018 policy's own two-category scheme, not a strict 1G/2G/3G numbering applied uniformly to every fuel. More broadly: the scientific 1G/2G/3G "generation" taxonomy and the National Policy's own Basic/Advanced policy categorisation are two separate classification systems that broadly overlap but are not one-to-one equivalents — treat them as related, not interchangeable. Compressed Bio-Gas (bio-CNG) in particular should not automatically be assumed to be "third generation" just because it is grouped under Advanced biofuels in this lesson's summary table; its own feedstock (agricultural residue, cattle dung, municipal solid waste, sewage-treatment-plant waste) generally places it in the same generation logic as 2G/3G waste-based routes, but the Policy's own Basic/Advanced label is the one actually used in scheme documents, not a formal generation number.

The Ethanol Blended Petrol (EBP) Programme is the delivery mechanism for the blending targets above — oil marketing companies procure ethanol and blend it into petrol sold at the pump, at a percentage that has climbed steeply and is a high-yield, frequently-tested current-affairs figure for this topic.

A number worth double-checking on the day you teach it
Because this figure is updated by PIB itself every few months, treat everything above as a snapshot as of the two July 2026 PIB releases cited — not as a permanently frozen fact. If a newer PIB release exists by the time this lesson is used, that release's figure should replace the one above.
With the headline E20 target reported met, the natural next question — what comes after 20%? — does not yet have a single settled answer, but it is no longer a purely open question either. Per PIB's own July 2026 material, the government is examining a phased move beyond 20%, toward E25, E27, and E30 blends, and has separately begun promoting E85 flex-fuel vehicles (launched around June 2026) as a parallel, higher-blend track for compatible vehicles rather than a change to the universal petrol-pump blend. None of E25/E27/E30 carries a locked target date at this stage — this should be taught as "under examination," not as an adopted policy, and re-verified before the exam for any firmer announcement.
If blending is rising, why does NITI Aayog keep raising mileage and compatibility caveats?
Because higher ethanol content changes a fuel's energy density and combustion behaviour, and older engines not specifically tuned for it lose some fuel efficiency even though the fuel itself blends and burns safely. NITI Aayog's own assessments have flagged a mileage loss of roughly 6-7% in non-optimised four-wheelers and roughly 3-4% in non-optimised two-wheelers running on E20 compared with pure petrol, unless the vehicle's engine and fuel system have been specifically recalibrated for the higher blend (as newer "E20-compatible" and flex-fuel vehicles are). This is why the government's own messaging distinguishes an engine optimised for E20 from an older engine simply tolerating E20 — the achievement figure (20% blended nationally) and the compatibility caveat (mileage loss in older vehicles) are both true at once and are not a contradiction; treat the mileage-loss figures as reported guidance requiring re-verification against a primary NITI Aayog release before being frozen as an exact number.
Alongside ethanol, the policy also carries a parallel biodiesel blending target — and here the story is the opposite of ethanol's success. The National Policy on Biofuels' 2022-amended trajectory envisages 5% biodiesel blending by 2030, but actual uptake has lagged far behind: biodiesel blending achieved was reported at only around 0.5% (IOCL data, FY2023-24).

4C — SATAT, GOBARdhan and PM JI-VAN Yojana
Ethanol and biodiesel are not the only biofuel routes the government supports — a separate family of schemes targets Compressed Bio-Gas (CBG) made from agricultural residue, cattle dung, municipal solid waste, and sewage-treatment-plant waste, and a further scheme — Pradhan Mantri JI-VAN Yojana — originally targeted 2G ethanol biorefineries specifically but was substantially broadened by a 2024 Cabinet amendment to cover a much wider range of advanced-biofuel feedstocks and technologies (detailed below).
SATAT ("Sustainable Alternative Towards Affordable Transportation") was launched by the Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas on 1 October 2018. Its mechanism: entrepreneurs set up Compressed Bio-Gas (CBG) plants using biomass/agricultural residue, cattle dung, and municipal solid waste as feedstock; oil marketing companies commit in advance to purchase the CBG produced, at a fixed procurement price, and sell it through their retail outlets alongside CNG — giving CBG producers a guaranteed offtake. SATAT's original stated ambition, widely reported at launch, was to establish CBG production at scale by the mid-2020s; the scheme's actual plant-count achievement against that original ambition needs re-verification against a direct MoPNG source before being treated as a frozen figure — this lesson teaches SATAT's mechanism and launch date as the stable facts, not a specific achieved-plant-count.
The name "GOBARdhan" itself is not new: it was first launched in 2018 under the then Ministry of Drinking Water and Sanitation's Swachh Bharat Mission (Grameen), for converting cattle dung and other bio-waste into biogas and bio-slurry for village sanitation and rural income (PIB PRID 2222150) — that department is now part of the Ministry of Jal Shakti, formed in 2019. In August 2026, the Union Cabinet approved a distinctly Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas-led scheme that adopts the same GOBARdhan name for a new, unified national Compressed Biogas financing/offtake mechanism — India's National Unified Scheme for Compressed Biogas — best understood as a successor and scale-up of the SATAT-era CBG effort under a pre-existing brand name, not as the origin of "GOBARdhan" as an initiative, and not as a competing scheme.

Is GOBARdhan the same thing as SATAT under a new name?
Not quite. SATAT was a market-mechanism scheme built around a guaranteed-procurement-price model for individual CBG plants, run since 2018 without a single fixed central outlay figure of its own. GOBARdhan is a distinctly Cabinet-approved, ten-year, ₹23,731-crore unified national scheme — it consolidates and scales up the same underlying goal (more CBG production from waste and agri-residue) into one funded, time-bound programme with its own budget line, rather than simply renaming SATAT. The safest exam framing is "successor/scale-up," not "rename."
The third scheme in this cluster originally targeted 2G ethanol biorefineries specifically: the Pradhan Mantri JI-VAN Yojana ("Jaiv Indhan – Vatavaran Anukool fasal awashesh Nivaran Yojana"), notified 07.03.2019, provides financial support for setting up commercial-scale advanced-biofuel bio-refineries — originally targeted narrowly at Second Generation (2G) ethanol projects using lignocellulosic biomass and agricultural residue as feedstock, but substantially broadened by a 2024 Cabinet amendment (below) to cover a wider range of advanced-biofuel technologies and feedstocks — precisely the 2G route the National Policy on Biofuels' ₹5,000 crore Viability Gap Funding (§4A) is meant to de-risk, among others now covered by the broadened scheme.

4D — Bharat Stage Emission Norms
Turn now from what goes into the fuel tank to what comes out of the exhaust. Bharat Stage (BS) emission norms are India's vehicular emission standards, regulating the permissible limits of a vehicle's exhaust emissions; the principal regulated pollutants include carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbons (HC), oxides of nitrogen (NOx), and particulate matter (PM), with additional metrics (such as particle number or NMHC) regulated for certain vehicle categories under the later BS-VI norms. They are modelled on the European Union's Euro emission standards and are regularly described using rough Euro-equivalence (BS-IV is broadly equivalent to Euro 4; BS-VI to Euro 6), though the equivalence is approximate rather than a line-by-line identical standard.
Think of BS stage numbers like software version numbers
Each higher BS number is a stricter, later "version" of the emission standard — tighter pollutant limits, better fuel-quality requirements, and (from BS-VI Phase 2 onward) more realistic testing conditions — much as a newer software version tightens what the previous version allowed. Just as a device can't always run the newest software without a hardware upgrade, a vehicle built for an older BS stage generally cannot simply be "updated" to meet a newer one without engine and after-treatment-system changes — which is exactly why each BS transition requires a nationwide manufacturing cut-off date, not a software-style patch.

The most-asked "why" question in this entire cluster is why BS-V was skipped altogether. On 6 January 2016, the government decided to leapfrog directly from BS-IV to BS-VI, advancing the nationwide BS-VI rollout to 1 April 2020 rather than first implementing an intermediate BS-V stage as the original roadmap had envisaged.
Why leapfrog straight to BS-VI instead of implementing BS-V first?
Two reasons work together. First, India's air-quality burden was judged severe enough to justify the bigger, faster leap directly to a Euro-6-equivalent standard, rather than taking two separate steps (BS-IV→BS-V→BS-VI) spread over a longer period. Second, and just as important administratively: implementing an intermediate BS-V stage would have meant refineries and vehicle manufacturers investing in BS-V-specific fuel-quality and engine/after-treatment upgrades that would then almost immediately need another round of upgrades for BS-VI a few years later — a "stranded investment" the leapfrog decision avoided by making one bigger jump instead of two smaller, wasteful ones.
BS-VI Phase 1 (from 1 April 2020) delivered a sharp cut in permitted pollutant levels relative to BS-IV — for the commonly cited passenger-car/light-duty comparison (exact limits vary by vehicle category and test cycle, and should not be read as a single flat figure across every vehicle class): for petrol vehicles, permissible NOx fell by roughly 25%; for diesel vehicles, the cuts were steeper still — combined HC+NOx down roughly 43%, NOx alone down roughly 68%, and particulate matter (PM) down roughly 82%. None of this would have been achievable on the old fuel alone — BS-VI is inseparable from a matching fuel-quality upgrade: sulphur content in both petrol and diesel was cut from 50 mg/kg (BS-IV) to 10 mg/kg (BS-VI), a five-fold reduction, because the advanced catalytic converters and particulate filters BS-VI engines rely on are themselves damaged or poisoned by higher-sulphur fuel — the fuel upgrade is what makes the engine-side pollutant cuts physically possible, not a separate, unrelated change.

A commonly cited effective date is 1 April 2023 (still provisional — see STATUS DATE). Separately, the Central Motor Vehicles (Fourth Amendment) Rules, 2026 replace the older MIDC laboratory drive cycle with the internationally harmonised WLTP (Worldwide Harmonized Light Vehicles Test Procedure, per AIS-175) for BS-VI Category M1 and Category M2 (up to 3,500 kg) vehicles, for models manufactured on or after 1 April 2027 — a confirmed, dated development.
MEASURE/DEFINITION: RDE/PEMS tests real-world on-road emissions rather than a fixed laboratory drive cycle; WLTP is a different, more realistic laboratory chassis-dynamometer drive cycle replacing the older MIDC cycle — the two are complementary layers (on-road testing, plus a more realistic lab cycle), not sequential "phases" of one single change, and neither has been officially branded "BS-VII" by the Government — no "BS-VII" emission standard has been officially notified or announced, and any exam option that names "BS-VII" as a confirmed standard should be treated as an unconfirmed distractor.
JURISDICTION SCOPE: All-India; Ministry of Road Transport & Highways, under the Central Motor Vehicles Rules, 1989 framework; RDE/PEMS testing sits alongside CPCB's separate vehicular-emissions mandate.
STATUS DATE: RDE/PEMS 1 April 2023 date — still provisional; CPCB's own vehicular-emissions pages were inaccessible during research, and the date has not yet been pinned to a specific CPCB/MoRTH gazette notification in this lesson's sourcing. WLTP/MIDC replacement — gazetted in early February 2026 (reported as 4 February 2026 by MarkLines Automotive Industry Portal and Times Drive, and as 5 February 2026 — rules in force from 7 February 2026 — by the independent regulatory tracker TeamLease RegTech); the G.S.R. 112(E) notification could not be directly retrieved from the official e-Gazette (egazette.gov.in) or a MoRTH press release to settle the single day, so treat the exact notification date as reported by trade press/regulatory trackers but not yet independently confirmed against a primary document — the 1 April 2027 effective/compliance date, by contrast, is undisputed across every source checked.
PRIMARY SOURCE: RDE/PEMS — no direct CPCB/MoRTH primary citation obtained for this lesson; treat as trade-press-level sourcing only until a primary document is found. WLTP/MIDC replacement — Gazette of India, G.S.R. 112(E), Central Motor Vehicles (Fourth Amendment) Rules, 2026, as reported by MarkLines Automotive Industry Portal and Times Drive (dating the notification 04.02.2026) and separately by TeamLease RegTech's regulatory tracker (dating the same notification 05.02.2026) — trade-press/regulatory-tracker corroboration of a primary gazette notification this lesson could not itself pull directly from the e-Gazette.
SOURCE-UPDATE DATE: RDE/PEMS — not available; no primary document has been located at all. WLTP/MIDC replacement — the same early-February-2026 gazetting date as above (reported as 4 or 5 February 2026 depending on source; not independently confirmed against the primary e-Gazette); this is a one-off gazette notification rather than a periodically-revised web page, so SOURCE-UPDATE DATE and STATUS DATE coincide here.
DYNAMIC?/CONFLICT: Do not present 1 April 2023 as a confirmed notified RDE date without a direct CPCB/MoRTH primary citation obtained at drafting/print time. The WLTP change is a genuinely new, separately-dated development — confirm before teaching that it is still described solely as a Central Motor Vehicles Rules amendment, since some trade coverage has already informally tagged it "BS6 phase 3," a label this lesson does not adopt because the Government has not used it. It also carries its own small, genuine date conflict — trade press (4 February) and a regulatory tracker (5 February) disagree on the exact notification day, and G.S.R. 112(E) could not be pulled directly via MoRTH/CPCB/e-Gazette (as of 13.09.2026) to adjudicate between them — so teach the 1 April 2027 compliance date as the firm fact, and teach the notification day only as "early February 2026, reported variously as 4th or 5th," not as settled.
CURRENT — CAFE-2 norms: adjacent to BS-VI, but a genuinely different regulation
FACT: CAFE (Corporate Average Fuel Efficiency) Stage 2 — India's fleet-average CO2 fuel-efficiency regulation, distinct from Bharat Stage.
VALUE: CAFE Stage 2 tightened the fleet-average CO2 ceiling from CAFE Stage 1's 130 g/km to 113 g/km, effective from 1 April 2022. This 113 g/km figure is the industry-wide reference target, not a flat ceiling every manufacturer or every vehicle must individually meet. Each manufacturer's own CAFE compliance target is mass-adjusted: it is derived by scaling the 113 g/km reference target by the ratio of that manufacturer's own fleet-average kerb weight to the industry-average kerb weight, so a manufacturer selling heavier vehicles is held to a proportionally higher (more lenient) target than one selling lighter vehicles. This mass-adjustment ratio formula is independently corroborated by a detailed automotive-industry technical explainer (Team-BHP), which states the identical relationship and worked a concrete example using an industry-average kerb weight of 1,145 kg — but this book has not yet directly read the BEE/MoRTH Gazette G.S.R. notification text that formally specifies this methodology, so the formula itself should still be checked against that primary text before being taught as verbatim-quotable in an exam answer, even though independent corroboration found no conflicting version of it. A statement like "CAFE-2 fixes 113 g/km for every individual vehicle or every manufacturer" is therefore a common MCQ trap — the correct framing is corporate-average and mass-adjusted, not a single flat number applied uniformly.
MEASURE/DEFINITION: CAFE norms are not a Bharat Stage. CAFE-2 sets a fleet-average CO2 (g/km) ceiling that each vehicle manufacturer's overall model range must meet — a fuel-efficiency/climate regulation, not a per-vehicle tailpipe-pollutant standard, administered under the Energy Conservation Act, 2001 framework rather than the Central Motor Vehicles Rules mechanism BS norms use. Where BS norms limit local pollutants — principally CO, HC, NOx, and PM, with further metrics regulated for certain vehicle categories under BS-VI — that affect air quality and health, CAFE norms limit carbon dioxide averaged across a manufacturer's whole fleet, targeting fuel economy and climate impact. A manufacturer can, in principle, be BS-VI compliant on every individual model while still needing to bring its overall fleet-average CO2 figure down to meet CAFE-2 — the two regulations are administered under different logics and must never be described as "the next stage of BS-VI."
JURISDICTION SCOPE: All-India; nodal monitoring by the Ministry of Road Transport & Highways (MoRTH), with standards framed under the Bureau of Energy Efficiency/Ministry of Power's Energy Conservation Act, 2001 mandate.
STATUS DATE: Effective from 1 April 2022; the 1 April 2022 effective date and the 113 g/km ceiling are confirmed against the Bureau of Energy Efficiency's own CAFE norms page as of 13.09.2026, but the exact gazette S.O./G.S.R. notification date could not be independently pinned within available sourcing.
PRIMARY SOURCE: Bureau of Energy Efficiency, CAFE norms page (udit.beeindia.gov.in/cafe/; beeindia.gov.in Fuel Efficiency page) — citing Gazette Notification S.O. 5020(E) (CAFE Stage-2 amendment); MoRTH is named on the same page as the nodal agency for annual fuel-consumption monitoring and reporting.
SOURCE-UPDATE DATE: Not independently pinned — BEE's CAFE norms page carries no visible last-revised stamp, so its content is treated as current only as of 13.09.2026, not as dated to a specific page-revision date.
DYNAMIC?/CONFLICT: No conflict found on the core 1 April 2022/113 g/km facts across sources checked. A further stage, CAFE-3, is separately reported as under development for a 2027-2032 window per trade coverage — do not confuse CAFE-3's still-future timeline with CAFE-2's already-effective one, and re-verify the exact S.O./G.S.R. notification date against a direct MoRTH/BEE gazette citation before print.
TRAP — "BS-VII" (still not notified — but official discourse has moved)
No "BS-VII" emission standard has been officially notified by the Government of India as of 13.09.2026. What has changed since is the discourse around it: at the 66th SIAM Annual Convention on 3 September 2026, MoRTH Secretary V. Umashankar stated that consultations on a post-BS-VI norm are in advanced stages and that a draft "should come in a couple of months," while flagging that manufacturers already juggling multiple powertrains (EVs, alternative fuels, IC engines) alongside tightening emission rules will find the implementation timeline challenging (reported via BusinessToday, Autocar India and Motorbeam, 03.09.2026 — trade-press reports of an on-record ministerial statement made at an industry convention, not yet a PIB/MoRTH document). This is still not a notification: no draft has been published, no effective date fixed, and "BS-VII" itself remains media/coaching shorthand rather than a name the Government has adopted. A statement asserting "BS-VII has been notified" or naming a BS-VII effective date should still be marked false — but so, now, would "no official discourse on a post-BS-VI norm exists." The safest exam framing is "under active consultation, not yet notified"; re-verify for a possible actual draft or notification before the exam, since a MoRTH Secretary naming a two-month horizon in September 2026 makes this one of the more likely near-term notifications in this book. One further point worth pre-empting: the WLTP test-cycle change notified for BS-VI M1/M2 vehicles from 1 April 2027 (§4D above) is also not "BS-VII" — it is a Central Motor Vehicles Rules amendment to how BS-VI itself is tested, not a new emission stage, even though some trade coverage has loosely called it "BS6 phase 3."
Finally, a brief but important scope note: the 2-wheeler and 3-wheeler emission-norm track is administered on its own separate timeline, distinct from the 4-wheeler track detailed above, though both tracks converged on the same nationwide BS-VI from 1 April 2020 cut-off. Treating "BS-I→BS-VI" as a single universal timeline that applies identically, stage-for-stage and year-for-year, to every vehicle category is a common oversimplification — the two-/three-wheeler track has had its own separate stage-implementation dates at various points, even though the two tracks currently sit at the same BS-VI standard.
4E — Electric Mobility: From FAME to PM E-DRIVE and PLI-ACC
Biofuels blend cleaner fuel into the tank and Bharat Stage tightens what the exhaust may emit — a third lever, electric propulsion, bypasses the exhaust pipe. Not itself named in this syllabus sub-clause, this stays a short overview — but a good answer should still place all three levers — fuel, tailpipe, powertrain — on one map.
The Ministry of Heavy Industries has run demand-incentive schemes for electric/hybrid vehicles since 2015 under the FAME banner — Faster Adoption and Manufacturing of (Hybrid &) Electric Vehicles, a NEMMP sub-scheme. FAME-I (2015–31 March 2019, ₹895 crore) supported roughly 2.8 lakh vehicles. FAME-II (2019–2024, ₹10,000 crore) supported roughly 16.3 lakh vehicles (mostly two-wheelers) and lapsed on its own sunset date, not from failure. A four-month bridge, the Electric Mobility Promotion Scheme (EMPS) 2024 (₹778 crore, e-2Ws/e-3Ws only), covered the gap until a full successor was ready.
CURRENT — PM E-DRIVE Scheme, the FAME successor
FACT: PM Electric Drive Revolution in Innovative Vehicle Enhancement (PM E-DRIVE) Scheme.
VALUE: Total outlay raised to ₹11,900 crore (from the original ₹10,900 crore); tenure extended to four years, now running through 31 March 2028 for every component, including e-2W/e-3W.
MEASURE/DEFINITION: A demand-incentive-plus-infrastructure scheme covering e-2Ws/e-3Ws, e-ambulances, e-trucks, and e-buses (14,028 buses targeted), plus public EV charging infrastructure, delivered via Aadhaar-authenticated e-vouchers.
JURISDICTION SCOPE: All-India; Ministry of Heavy Industries.
STATUS DATE: Cabinet approved 11 September 2024; extended to 31 March 2028 across all components and the outlay raised to ₹11,900 crore via a Ministry of Heavy Industries Gazette Notification dated 10 August 2026. Fund-limited — a component can still close early if its allocation is exhausted.
PRIMARY SOURCE: PIB, "Cabinet approves PM E-DRIVE Scheme" (PRID 2053889, 11.09.2024); Ministry of Heavy Industries, Gazette Notification, 10.08.2026 (no separate PIB PRID located; corroborated by Business Standard, 11.08.2026, and electrive.com, 14.08.2026).
SOURCE-UPDATE DATE: 10.08.2026 — the most recent underlying notification, superseding the 11.09.2024 release it followed.
DYNAMIC?/CONFLICT: This box previously froze the ₹10,900 crore outlay and the 31 March 2026 e-2W/e-3W terminal date — both overtaken within about eleven months by the 10.08.2026 notification above. Re-verify before the exam for any further outlay/tenure change.
Parallel to this, a supply-side scheme targets the battery itself — the costliest, most import-dependent EV component: the Ministry of Heavy Industries' Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme for Advanced Chemistry Cell (ACC) battery storage, aimed at building domestic giga-scale cell-manufacturing capacity rather than subsidising vehicle purchases.
CURRENT — PLI scheme for Advanced Chemistry Cell (ACC) battery storage
FACT: National Programme on Advanced Chemistry Cell (ACC) Battery Storage — Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme.
VALUE: Total outlay ₹18,100 crore; target capacity 50 GWh of ACC manufacturing, initially allotted across four selected bidders in 2022 (see DYNAMIC?/CONFLICT below for the current, reduced allocation).
MEASURE/DEFINITION: Incentivises domestic giga-scale battery-cell manufacturing, conditional on minimum domestic value addition (25% initially, rising to 60% within five years) and a minimum investment of ₹225 crore per GWh.
JURISDICTION SCOPE: All-India; Ministry of Heavy Industries.
STATUS DATE: Cabinet approved 12 May 2021; capacity allotted to the four bidders by 24 March 2022; implementation status reported as of October 2025.
PRIMARY SOURCE: PIB, "Allotment made for 50 GWh of battery capacity to 4 successful bidders" (PRID 1809037, 24.03.2022); Ministry of Heavy Industries, PLI-ACC scheme page (heavyindustries.gov.in).
SOURCE-UPDATE DATE: PIB release — 24.03.2022, unrevised since; MHI's own scheme page carries no visible update stamp, so is treated as current only as of 13.09.2026.
DYNAMIC?/CONFLICT: Running well behind schedule. An IEEFA analysis (Jan 2026) put commissioned capacity at only ~1.4 GWh — ~2.8% of the 50 GWh target — as of October 2025, with only ~40 GWh still allocated after some bidders scaled back, and no incentive yet disbursed. The Ministry's page and third-party trackers differ on "capacity allocated" (30 GWh vs 50 GWh) as of 13.09.2026 — re-verify before quoting an exact figure.

★ Quick Facts
  1. Scientific generations: 1G (sugarcane/molasses/foodgrains → ethanol; non-edible oil/UCO → biodiesel); 2G (agri-residue → ethanol); 3G (primarily algae-based routes — the least standardised tier). Policy categories (National Policy on Biofuels, 2018 — a separate system, do not force-map onto 1G/2G/3G): Basic Biofuels (1G ethanol + biodiesel); Advanced Biofuels (2G ethanol, drop-in fuels, bio-CNG, bio-methanol, bio-hydrogen).
  2. National Policy on Biofuels, 2018 — Cabinet approved 16 May 2018: Basic vs Advanced categorisation; ₹5,000 crore VGF for 2G ethanol bio-refineries; National Biofuel Coordination Committee (NBCC), chaired by the Petroleum Minister.
  3. 2022 Amendment (18 May 2022): E20 target advanced from 2030 to ESY 2025-26; expanded feedstock basket; export of biofuels permitted in specific cases; NBCC membership expanded.
  4. Ethanol blending trend: <1.5% (2013-14) → ~8.1% (ESY2020-21) → 10% (June 2022) → 12.1% (ESY2022-23) → 14.60% (ESY2023-24) → 19.20% (ESY2024-25) → 20% (ESY2025-26) — achieved ~5 years ahead of the original 2030 target, per PIB (05.07.2026 & 10.07.2026).
  5. Post-E20: no locked beyond-20% target; government "examining" phased E25/E27/E30, plus E85 flex-fuel promotion (from ~June 2026). NITI Aayog flags a mileage loss of ~6-7% (non-optimised 4-wheelers) and ~3-4% (non-optimised 2-wheelers) on E20 — re-verify exact figures before freezing.
  6. Biodiesel: 5% target by 2030 vs ~0.5% achieved (IOCL, FY2023-24) — a strong target-vs-achievement contrast; the achieved figure is weakly (trade-press) sourced.
  7. SATAT (MoPNG, launched 1 October 2018): CBG from biomass/agri-residue/cattle dung/MSW, sold via OMC outlets under a guaranteed-procurement model.
  8. GOBARdhan (Cabinet approved 6 August 2026): National Unified Scheme for Compressed Biogas; ₹23,731 crore, FY2026-27 to FY2035-36 — successor/scale-up of the SATAT-era approach; treat all parameters as provisional (scheme is very new).
  9. Pradhan Mantri JI-VAN Yojana: originally funded 2G ethanol bio-refineries only (lignocellulosic/agri-residue feedstock) — original-scope named plants: IOCL Panipat, BPCL Bargarh, HPCL Bathinda, NRL Numaligarh; the 2024 Cabinet amendment (09.08.2024) broadened the scheme well past 2G-only, to a wider "Advanced Biofuel Projects" scope (agri/forestry residue, industrial waste, synthesis gas, algae), added bolt-on/brownfield project eligibility, and extended the implementation timeline to 2028-29.
  10. BS norms' principal regulated pollutants include CO, HC, NOx and PM tailpipe emissions (plus further metrics for some vehicle categories under BS-VI); broadly Euro-equivalent (BS-VI ≈ Euro 6).
  11. BS-I→BS-VI timeline (4-wheeler, nationwide): BS-I (2000) → BS-II (2005) → BS-III (2010) → BS-IV (2017) → BS-V (skipped) → BS-VI (1 April 2020).
  12. BS-V was formally skipped by a decision dated 6 January 2016 — India leapfrogged straight from BS-IV to a Euro-6-equivalent BS-VI to avoid stranded intermediate investment and to secure a bigger, faster air-quality gain.
  13. BS-VI Phase 1 pollutant cuts (vs BS-IV): petrol NOx ↓~25%; diesel HC+NOx ↓~43%, NOx ↓~68%, PM ↓~82% — enabled by cutting fuel sulphur from 50 mg/kg to 10 mg/kg.
  14. BS-VI Phase 2 / RDE norms add on-road Real Driving Emissions (PEMS-based) testing to the earlier lab-cycle-only regime; commonly cited effective date 1 April 2023 is provisional, pending CPCB/MoRTH primary confirmation. A separate, confirmed change — replacing the MIDC test cycle with WLTP for BS-VI M1/M2 (≤3,500 kg) vehicles — is notified (G.S.R. 112(E), 04.02.2026) for models from 1 April 2027.
  15. CAFE-2 (from 1 April 2022) regulates fleet-average CO2 g/km — a fuel-efficiency/climate regulation, distinct from and not a Bharat Stage.
  16. "BS-VII" has not been officially notified — treat any exam statement claiming it exists or naming its effective date as false unless a genuine primary notification is later found.
  17. 2/3-wheelers run a separate emission-norm timeline track, though currently aligned with 4-wheelers at BS-VI (1 April 2020).
  18. Every headline figure in this lesson — ethanol %, biodiesel %, GOBARdhan's parameters, the BS-VI Phase 2 date — must be re-verified against a primary CEA/MNRE/PIB/CPCB/MoRTH source before the exam, per this book's standing production discipline.

TRAP — where an examiner twists it
Looks like thisActually this
"All fuel-ethanol produced in India is 1G ethanol"Ethanol can be 1G (molasses/grain) or 2G (agri-residue) — the generation depends on feedstock, not on the fact that the product is "ethanol"
"Biodiesel from used cooking oil is an Advanced biofuel"Under the National Policy on Biofuels' own two-category scheme, biodiesel (from non-edible oil/UCO/animal fat) is classed as a Basic biofuel, not Advanced — Advanced covers 2G ethanol and other Policy-designated routes such as bio-CNG (not the scientific 3G tier, which is a separate, distinct classification)
"CAFE-2 is simply the next phase of BS-VI"CAFE-2 is a separate fuel-efficiency/CO2-fleet-average regulation, administered on its own logic; BS-VI Phase 2/RDE is the actual next phase of BS-VI, regulating local pollutants — the two are adjacent, not sequential parts of one standard
"BS-VII has been notified as India's newest emission standard"No BS-VII norm has been officially notified — this is an unconfirmed distractor, not a fact
"India skipped BS-V purely to save money"Cost avoidance (no stranded BS-V-specific investment) was one reason, but the decision (06.01.2016) was equally about leapfrogging directly to a stricter, Euro-6-equivalent BS-VI sooner rather than taking two smaller steps
"GOBARdhan is just SATAT renamed"GOBARdhan is a distinctly Cabinet-approved (06.08.2026), ten-year, ₹23,731-crore unified national scheme — a successor/scale-up of the SATAT-era approach, not a mere rebranding of the same scheme
"PM JI-VAN Yojana funds ordinary (1G) ethanol distilleries"PM JI-VAN Yojana specifically funds 2G ethanol bio-refineries using lignocellulosic/agri-residue feedstock — 1G distilleries are supported through different mechanisms, not this scheme
"India achieved 20% ethanol blending on the original 2018 policy's own schedule"The original 2018 target year for 20% (E20) was 2030; the 2022 amendment advanced it to ESY 2025-26, and PIB reports it was met roughly five years ahead of the original 2030 schedule — the "on schedule" framing is only true against the amended, not the original, target
"Biodiesel blending is broadly on track, similar to ethanol"The two tell opposite stories: ethanol blending hit its (amended) target; biodiesel blending (~0.5% achieved vs a 5%-by-2030 target) has a large, unresolved gap — do not generalise ethanol's success to biodiesel
"BS-VI Phase 2/RDE testing is purely a laboratory procedure, like earlier BS stages"Phase 2/RDE specifically adds on-road, real-driving (PEMS-based) testing on top of the earlier lab-cycle-only regime — the shift from lab-only to lab-plus-road testing is the defining change of this phase
Why this lesson matters
Biofuels and Bharat Stage norms are the two levers India pulls on the demand side of transport energy — one changes what fuel goes in, the other changes what is allowed to come out — and both are unusually rich in the classification traps (biofuel generations, BS vs CAFE) and target-vs-achievement contrasts (ethanol's success, biodiesel's shortfall) that objective exams are built to test. Just as importantly, this lesson is a working example of the whole book's central production discipline: almost every fact here is dated, sourced, and explicitly flagged for re-verification, because in this cluster — more than almost anywhere else in the syllabus — the "correct" answer to a numeric question can genuinely change between the day this lesson is drafted and the day it is read in an exam hall.
