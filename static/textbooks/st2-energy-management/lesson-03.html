Lesson 3 — National and State Energy Policy

APPSC Group-II Mains · Paper-II Section-B · Sub-topic 2 — Energy Management · MASTER PLAN ST2, Lesson 3 of 6 · English draft — bilingual (Telugu/Tenglish) pass to follow · current-affairs references, where any appear, must be re-verified before the exam

The syllabus names just one item here — "National Energy Policy" — but the ground reality in 2026 is that two different documents, from two different institutions, both plausibly answer to that name. NITI Aayog put out a Draft National Energy Policy in June 2017 and it has never been finalised — nine years on, it is still exactly what its title says, a draft. Separately, the Ministry of Power released a Draft National Electricity Policy for public consultation in January 2026 — a narrower instrument confined to the electricity sector, and itself still a draft. An examiner who wants a clean, single-line trap has been handed one on a plate: this lesson exists to make sure you are never the student who confuses the two. Alongside them we trace the older 2006 predecessor that prominently formalised the phrase "energy security" in India's policy vocabulary, and then bring the story down to Andhra Pradesh's own state-level clean-energy policy and NITI Aayog's freshly drafted blueprint for the state.
3A — From the Integrated Energy Policy to the Draft National Energy Policy, 2017
A major early attempt to treat India's energy system through a single, economy-wide integrated policy framework came from the Planning Commission's Integrated Energy Policy (IEP), 2006 — the report of an Expert Committee chaired by economist Dr Kirit Parikh. Rather than treating coal, oil, electricity, and renewables as separate silos each with its own ministry and its own targets, the IEP tried to look at India's entire energy system as one integrated whole and ask a single question: how much energy, of what kinds, does India need to sustain a given rate of economic growth, and how much of that can the country secure reliably?
The IEP's most durable contribution to India's policy vocabulary was to prominently formalise "energy security" as a named policy category in its own right — not merely "how much energy do we produce," but "how reliably, affordably, and independently can India meet its energy needs, including in the face of external supply shocks." That framing — energy security as a distinct, first-class policy objective — is the thread this whole lesson follows: it reappears, restated and expanded, in the Draft National Energy Policy of 2017, and again in the Draft National Electricity Policy of 2026.
Think of the IEP as the blueprint before the building codes
An early master plan rarely survives untouched — later planners revise it, split it into separate codes for water, power, and roads, and update its numbers as the city grows. The IEP, 2006 is an early master plan for India's energy sector: it is a historical predecessor to the later 2017 and 2026 policy-drafting exercises, which have taken up and updated its analysis without themselves being finalised into a replacement policy, but the underlying idea it prominently advanced — treat energy holistically, and treat security of supply as a policy goal in its own right — is still the foundation everything after it builds on.
A decade on, the Draft National Energy Policy (NEP), 2017 picked up that same integrating ambition and gave it an institutional home. It was prepared by NITI Aayog — the Planning Commission's successor body — and released for public comments on 27 June 2017. Structurally, NEP 2017 was designed as an "umbrella policy": rather than replacing the individual ministries' own plans (coal, petroleum & natural gas, power, new & renewable energy, atomic energy each retain their own policies and schemes), it was meant to sit above all of them, coordinating their targets into one coherent national direction and resolving the inconsistencies that inevitably creep in when five or six ministries plan somewhat independently.
The four objectives of Draft NEP, 2017
NEP 2017 organised itself around four explicitly named objectives, and this fourfold structure is itself a strong candidate for a direct objective/classification question. (1) Access and affordability — ensuring every household has access to energy (particularly electricity and clean cooking fuel) at a price it can afford. (2) Security and independence — reducing India's dependence on energy imports and building resilience against external supply disruptions, the direct descendant of the IEP's "energy security" framing. (3) Sustainability — meeting energy needs in a way that is environmentally sustainable, including a growing role for renewables and a lower carbon trajectory. (4) Economic growth — ensuring the energy sector itself contributes efficiently to GDP growth rather than acting as a drag on it, through competitive pricing, efficient markets, and adequate investment.
NEP 2017 also carried a set of numeric targets reflecting the 2016-17 policy environment it was drafted in — most prominently, 175 GW of installed renewable energy capacity by 2022 and 40% non-fossil-fuel-based installed electric capacity by 2030 (the same Paris Agreement-era Nationally Determined Contribution figure India had committed to internationally around that time).
TRAP — these 2016-17 targets are historical illustrations of NEP 2017's original ambition, not today's live targets
Both the 175 GW-by-2022 and the 40%-by-2030 figures belong to the policy environment of 2016-17 and have since been superseded by later commitments and by actual achieved progress (India's non-fossil installed capacity share had already crossed roughly 50% years before 2030, and national ambition has since moved to the 500 GW non-fossil-by-2030 target — covered in Lesson 5). Do not quote either figure as a current national target: quote them only as evidence of what NEP 2017 itself set out to achieve at the time it was drafted.

Why has a draft policy stayed a draft for nine years? — the institutional/coordination angle
A widely repeated line of commentary — sourced to policy-analysis and current-affairs commentary rather than to any official government explanation, and taught here explicitly as commentary, not settled fact — points to an internal contradiction inside NEP 2017 itself: the same draft that commits India to rising shares of renewable energy and a lower-carbon growth path also discussed India's ambitions around coal production and, in some readings, coal-linked exports, without fully reconciling the two. A document trying to speak for coal, oil, gas, nuclear, and renewables all at once, coordinated across several ministries with genuinely different institutional priorities, is also simply a harder document to get every stakeholder to sign off on than a narrower, single-sector policy. This is a structural, sign-off problem specific to NEP 2017's own whole-of-energy design — Section 3B's box below covers the separate question of why the Ministry of Power did not simply keep waiting for that sign-off.
TRAP — "non-binding" criticism is commentary, not an official finding
A further criticism found in secondary/policy-commentary sources is that NEP 2017, even if it were finalised tomorrow, was designed as a coordinating "umbrella" framework rather than a binding regulatory instrument with enforceable targets — meaning individual ministries could in principle continue on their own paths regardless of what NEP says. This is a fair analytical point raised in commentary, but it is not an official government finding, and an APPSC-style question that presents it as settled fact rather than as one line of criticism should be treated with caution.
3B — The Draft National Electricity Policy, 2026
On 21 January 2026, the Ministry of Power released the Draft National Electricity Policy, 2026 for public consultation with stakeholders — a document explicitly framed around India's "Viksit Bharat @2047" vision (the goal of India becoming a developed nation by the 100th year of independence). This is important to hold apart from NEP 2017 from the very first sentence: the 2026 draft is issued by a different ministry, has a different (narrower) legal scope, and is designed to do a different job.
Structurally, the Draft National Electricity Policy, 2026 is designed to replace the National Electricity Policy, 2005 — the Ministry of Power's own earlier, electricity-sector-specific policy issued under Section 3 of the Electricity Act, 2003. Its scope is confined to the electricity sector alone (generation, transmission, distribution, and the regulatory/market architecture around them) — it does not attempt to cover coal, oil and gas, or the whole cross-ministry energy landscape the way NEP 2017's "umbrella" framing does.
Key numeric targets in the Draft National Electricity Policy, 2026
Per-capita electricity consumption: from a baseline of 1,460 kWh (FY2024-25, the same figure carried over from Lesson 1) to a target of 2,000 kWh by 2030 and over 4,000 kWh by 2047 — roughly a 2.7-times increase over the baseline by the Viksit Bharat @2047 horizon.
Emissions intensity: a 45% reduction in emissions intensity from 2005 levels by 2030 — the same headline figure that also appears in India's Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) commitments.
Net-zero: alignment with India's national net-zero-by-2070 commitment.
Nuclear capacity: an ambitious 100 GW of nuclear capacity by 2047 — more than eleven times the roughly 8,780 MW installed nuclear capacity of December 2025 referenced in Lesson 2 — reflecting a much larger role envisaged for nuclear power in the electricity-sector mix over the coming decades. This is not a second, independently-arrived-at figure: it is the same underlying DAE Nuclear Energy Mission target (linked to Union Budget 2025-26, and detailed with its own trajectory — 8,780 MW → 22,380 MW by 2031-32 → 47 GW by 2037 → 67 GW by 2042 → 100 GW by 2047 — in Lesson 2) restated here in its electricity-policy context, one government ambition cited in two places rather than two policy bodies coincidentally agreeing.

Why did the Ministry of Power draft its own separate electricity policy instead of simply finalising NEP 2017? — the urgency/speed angle
Set the coordination difficulty covered in Section 3A's box aside for a moment and look at the calendar instead: the Electricity Act, 2003 itself calls for the Union government to issue a National Electricity Policy periodically, and the one actually in force — the National Electricity Policy, 2005 — was already two decades old by 2025, drafted for a power sector that barely had grid-scale solar or battery storage, let alone the distribution-sector stress and market reforms India's electricity system has since accumulated. That obsolescence created a statutory and operational urgency NEP 2017's coordination problem does nothing to relieve: whatever the reasons a nine-year-old, cross-ministry umbrella draft has for remaining unsigned, the electricity sector could not simply wait on it, because it needed a current instrument now, not eventually. So the Ministry of Power moved on the narrower, more tractable electricity-only piece on its own timeline rather than let the sector run indefinitely on a two-decade-old policy pending NEP 2017's uncertain resolution.

★ THE DISAMBIGUATION — "National Energy Policy" ≠ "National Electricity Policy"
This is the single most important teaching point in this entire lesson, and a strong candidate for one of the most exam-relevant traps in this whole book. Two real, currently-live documents both plausibly answer to the syllabus phrase "National Energy Policy" — and they are not the same document.

Document 1 — the Draft National Energy Policy, 2017. Issued by NITI Aayog. Released for public comment on 27 June 2017. Scope: the whole energy sector — coal, oil & gas, electricity, renewables, nuclear — coordinated as one "umbrella" policy across multiple ministries. Status as of this lesson's drafting date: still an unfinalised draft, roughly nine years after release.

Document 2 — the Draft National Electricity Policy, 2026. Issued by the Ministry of Power. Released for public consultation on 21 January 2026. Scope: the electricity sector only — designed specifically to replace the older National Electricity Policy, 2005. Status as of this lesson's drafting date: still a draft, under public consultation.

If a question simply says "National Energy Policy" with no year or issuing body attached, do not assume it means the 2026 electricity-only draft, and do not assume it means the 2017 NITI Aayog draft either — check which year, which ministry, and which scope the question actually names, because both are real, both are currently drafts, and an examiner who knows this topic well can build an entire question around exactly this confusion.

One further warning: the government's own PIB press release announcing the 2026 document is itself titled "Draft National Electricity Policy (NEP), 2026 Released for Public Consultation with Stakeholders" (PRID 2216661) — the source material uses the bare acronym "NEP" for both the 2017 whole-of-energy draft and the 2026 electricity-only draft. This lesson therefore never abbreviates the 2026 document as "NEP" anywhere in its own running text (it is always written out as "the Draft National Electricity Policy, 2026" or "the 2026 Electricity Policy"), precisely because the primary source itself cannot be relied on to keep the two apart. A reader who encounters "NEP" in any external source — including an official one — must independently check which document is meant from year and issuing body, never from the bare acronym alone.

A third document also answers to this abbreviation: CEA's statutory National Electricity Plan (NEP), 2022-32, notified under Section 3(4) of the Electricity Act, 2003 — see Lesson 1 §1E2. Three real documents, not two, currently share the letters "NEP"; always match the abbreviation to its issuing body, legal status, and year before answering an exam question that uses it.
A related but separate legislative strand runs alongside these two policy documents: the Ministry of Power's Draft Electricity (Amendment) Bill, 2025, released for public comments on 9 October 2025, proposes to amend the Electricity Act, 2003 itself — the same Act under whose Section 3 the National Electricity Policy, 2005 and its Draft 2026 replacement (discussed above) are issued. Where the two National/National Electricity Policy documents are policy instruments, this Bill is a proposed law; its energy-management-relevant strand includes proposed monetary penalties (reported at 35-45 paise per unit of shortfall) for non-compliance with Renewable Purchase Obligations, and a proposed empowerment of the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission to use market mechanisms in pursuit of India's 500 GW non-fossil-capacity-by-2030 target (Lesson 5). Its other major strands — shared distribution-network access, phased cross-subsidy removal, and a proposed Union-State "Electricity Council" — fall in distribution-sector-reform territory this book does not treat in depth.

3C — Andhra Pradesh's Own Energy Policy
Energy policy in India is not solely a Union-government subject — electricity sits in the Concurrent List of the Constitution, and states run substantial policy programmes of their own, particularly around attracting renewable-energy investment. Andhra Pradesh's own flagship instrument here is the AP Integrated Clean Energy Policy 4.0, announced on 18 October 2024 — the fourth iteration of the state's clean-energy policy framework, reflecting a state government that has revised and re-issued its energy policy repeatedly as circumstances (and its own investment ambitions) have changed.

Reading a state policy's numbers the way you'd read a company's five-year plan
Treat AP ICE Policy 4.0's targets the way you would a company's five-year business plan: an investment goal (₹10 lakh crore), a set of physical-capacity milestones to be built toward that goal (78.5 GW solar, 35 GW wind, storage), supporting infrastructure to make the core product usable (storage, EV charging), and a long-run mission statement (net-zero by 2047) that all the intermediate milestones are meant to serve.
Beyond the state's own policy, a fresh, still-evolving initiative links Andhra Pradesh directly back to the Union government's institutions: NITI Aayog's "ASSET" (Accelerating Sustainable State Energy Transition) initiative, launched in collaboration with the Ministry of Power and the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy. Under this platform, NITI Aayog prepared a draft energy-transition blueprint specifically for Andhra Pradesh, presented in early February 2026, with the explicit goal of positioning AP among India's top three renewable-energy hub states by 2035.

How does the energy-security thread tie IEP 2006, NEP 2017, and the 2026 Electricity Policy together?
Follow the same idea across three documents, forty years apart in spirit if not in years: the IEP, 2006 prominently formalised "energy security" as a distinct national policy goal, meaning reliable, affordable, and reasonably independent energy supply. NEP 2017 carried that forward explicitly as its second of four objectives ("security and independence"). The Draft National Electricity Policy, 2026 restates the same underlying concern, but this time with a fully quantified delivery mechanism attached rather than a restated principle: the per-capita consumption, emissions-intensity, and nuclear-capacity figures in its own key-numeric-targets box (Section 3B above) operationalise several overlapping objectives — including access, energy security, decarbonisation, and self-reliance — rather than "security and independence" alone, all under the wider "Viksit Bharat @2047" self-reliance framing. The vocabulary evolves — "energy security" becomes "Atmanirbhar" self-reliance becomes "Viksit Bharat @2047" — but the underlying policy problem each generation of drafters is trying to solve is the same one the Kirit Parikh Committee prominently formalised in 2006. What changes across the three restatements, in other words, is not the goal but the instrument: a named category (2006) → an explicit objective in a four-part framework (2017) → a bundle of dated, numeric delivery targets (2026).

  13. 2026 Electricity Policy status: still a draft, under public consultation as of this lesson's drafting date — re-verify before the exam.
  14. THE key distinction: "National Energy Policy" (NITI Aayog, 2017, whole-of-energy, still draft after 9 years) is NOT the same document as "National Electricity Policy" (Ministry of Power, 2026, electricity-only, in consultation).
  15. A third "NEP": CEA's statutory National Electricity Plan (NEP), 2022-32, notified under Section 3(4) of the Electricity Act, 2003 (see Lesson 1 §1E2) — three real documents, not two, share the letters "NEP"; always check issuing body, legal status, and year.
  16. Energy-security framing runs as a connecting thread: IEP 2006 named it → NEP 2017 made it the second of four named objectives → the 2026 Electricity Policy's Viksit Bharat @2047 framing cashes it out as dated, numeric delivery targets (per-capita consumption, emissions-intensity, nuclear capacity) rather than a restated principle.
  17. AP Integrated Clean Energy Policy 4.0 — announced 18 October 2024; a five-year state policy targeting ₹10 lakh crore investment and net-zero by 2047; its exact G.O. number needs direct confirmation before being quoted as settled fact.
  18. AP ICE Policy 4.0 capacity-mix targets: 78.5 GW solar, 35 GW wind, 22 GW pumped storage, 25 GWh battery storage, 1.5 MTPA green hydrogen.
  19. AP ICE Policy 4.0 biofuel targets (corrected, provisional pending primary-G.O. confirmation): ethanol 1,500 KLPD (≈1.5 MLD — NOT 1,500 MLD, a common unit error) and bio-CNG/CBG 10,000 TPD (NOT 1,000 TPD, a common order-of-magnitude error).
  20. NITI Aayog–AP "ASSET" blueprint — a draft energy-transition blueprint (Feb 2026) aiming to make Andhra Pradesh one of India's top three renewable-energy hub states by 2035; still evolving, re-check status before the exam.
★ PYQ · TRAP · CURRENT
TRAP — where an examiner twists it
Looks like thisActually this
"India's National Energy Policy was released by the Ministry of Power in 2026"The Ministry of Power's 2026 document is the Draft National Electricity Policy — a narrower, electricity-only instrument. The Draft National Energy Policy is NITI Aayog's whole-of-energy 2017 document — two different documents, two different institutions, two different scopes
"The National Energy Policy, 2017 has been notified and is now in force"It remains an unfinalised draft roughly nine years after release — "released for public comments" is not the same as "notified"
"The Draft National Electricity Policy, 2026 has already replaced the 2005 policy"As of this lesson's drafting date it is still a draft under public consultation — it is designed to replace the 2005 policy once finalised, but has not done so yet
"NEP 2017's target was 40% non-fossil capacity by 2030, so that is India's current target"That figure belongs to NEP 2017's original 2016-17 policy environment and has since been superseded by later commitments and by capacity actually achieved; treat it as historical, not current
"NEP 2017 was a binding law that all ministries had to follow"Even proponents describe it as a non-binding "umbrella" coordinating framework, not an enforceable regulatory instrument — and this criticism itself is commentary, not an official finding
"The Integrated Energy Policy, 2006 and the National Energy Policy, 2017 are the same document, just renamed"They are sequential, separate documents from separate bodies — IEP 2006 from the Planning Commission (historical predecessor), NEP 2017 from NITI Aayog (its institutional successor), building on but not identical to the earlier one
"AP's Integrated Clean Energy Policy 4.0 is the state's first-ever clean energy policy"The "4.0" in its name signals it is the fourth iteration of the state's clean-energy policy framework, not a first attempt
"The NITI Aayog–AP ASSET blueprint is a finalised, notified state policy"It is an early-2026 draft blueprint, still evolving — not a notified Union or state policy
"Energy security is a concept the 2026 Electricity Policy invented"The concept traces back to the Integrated Energy Policy, 2006, which prominently formalised it as a distinct policy category; NEP 2017 and the 2026 Electricity Policy both restate and update the same underlying thread
"Since NEP 2017 is 'draft' and the 2026 Electricity Policy is 'draft,' the two statuses mean the same level of progress"Both are technically unfinalised drafts, but their histories differ sharply — NEP 2017 has been stalled for about nine years, while the 2026 Electricity Policy was released only recently and is still moving through its initial consultation phase; do not treat "draft" as a single undifferentiated status without checking each one's own age and stage
Why this lesson matters
Every other lesson in this book teaches you facts and figures about India's energy system; this lesson teaches you about the governance documents that are supposed to steer that system — and it deliberately puts front and centre the single trickiest naming trap the whole syllabus offers on this sub-topic. Get the Integrated Energy Policy → Draft National Energy Policy (NITI, 2017) → Draft National Electricity Policy (Ministry of Power, 2026) lineage straight, keep the two 2026-adjacent drafts firmly apart by issuing body and scope, and treat every status claim in this lesson as something to re-verify at drafting-and-exam time rather than as a frozen fact — because as this lesson itself demonstrates, in this sub-topic the policy documents move almost as fast as the capacity numbers do.
