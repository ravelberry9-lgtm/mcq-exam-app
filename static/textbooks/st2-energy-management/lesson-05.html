Lesson 5 — Renewable Energy: Sources and Installed Capacities

APPSC Group-II Mains · Paper-II Section-B · Sub-topic 2 — Energy Management · MASTER PLAN ST2, Lesson 5 of 6 · English draft — bilingual (Telugu/Tenglish) pass to follow · current-affairs references, where any appear, must be re-verified before the exam

Lesson 1 already drew the basic line between renewable and non-renewable energy and worked through the CEA's capacity-vs-demand vocabulary. This lesson stays inside the renewable half of that line and goes deep: which sources make up India's renewable energy (RE) portfolio, how large each one actually is right now, which of them are growing fastest, and — importantly for the exam — how "installed capacity" differs conceptually from "share of actual generation" (a stock-vs-flow distinction worth knowing, illustrated later in this lesson with one simple analogy), a distinction examiners are known to test directly. The schemes and programmes that drive this growth (PM Surya Ghar, PM-KUSUM, the National Green Hydrogen Mission's scheme architecture, RPO, Green Energy Corridor, the International Solar Alliance) are deliberately held back for Lesson 6 — this lesson is about sources and numbers, not schemes.
A one-line recap from NCERT Class 10 Science, since Lesson 1 covered the classification in full: a source is renewable if it is naturally replenished on a human timescale and does not get "used up" — sunlight, wind, flowing/falling water, biomass, and geothermal heat all qualify. The basic conversion mechanisms worth remembering are: solar photovoltaic (PV) — sunlight striking a semiconductor (typically silicon) directly generates a voltage via the photovoltaic effect, producing DC electricity without any moving parts; wind — moving air turns turbine blades, which spin a rotor connected to a generator, converting kinetic energy into mechanical rotation and then into electrical energy; and biomass/anaerobic digestion — organic matter (crop residue, animal dung, municipal waste) is either combusted directly for heat/power or broken down by bacteria in an oxygen-free (anaerobic) digester to release biogas (chiefly methane), which is then burned for heat or electricity. These three mechanisms underlie almost everything that follows in this lesson.

TRAP — "renewable-only" capacity, "RE + large hydro," and "non-fossil" are three different numbers, not three names for one number
A statement-based question can quietly swap one of these three totals for another. Renewable-only (MNRE's own count) excludes both large hydro and nuclear — 243.49 GW. RE + large hydro adds hydro projects above 25 MW back in — 295.55 GW — because hydro is scientifically renewable even though MNRE's own scheme-administration convention tracks it apart from "small hydro." Non-fossil is the widest of the three, adding nuclear on top — 304.33 GW — since nuclear, while not renewable in the strict fuel-replenishment sense, is also not a fossil fuel. Do not treat any pair of these as interchangeable; each has its own precise membership.

Second, bio-power, small hydro and waste-to-energy together still add up to barely 7% of the RE-only total — they matter for the exam (each is a separately-reported MNRE category, and therefore useful source-wise MCQ material) but they are minor in scale next to solar and wind.
Why do MNRE and CEA sometimes report slightly different percentages for the same idea?
Because they are rarely quoting the same snapshot date or the same denominator. Lesson 1 recorded CEA's own figures — total installed generation capacity of 551,994.76 MW and a "RES share" of 43.42% (non-fossil share 54.44%) — as on 31.07.2026. This lesson's MNRE figures above are dated 31.08.2026, one month later, and MNRE's "RE only" total (243.49 GW) can differ from CEA's "RES" category (Lesson 1) because the two track slightly different reporting scopes and are rarely published on the same as-of date — treat them as close cousins reporting the same underlying reality at different snapshots, not as two measurements of one identical quantity. Never divide one agency's numerator by another agency's denominator from a different date — that is exactly the kind of silent error a rushed exam-answer can fall into.

Why utility-scale still dominates
Think of it the way a large factory beats many small workshops on sheer output per rupee of setup effort: a single ground-mounted solar park spread over hundreds of acres can be built, financed, and connected to the grid far faster per installed megawatt than convincing millions of individual households or farms to add rooftop or off-grid systems one at a time. That is why ground-mounted capacity (123.99 GW) still dwarfs rooftop (32.59 GW) even though rooftop solar gets more day-to-day public attention through subsidy schemes.
TRAP pre-empt — "solar is India's single largest installed source"
Within the renewable-only portfolio, solar (168.04 GW) is unambiguously the largest single category — it has overtaken wind by a wide margin. But do not extend this to "solar is India's single largest source of installed capacity, full stop": coal-based thermal capacity alone (see Lesson 2) still substantially exceeds total solar capacity. Read "largest source" claims carefully for their scope — largest within renewables is a different, narrower claim than largest overall.
5B2 — Large Hydro as a Renewable Source
Lesson 1 already flagged this boundary once, in the abstract, while setting up CEA's "RES vs non-fossil" split; this lesson's own RE-only totals make it concrete. Large hydro (conventionally, plants above 25 MW installed capacity — the same 25 MW line, read from the other side, that defines "small hydro" below) is genuinely renewable in the strict physical sense: the hydrological cycle — evaporation, rainfall, river flow — continuously replenishes the water that turns the turbines, exactly as sunlight and wind are continuously replenished. Yet CEA's own "RES" bookkeeping category deliberately excludes large hydro, tracking it instead inside the broader "non-fossil" category alongside nuclear power. Every RE-only capacity figure quoted elsewhere in this lesson — solar, wind, small hydro, bio-power, waste-to-energy — sits inside that RES boundary; large hydro is a genuine renewable technology that simply sits outside it, by CEA's bookkeeping convention rather than by physical fact.
Large hydro projects come in two broad operating types. Reservoir (storage) hydropower impounds water behind a dam in a large reservoir, so the utility can release water — and therefore generate power — largely on demand, independent of the river's moment-to-moment natural inflow; this gives reservoir hydro strong dispatchability, making it valuable for peaking and grid-balancing. Run-of-river hydropower, by contrast, has little or no storage: generation tracks the river's natural flow closely, so output rises and falls with seasonal and monsoonal flow patterns and offers far less flexibility to ramp output up or down on demand than a reservoir scheme.
TRAP — the same 25 MW line separates "large hydro" from "small hydro," read from opposite sides
One convention, two labels. Hydro projects above 25 MW are "large hydro" — tracked outside CEA's/MNRE's RES/RE-only totals, inside "non-fossil" instead. Hydro projects up to 25 MW are "small hydro" — counted inside RES/RE-only, alongside solar, wind, bio-power and waste-to-energy (see 5C, below). A statement-based question can quietly swap "large" for "small" hydro, or misstate which side of the 25 MW line sits inside the renewable-only count — treat this threshold as a clean, testable definitional trap in its own right, not a detail to skim past.
Reservoir-type large hydro's ability to ramp output up and down quickly gives it a genuine grid-balancing/peaking role — a natural complement as variable solar and wind capacity keeps growing, in the same spirit as the storage/flexibility theme Lesson 6 develops through battery energy storage systems (BESS) and pumped storage hydro (PSH). Pumped storage hydro is, in fact, best understood as a specialised large-hydro variant, purpose-built to shift energy in time rather than to add new firm generation — Lesson 6 carries PSH's own capacity and policy detail. Large hydro projects also carry environmental and social costs distinct from solar or wind — submergence of land, displacement of communities, and disruption to riverine ecology among them — a recognition-level caution worth keeping in mind here rather than a detailed treatment at this stage.

5C — Wind, Small Hydro, Biomass and Waste-to-Energy
Wind is the second pillar of the RE portfolio, but its story has two very different halves — one built, one still only planned.
Onshore wind stood at 58,520.32 MW (58.52 GW) as on 31.08.2026 (MNRE Physical Progress) — entirely land-based turbines, and, at nearly a quarter of the RE-only total, easily the second-largest real capacity block in this lesson after solar. Unlike solar, which has scaled up mostly over the last decade, India's wind programme has been running since the 1980s; that longer history is part of why wind's growth today looks less like a sudden boom and more like decades of steady, geographically concentrated build-out, layered with an emerging second growth channel — repowering old sites — alongside fresh capacity addition.
Why is onshore wind capacity so concentrated in just a handful of states?
Wind speed and consistency, not policy preference, decide where turbines actually get built, and India's strongest, most reliably windy land sits in a narrow set of locations. Gujarat and Tamil Nadu between them host the largest share of the country's installed wind fleet: Gujarat's Kutch and Saurashtra coastal belt catches strong, steady sea breezes and monsoon-season wind off the Arabian Sea, while Tamil Nadu's wind corridor around Tirunelveli, Coimbatore and Kanyakumari benefits from the Palghat Gap — a natural break in the Western Ghats that funnels and accelerates monsoon wind through an otherwise mountain-blocked stretch of the peninsula, a textbook physical-geography feature in its own right. Karnataka's Deccan-plateau belt (Chitradurga, Gadag, Davanagere) and Andhra Pradesh's Anantapur–Kurnool corridor share the same elevated, largely open terrain that makes large wind farms straightforward to site; Rajasthan's Jaisalmer–Barmer desert plains add sparse population and abundant flat land to genuinely strong desert wind; Maharashtra rounds out the group via its own stretch of the Western Ghats wind belt. This is the same underlying logic behind small hydro's site-limited growth (below): wind, like flowing water, cannot be sited wherever demand happens to be — generation follows the resource, not the other way round.
India's wind fleet is now old enough that a meaningful share of it qualifies for repowering — replacing an ageing, low-capacity turbine with a taller, more efficient modern one on the same well-sited ground — rather than growth coming only from fresh land. MNRE's National Repowering and Life Extension Policy for Wind Power Projects (issued 07.12.2023) targets turbines rated below 2 MW that have reached, or are approaching, the end of their design life, and identifies roughly 25.4 GW of such older sub-2 MW capacity as eligible for modernisation — turbines capable of generating around 1.5 times more annual output once replaced or refurbished, without needing a fresh site at all, and with a maximum extended lifespan of 40 years from original commissioning. Repowering is thus a second, distinct growth lever for onshore wind, running alongside greenfield capacity addition in the states named above.
TRAP — offshore wind: policy exists, commissioned capacity does not
India has repeatedly announced offshore wind ambitions — a national offshore wind energy policy framework, potential estimates of roughly 70 GW along the Gujarat and Tamil Nadu coastlines, a Viability Gap Funding (VGF) scheme to de-risk early projects, and ministerial statements (including one on 18.02.2026 describing offshore wind as a potential "strong pillar" of the country's clean-energy future) — but as of this lesson's research, zero offshore wind capacity has actually been commissioned in India. SECI's earlier tender round — a 500 MW Gujarat VGF project and a separate 4,000 MW Tamil Nadu seabed-lease auction, together 4.5 GW — was cancelled in August 2025 after drawing minimal developer interest; analysts had pegged the tariff developers would actually need at roughly ₹9.60/kWh (Tamil Nadu) and ₹10.50/kWh (Gujarat), far above the tender's own ₹4/kWh and ₹4.5/kWh ceiling tariffs. MNRE has since redesigned the tender around the original Cabinet-approved (19.06.2024) ₹7,453 crore VGF package — ₹6,853 crore of installation support plus ₹600 crore for upgrading two ports — still scoped to the same 1 GW (500 MW Gujarat + 500 MW Tamil Nadu), though as of this lesson's research MNRE was still weighing whether to proceed immediately given weak global offshore-wind demand and rising project costs. A separate, standing incentive — a complete waiver of inter-state transmission (ISTS) charges for 25 years on offshore wind projects commissioned on or before 31 December 2032 (PIB, PRID 1928128, 29.05.2023) — remains layered on top of this VGF support. This is precisely the "policy vs ground reality" gap the research audit flagged as a strong analytical angle: a policy framework and a funding scheme are not the same thing as installed, generating turbines. Any question that implies India already has offshore wind capacity in its RE mix should be treated as false.
Small hydro power (conventionally, projects up to 25 MW capacity — larger plants are classified as "large hydro" and tracked outside the RE-only total) stood at 5,181.76 MW (5.18 GW) as on 31.08.2026. It is a mature, slow-growing category — small hydro sites are geographically limited to locations with suitable perennial flow and head, unlike solar and wind, which can be sited far more flexibly.
Biomass/bio-power is reported by MNRE in two cogeneration sub-categories: bagasse cogeneration (9,821.32 MW) — power generated by sugar mills burning bagasse, the fibrous residue left after crushing sugarcane, often alongside process steam for the mill itself — and non-bagasse cogeneration (1,047.85 MW) — cogeneration using other biomass feedstock (agricultural residue, rice husk, and similar). Together this is 10,869.17 MW (≈10.87 GW). Biomass power sits under MNRE's broader National Bioenergy Programme umbrella; that programme's full structure is Lesson 6's territory, not this one's.
Waste-to-energy (WtE) — electricity generated from municipal solid waste and similar refuse — is the smallest RE-only category, at 324.24 MW grid-connected plus 554.16 MW off-grid, a combined 878.40 MW (≈0.88 GW) as on 31.08.2026. Its scale is dwarfed by every other category in this lesson, but it is a distinctly-reported MNRE category and remains examinable in its own right, particularly as a bridge to the waste-management sub-topic covered in this book series' `book_st4`.
Green hydrogen is best understood, at this stage of the book, as an emerging policy pillar rather than a meaningfully MW-reported source. Hydrogen produced by splitting water using electricity from renewable sources (electrolysis) is described as "green" precisely because its production draws on RE capacity rather than fossil fuel; the Union Cabinet approved the National Green Hydrogen Mission on 04.01.2023. Because green hydrogen is a manufactured energy carrier rather than a generation source with an "installed capacity" in the solar/wind sense, MNRE's Physical Progress page (the table above) does not carry a comparable green-hydrogen MW figure the way it does for solar or wind. Treat green hydrogen here only as a name and a concept to recognise; the Mission's production target, outlay, and scheme architecture are built out fully in Lesson 6.
Why does green hydrogen still belong in this lesson's map, even without a capacity number?
The exam can ask "which of the following is a renewable energy source/carrier in India's current policy framework" — a classification-style question exactly like the ones the research audit confirmed are common. Green hydrogen deserves a place on that list even though it cannot yet be slotted into a GW bar chart the way solar or wind can; the distinction between an energy source (sunlight, wind) and an energy carrier (hydrogen, which must first be produced using energy from somewhere else) is itself a fair definitional trap. In exam-answer shorthand: is hydrogen a primary renewable-energy source? No. Is green hydrogen a renewable-derived energy carrier? Yes — produced using electricity from renewable sources, but not itself a generation source with its own installed-capacity figure.
Geothermal and tidal energy are worth recognising by name even though neither has any commissioned utility-scale capacity in India yet. Geothermal energy (heat drawn from the earth's crust) remains at the pilot/exploratory stage — Ladakh's Puga Valley, in the Himalayan geothermal belt, is the most commonly cited site. Tidal energy (harnessing the rise and fall of ocean tides) is similarly pre-commercial — the Gulf of Khambhat and Gulf of Kutch in Gujarat are the two locations most often named for their tidal-range potential, but studies and small pilot proposals have not translated into any commissioned capacity. Offshore wind belongs in the same "recognise, don't overweight" bucket: a real national offshore wind policy framework and Viability Gap Funding (VGF) scheme exist, and tenders have been floated — but one major tender round (4.5 GW, Gujarat + Tamil Nadu) was cancelled in August 2025 after weak developer interest, and zero offshore wind capacity has actually been commissioned in India so far. Treat all three here only as names to recognise, not as meaningful lines in India's installed-capacity mix.

THE core trap — "installed capacity share" is not "share of generation/demand met"
This is the single most important distinction in this lesson, and the research audit confirms it is explicitly flagged by coaching material as a common student error. Installed capacity share is a stock measure — what fraction of the equipment sitting on the grid (measured in MW/GW) belongs to renewable sources, regardless of whether that equipment is actually generating power at any given moment. Lesson 1's CEA figure of "RES = 43.42% of installed capacity" (as on 31.07.2026) is exactly this kind of number. Share of generation, or share of demand/peak-demand met, is a flow measure — what fraction of the electricity actually flowing through the grid over some period, or at some instant, was supplied by renewables. A frequently quoted figure of this second kind is that on 29 July 2025, renewables met roughly 51.5% of the country's total electricity demand of 203 GW that day (PIB, PRID 2183866, "India achieved Historic milestone in power sector") — note the source's own wording is "total electricity demand," not "peak power demand"; the two are often loosely conflated in secondary/coaching-material reporting, and this lesson corrects that loose paraphrase rather than repeating it. This number looks like it contradicts the ~43% installed-capacity figure, but does not, because the two are measuring completely different things: one is "what fraction of the machines are RE machines," the other is "what fraction of the electricity flowing on one high-RE day came from those machines." Neither number can be substituted for the other in an answer. A further complication is worth naming explicitly, precisely because this lesson's core teaching point is never confusing a stock figure with a flow figure: a second, genuinely distinct official statistic — non-fossil installed-capacity share reported at a very similar 51.56% (254.02 GW of a 509.74 GW all-India installed base, as on 30.11.2025 — CEA, Executive Summary on Power Sector, November 2025 (Actual)) — carries almost the same "~51.5%" headline purely by coincidence, while actually being a stock measure for a different total (non-fossil, not RE-only) on a different date; treat any bare "51.5%" claim in this space as ambiguous until its measure and date are confirmed. (All figures here are dated and specific enough that they must be re-verified against CEA's own daily/monthly generation reports before being quoted as current in the exam — this lesson passes them on exactly as the research audit recorded them, with that caveat attached.)
A quick everyday analogy for the stock-vs-flow trap
Imagine a car-sharing fleet where 43% of the parked cars are electric. That is the "installed capacity share" — a fact about the fleet's composition, true all day regardless of who is driving. Now imagine that, during one particular rush hour, 51.5% of the cars actually out on the road happen to be electric — perhaps because that hour coincides with when electric-car owners commute. That second number describes the traffic at one moment, not the fleet as a whole; a low-generating fossil "car" can sit idle in the depot exactly the way a thermal plant can sit under-dispatched (deliberately backed down, not because it is inherently low-capacity-factor) during a sunny, windy midday peak, temporarily pushing the moving share of RE above its parked share.

★ Quick Facts
  1. Total RE-only installed capacity: 243.49 GW; RE + large hydro: 295.55 GW; non-fossil (RE + large hydro + nuclear): 304.33 GW — all as on 31.08.2026, MNRE Physical Progress.
  2. Solar is the largest single RE source at 168.04 GW — nearly three times wind's 58.52 GW — but this does not make solar India's single largest source of installed capacity overall (coal-based thermal capacity remains larger; see Lesson 2).
  3. Solar's four sub-categories: ground-mounted/utility-scale (123.99 GW, 73.8%), grid-connected rooftop (32.59 GW, 19.4%), off-grid (6.63 GW, 3.9%), hybrid solar component (4.83 GW, 2.9%).
  4. Wind — onshore only, 58.52 GW installed. Offshore wind has a policy framework, potential estimates (~70 GW), and a VGF tender mechanism, but zero commissioned capacity as of this lesson's research.
  5. Small hydro (≤25 MW plants) stands at 5.18 GW — a mature, slow-growing category limited by suitable perennial-flow sites.
  6. Bio-power splits into bagasse cogeneration (9.82 GW) and non-bagasse cogeneration (1.05 GW), totalling 10.87 GW.
  7. Waste-to-energy is the smallest RE-only category: 0.32 GW grid-connected + 0.55 GW off-grid = 0.88 GW.
  8. Green hydrogen is an energy carrier, not a generation source with an installed-capacity figure comparable to solar/wind — its production draws on RE capacity rather than adding its own MW line to the mix. National Green Hydrogen Mission: Cabinet approval 04.01.2023; full scheme detail is Lesson 6.
  9. 500 GW non-fossil-by-2030 is one of the five "Panchamrit" pledges announced at COP26, Glasgow, November 2021.
  10. Progress toward 500 GW: ~266.78 GW (51.93% of installed base) on 31.12.2025 (PIB, PRID 2213238) → 304.33 GW on 31.08.2026 (MNRE) — roughly 61% of the target on the more solidly sourced figure.
  11. The "300 GW crossed" non-fossil-capacity milestone (300.50 GW as on 31.07.2026, over 60% of the 500 GW target) is now firmly confirmed by PIB PRID 2296782, "India Achieves Landmark 300 GW Non-Fossil Fuel Power Capacity" (MNRE, released 09.08.2026) — no longer a provisional claim.
  12. India ranks 3rd globally in installed RE capacity (behind China, USA; ahead of Brazil) — PIB, 08.04.2026, citing IRENA's Renewable Energy Statistics 2026 (India ~250.52 GW; China ~2,258.02 GW; USA ~467.92 GW).
  13. THE core trap: installed-capacity share (~43%, a stock measure) is conceptually distinct from share of total electricity demand actually met by RE (~51.5% on 29.07.2025, a flow measure — the source says "total electricity demand," not "peak demand") — never substitute one for the other. A second, unrelated statistic (non-fossil installed-capacity share, ≈51.5% as on 30.11.2025) coincidentally carries the same headline number for a completely different measure — a trap within the trap.
  14. Non-fossil ≠ renewable: non-fossil additionally includes nuclear (8.78 GW) and large hydro (52.06 GW) — categories that are not part of MNRE's "RE-only" count.
  15. MNRE's own reporting shows solar capacity growing from roughly 150.26 GW to 168.04 GW within months during 2026 — direct evidence of how volatile every figure in this cluster is; re-pull before use.
  16. PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana (rooftop) and PM-KUSUM (agricultural/off-grid solar) are named here only as context for why certain solar sub-categories are growing — their scheme parameters belong to Lesson 6.
  17. Bagasse cogeneration (sugar-mill by-product) is nearly ten times larger than non-bagasse cogeneration within India's bio-power capacity — a useful discriminator if a question asks which biomass sub-category dominates.
★ PYQ · TRAP · CURRENT
TRAP — where an examiner twists it
Looks like thisActually this
"India's installed RE capacity, RE+hydro capacity, and non-fossil capacity are three names for the same figure"Three distinct, nested totals — 243.49 GW (RE only) → 295.55 GW (+ large hydro) → 304.33 GW (+ nuclear); each has a different, precise membership
"Solar is India's single largest source of installed electricity capacity"Solar is the largest renewable source, but coal-based thermal capacity overall still exceeds it — "largest within RE" and "largest overall" are different claims
"India has operational offshore wind farms"India has offshore wind policy (a VGF scheme, potential estimates, ministerial statements) but zero commissioned offshore wind capacity as of this lesson's research — a textbook "policy vs ground reality" gap
"43% of India's installed capacity is renewable, so renewables also supply 43% of the electricity actually used"43% (CEA, 31.07.2026) is an installed-capacity share (a stock measure); the share of actual electricity demand met (e.g., ~51.5% of total demand on 29.07.2025 — not specifically "peak" demand, despite how it is often paraphrased) is a separate flow measure — the two numbers are conceptually different and need not match
"Non-fossil energy and renewable energy mean the same thing"Non-fossil = renewable + large hydro + nuclear; nuclear is not renewable (it consumes a finite fuel) but is also not a fossil fuel — non-fossil is the wider category
"Green hydrogen has its own installed-capacity figure alongside solar and wind in MNRE's tables"Green hydrogen is a manufactured energy carrier, produced using electricity from RE sources — it does not carry a comparable stand-alone "installed generation capacity" line the way solar or wind does
"India already ranks 1st or 2nd in the world for installed RE capacity"India ranks 3rd, behind China and the USA — confirmed via PIB (08.04.2026), citing IRENA's Renewable Energy Statistics 2026, though the underlying figures are internationally sourced and should be re-checked for the latest edition before the exam
"India has already reached its 500 GW non-fossil target"As on 31.08.2026, non-fossil capacity stood at 304.33 GW — roughly 61% of the 500 GW target, with the target date still 2030; a "300 GW crossed" milestone (300.50 GW as on 31.07.2026) is separately confirmed by PIB PRID 2296782 (MNRE, 09.08.2026), itself over 60% of the 500 GW target
"Bagasse and non-bagasse cogeneration are roughly equal contributors to India's bio-power capacity"Bagasse cogeneration (9.82 GW) is nearly ten times larger than non-bagasse cogeneration (1.05 GW) — sugar-mill by-product power dominates the bio-power category
"Renewable and non-conventional energy are simply two different eras of the same single term"MNRE's own naming has drifted over time between "non-conventional energy sources" (an older usage) and "renewable energy" (the current standard usage) — the underlying category is broadly the same, but a question testing terminology history should not be answered as if the two labels were introduced together or mean subtly different things today
Why does this lesson matter, and what does Lesson 6 build on top of it?
Everything in this lesson has been about what exists on the ground and how it is measured — the sources, their installed capacities, the stock-vs-flow distinction, and the honest gaps between policy ambition (offshore wind, the 500 GW pledge) and commissioned reality. Lesson 6 now asks the natural next question: what is government doing to grow these numbers? — the schemes (PM Surya Ghar, PM-KUSUM, the National Green Hydrogen Mission's actual scheme architecture), the regulatory mechanisms (Renewable Purchase Obligation), the enabling infrastructure (Green Energy Corridor), the diplomatic instrument (International Solar Alliance), and the achievements/rankings narrative built on top of the capacity figures this lesson has just carefully defined. Keep this lesson's numbers — and its caution about re-verifying every one of them — firmly in mind as the baseline against which Lesson 6's scheme-by-scheme claims should be checked.
