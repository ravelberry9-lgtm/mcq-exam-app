1. Acid rain

Acid rain refers to precipitation — rain, snow, fog, or even dry deposition — made abnormally acidic by atmospheric pollutants, chiefly sulphur dioxide (SO₂) and oxides of nitrogen (NOx). Both gases are released mainly by burning fossil fuels in power plants, industry, and vehicles. Once in the atmosphere, SO₂ oxidises and combines with water vapour to form sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄), while NOx similarly forms nitric acid (HNO₃); both fall back to Earth dissolved in precipitation, lowering its pH well below the natural rainwater baseline of about 5.6 (itself mildly acidic due to dissolved atmospheric CO₂ forming weak carbonic acid).

The effects are wide-ranging: acidification of soils and freshwater bodies (harming aquatic life, particularly fish, and altering nutrient availability for plants), forest dieback (acid damage to foliage and acid-mobilised aluminium in soil damaging root systems), and corrosion of exposed stone and metal structures — acid rain reacts with the calcium carbonate in marble and limestone, a process popularly called "marble cancer" or "stone leprosy," of particular concern for monuments such as the Taj Mahal.

The Taj Mahal/marble-cancer connection is included here as a widely-cited example, but a specific India-government (ASI, CPCB, or MoEFCC) source directly linking Taj Mahal marble degradation to acid rain — as opposed to general air pollution/industrial emissions from the Mathura refinery and other sources in the Taj Trapezium Zone — has not yet been checked in this research pass and should be verified before this specific example is treated as settled rather than illustrative.

2. Stratospheric ozone and its depletion

Ozone (O₃) exists in two very different contexts in the atmosphere, and confusing them is a classic exam trap. Stratospheric ozone (the "ozone layer," roughly 15–35 km altitude) is beneficial — it absorbs most of the sun's harmful ultraviolet-B (UV-B) radiation, protecting life on Earth's surface. Tropospheric ozone (ground-level, in the air we breathe) is a harmful pollutant and a component of photochemical smog, formed by sunlight acting on NOx and volatile organic compounds — it is not a "good ozone/bad ozone" distinction of the same molecule behaving differently by choice, but a matter of altitude and consequent exposure.

Trap. "Ozone is good" and "ozone is bad" are both correct — for different layers. A question testing this distinction is testing whether the answer specifies stratospheric (protective) versus tropospheric (harmful, smog-forming) ozone, not a blanket statement either way.

Stratospheric ozone depletion is caused chiefly by chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), halons, and related halogenated compounds, which release chlorine and bromine atoms in the stratosphere under UV light; these atoms catalytically destroy ozone molecules, with a single chlorine atom capable of destroying many thousands of ozone molecules before it is removed from the stratosphere. The severe, seasonal thinning over Antarctica is commonly called the "ozone hole." A substance's Ozone Depletion Potential (ODP) is an index of how much damage it can do to the ozone layer relative to an equal mass of CFC-11 (trichlorofluoromethane), which is defined as the reference substance with ODP = 1. Per the Montreal Protocol's own controlled-substances annexes, ODP values vary considerably across the regulated substances: CFC-12 shares CFC-11's ODP of 1.0, but the halons — used in fire suppression — are markedly more ozone-damaging per unit mass: halon-1211 has an ODP of 3.0 and halon-1301 an ODP of 10.0 (the highest ODP among commonly listed substances), while methyl bromide, an agricultural fumigant, has a comparatively lower ODP of 0.6.

Source: UNEP Ozone Secretariat, Montreal Protocol Annex A ("Group II" halons) and Annex E (methyl bromide) controlled-substances tables (ozone.unep.org).

3. The treaty response — Vienna Convention, Montreal Protocol, Kigali Amendment

The international response to ozone depletion is organised in two tiers, a distinction worth holding onto for exam purposes: the Vienna Convention is a framework agreement (setting out cooperation and research obligations without binding control measures of its own), while the Montreal Protocol under it is the operative instrument that actually mandates phasing out specific substances.

The Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer was adopted on 22 March 1985. The Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer followed, adopted on 16 September 1987. Both instruments have since reached universal ratification: as of UNEP's own status page (dated 10 August 2026), both the Vienna Convention and the Montreal Protocol stand at 198 parties each — the Montreal Protocol is often cited as the first (and so far only) UN treaty to achieve universal ratification by every UN member state.

The Montreal Protocol has itself been amended several times to broaden its coverage. The most recent and, for current exam purposes, most significant is the Kigali Amendment, adopted by the Twenty-Eighth Meeting of the Parties, held in Kigali, Rwanda, from 10 to 15 October 2016. Unlike the original Protocol's ozone-depleting substances, the Kigali Amendment targets hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) — refrigerant gases that do not deplete the ozone layer at all (they were, in fact, introduced as ozone-safe replacements for CFCs) but are extremely potent greenhouse gases, with global warming potentials the Government of India's own record states as "ranging from 12 to 14,000" times that of CO₂. This is why the Kigali Amendment is best understood as a climate-mitigation instrument operating through an ozone-protection treaty, not an ozone measure itself.

Trap. HFCs do not deplete the ozone layer — that is precisely why they replaced CFCs after Montreal. The Kigali Amendment regulates them purely for their potency as greenhouse gases. Do not describe HFCs as ozone-depleting substances.

India and the Kigali Amendment

India's Union Cabinet approved ratification of the Kigali Amendment on 18 August 2021; India's ratification was subsequently deposited on 27 September 2021, per UNEP's own ratification table. As an Article 5 (developing-country) Party, India secured a phase-down schedule starting later than developed countries', in four steps: a cumulative reduction of 10% by 2032, 20% by 2037, 30% by 2042, and 85% by 2047, relative to its HFC baseline — the Government of India's own press release states this schedule in exactly these terms. The same release estimated that HFC phase-down would prevent emissions of "up to 105 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent" of greenhouse gases and help "avoid up to 0.5 degree Celsius of global temperature rise by 2100." Domestically, the commitment required developing a national HFC phase-down strategy in consultation with industry (targeted for completion by 2023) and amending the existing Ozone Depleting Substances (Regulation and Control) Rules (targeted for mid-2024) to give the schedule legal effect.

Source: UNEP Ozone Secretariat, Vienna Convention/Montreal Protocol/Kigali Amendment treaty pages and status-of-ratification table (ozone.unep.org); PIB, "Cabinet approves Ratification of Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol..." (18 Aug 2021, pib.gov.in).

4. Ocean acidification

Ocean acidification is the ongoing decrease in seawater pH caused primarily by the ocean absorbing a large share of atmospheric CO₂ — the UN's own SDG indicator methodology document defines it as "the decrease of seawater pH over an extended period, typically of decades or longer, which is caused primarily by the uptake of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere," noting separately that the ocean absorbs "up to 30% of the annual emissions of anthropogenic CO2 to the atmosphere." Because the pH scale is logarithmic, even a small absolute pH change represents a large change in acidity: IPCC AR6 Working Group I's own Chapter 5 (Executive Summary, verified directly against the chapter PDF) states, more precisely than a single global figure, that ocean surface-layer pH has been decreasing at "a very likely rate of decrease in pH in the ocean surface layer of 0.016 to 0.020 per decade in the subtropics and 0.002 to 0.026 per decade in subpolar and polar zones since the 1980s" — a regional breakdown, not one uniform global rate. A separate claim, that "present-day surface pH values are unprecedented for at least 26,000 years," could not be directly located in this pass within IPCC AR6 WGI Chapter 5 or WGII Chapter 3 (it may appear in a paleoclimate subsection not reached during this fetch, or in the IPCC's earlier Special Report on the Ocean and Cryosphere, SROCC, 2019) and is not repeated here pending confirmation.

The commonly cited headline figure — surface ocean pH falling from roughly 8.2 in pre-industrial times to roughly 8.1 today, an increase in acidity of around 26–30% — is well supported by the underlying data, though the exact numbers depend on which time window is used: one frequently cited pairing gives pre-industrial (1850) pH of about 8.17 falling to about 8.08 by 2021, while a separate, more recent-window figure gives average ocean-surface pH falling from about 8.15 in 1950 to about 8.05 by 2020 — a decline described as "an increase of around 26% in hydrogen ion concentration" (each 0.1 drop in pH corresponds to roughly a 26% rise in hydrogen-ion concentration, since the scale is logarithmic). Both framings support the same "roughly 8.2 to 8.1, about a quarter more acidic" figure commonly used in Indian exam material; an answer should treat this as an approximation rather than insist on one single decimal figure.

Ocean acidification is addressed directly under SDG Target 14.3: "Minimize and address the impacts of ocean acidification, including through enhanced scientific cooperation at all levels," tracked via Indicator 14.3.1, "Average marine acidity (pH) measured at agreed suite of representative sampling stations."

Trap. Ocean acidification and ozone depletion are frequently confused by rote-memorisers because both involve "the atmosphere" and "chemical damage" — but they are unrelated mechanisms (CO₂ dissolving in seawater, versus halogen-catalysed ozone destruction in the stratosphere) with unrelated causal gases (CO₂ versus CFCs/halons) and unrelated treaty regimes (UNFCCC/Paris Agreement versus the Vienna Convention/Montreal Protocol).

Ocean acidification, coral bleaching and India's fisheries

India's own ocean-acidification research is run by the Centre for Marine Living Resources and Ecology (CMLRE), an institute under the Ministry of Earth Sciences. Its ocean-acidification research programme states that global open-ocean pH is "declining at the rate of 0.0015 to 0.0024/year" from atmospheric CO₂ uptake alone, and explicitly flags that India's own coastal waters are expected to acidify faster than this open-ocean baseline, because of additional local stressors layered on top of CO₂ uptake — the programme names "agricultural fertilizers, industrial emissions, domestic and industrial sewages and input of acidic waters through natural processes, such as upwelling" as compounding factors along the Indian coast. The programme's stated objectives are to establish the rate of acidification change along India's coast, identify which stretches of coastline are most vulnerable, and assess impacts on biological resources through in-situ mesocosm experiments — i.e., this is an active, ongoing government research mandate rather than a closed, fully-reported study.

A distinct but related data point, more exam-relevant because it is specific and quantified, comes from the Ministry of Earth Sciences' own reply in the Lok Sabha (4 December 2024) on the Fourth Global Coral Bleaching Event, which affected four Indian coral-reef areas during 2023–24, driven by "increase in sea surface temperature: El Niño effect": Lakshadweep saw "widespread bleaching" at 84.6%, making it the worst-affected Indian reef system; the Gulf of Mannar saw "small scale bleaching in some patchy areas" at 27%; and the Andaman & Nicobar Islands saw localised bleaching of 15–18%, confined to South Andaman. The Ministry's reply notes that most Gulf of Mannar and Andaman reefs have since recovered, while Lakshadweep's reefs show only partial recovery and remain under monitoring by the National Centre for Coastal Research (NCCR), the Zoological Survey of India (ZSI), INCOIS, and ISRO's Space Applications Centre. The same reply also records India's largest coral-translocation project, in the Gulf of Kachchh, having moved over 16,000 corals to reduce pressure on stressed reef patches.

Trap. Coral bleaching (as in the Lakshadweep/Gulf of Mannar/Andaman figures above) and ocean acidification are not the same mechanism, and an answer should not cite one data set as evidence for the other. Bleaching, including the 2023–24 event described here, is triggered primarily by thermal stress — abnormally warm sea-surface temperatures expelling the symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae) that give corals their colour and much of their food. Acidification instead works chemically: falling ocean pH lowers the availability of carbonate ions corals need to build their calcium-carbonate skeletons, slowing growth and weakening existing structures, independent of temperature. The two stresses often act together on the same reefs (as CMLRE's own programme notes), but they are examined as distinct processes with distinct causal chains.

Source: CMLRE (Ministry of Earth Sciences), "Ocean Acidification and its potential impacts on biological resources of the Indian coastal..." research-programme page (cmlre.gov.in, page content as last updated 29 July 2021); Ministry of Earth Sciences, Lok Sabha reply on the Fourth Global Coral Bleaching Event (moes.gov.in, 4 December 2024).

Kigali Amendment — India's compliance status, 2026 update

As of World Ozone Day, 16 September 2026 — the Kigali Amendment's tenth anniversary — UNEP records 174 countries as having ratified it, a coverage UNDP's Chemicals and Waste Hub describes as meaning "all HFC-producing countries and major consuming countries are now Parties to the Amendment," representing 100% of global HFC production and 96% of global HFC consumption.

For India specifically, a Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change office memorandum dated 1 April 2026 (F.No. IA-Z-11013/66/2026-IA-II), issued to State-level Environmental Impact Assessment Authorities and Expert Appraisal Committees, gives the clearest recent statement of where India's Kigali clock actually stands: it confirms that India's HFC phase-down obligation "begins from 1 January 2028" — i.e., 2028 is India's freeze/baseline year, before which India is not yet required to start cutting HFC consumption — and, to prevent a pre-freeze capacity rush that would inflate that baseline, directs that no Environmental Clearance be granted for new or additional HFC production capacity unless production actually starts "on or before 31st December, 2027." Any HFC production that does go ahead remains "bound by the limits...prescribed under the ODS Rules, 2000 as amended from time to time." Read together with the previously-established four-step reduction schedule (10% by 2032, 20% by 2037, 30% by 2042, 85% by 2047 — see above), the practical picture is: India has ratified, has fixed its 2028 starting line, and is now using clearance-stage regulation to stop industry from padding its baseline ahead of that date, rather than being mid-way through an active phase-down yet.

Trap. "India has ratified the Kigali Amendment" (true, since 2021) is not the same as "India has started reducing HFCs" (not true until the 2028 freeze year, per the 1 April 2026 MoEFCC memorandum). A question testing the phase-down schedule is testing whether the answer distinguishes ratification/legal commitment from the actual start of the reduction obligation.

Source: MoEFCC office memorandum, F.No. IA-Z-11013/66/2026-IA-II, dated 1 April 2026 (hosted at parivesh.nic.in); UNEP, "World Ozone Day, 16 September 2026" (unep.org/ozonaction).

The Montreal Protocol's climate co-benefit

Although the Montreal Protocol was designed to protect the ozone layer, not the climate, its side-effect on global warming has turned out to be large enough that it is now routinely described as a climate treaty in its own right. NOAA's coverage of the 2022 WMO/UNEP Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion states that the Protocol's phase-out of CFCs and other ozone-depleting substances has already avoided "a potential additional global temperature increase of 0.5 to 1 degree Fahrenheit by 2100," and that the Kigali Amendment's HFC controls are projected to "avoid another 0.5 to 1 degree Fahrenheit of additional warming by 2100" on top of that. The same assessment reports that stratospheric chlorine has fallen 11.5% from its 1993 peak and stratospheric bromine 14.5% from its 1999 peak — the underlying chemistry behind the ozone-recovery trend covered below.

UNEP's own Ozone Secretariat states the combined effect in Celsius terms and adds a longer-range HFC-specific figure: ODS phase-out under the Protocol is estimated to avoid roughly 0.5–1 °C of warming by mid-century relative to an uncontrolled-emissions scenario, ozone-layer protection is estimated to have prevented around 135 billion tonnes of CO₂-equivalent emissions between 1990 and 2010, and full compliance with the Kigali Amendment's HFC controls could avoid up to 420 gigatonnes of CO₂-equivalent emissions by 2100 (described as "equivalent to over 10 years of current human-caused CO₂ emissions"). UNEP's 2026 World Ozone Day material restates the near-term Kigali figure as up to 0.5 °C of avoided warming by century's end on its own, "and double that figure when paired with energy-efficient cooling technologies."

Trap. Do not present a single "the Montreal Protocol avoided X °C of warming" figure as if it were one settled number — different UNEP/NOAA materials report it in different units (Fahrenheit vs Celsius) and split it differently between the original Protocol's ODS phase-out and the Kigali Amendment's HFC controls on top of it. For exam purposes, the safe, defensible statement is the qualitative one: the Montreal Protocol and its Kigali Amendment are jointly estimated to avoid roughly half a degree to a full degree Celsius of additional warming by 2100, making the Protocol arguably the most effective climate treaty in force even though it was never negotiated as one.

Source: NOAA, "Montreal Protocol emerges as powerful climate treaty" (noaa.gov, 11 Jan 2023), citing the 2022 WMO/UNEP Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion; UNEP Ozone Secretariat, "Facts and figures on ozone protection" (ozone.unep.org); UNEP, "World Ozone Day, 16 September 2026" (unep.org/ozonaction).

The 2025 Antarctic ozone hole and the current recovery outlook

The most recent full Antarctic ozone season on record, 2025, is reported by two different primary sources using two different metrics, and an answer should keep the two straight rather than quoting one number as if it were the other. Measuring by area, a WMO news release (drawing on NOAA, NASA, and the EU's Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service) reports the 2025 ozone hole's maximum extent as 18.71 million square kilometres, the fifth-smallest since 1992 and about 30% smaller than the largest hole on record (2006); the hole also closed unusually early, by 1 December 2025 — its earliest closure since 2019, nearly three weeks ahead of the past decade's average. Measuring by ozone mass deficit instead, the WMO's own Ozone and UV Bulletin No. 4 (16 September 2026) gives the 2025 hole's peak ozone mass deficit as 36.7 million tonnes (on 29 September 2025) against a 1990–2010 historical-mean peak of 50.1 million tonnes — over 25% below that baseline, and the bulletin ranks 2025 as the "fourth to fifth weakest" ozone hole since severe depletion began in 1992. Both figures point the same way — a small, short-lived, recovering hole — they are simply not interchangeable numbers.

On the longer recovery trajectory, the most recent long-range projection located in this pass is still the 2022 WMO/UNEP Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion (as reported by NOAA): full ozone-layer recovery to 1980 benchmark values is projected for around 2066 over Antarctica, 2045 over the Arctic, and 2040 for the global mid-latitudes — the Antarctic figure lags the others because the ozone hole is a more extreme, localised depletion phenomenon than the more modest global thinning. NASA scientist Paul Newman is quoted making the counterfactual concrete: "this year's [2025] hole would have been more than one million square miles larger if there was still as much chlorine in the stratosphere as there was 25 years ago."

The WMO's September 2026 Ozone and UV Bulletin No. 4 was fetched directly for the 2025 ozone-mass-deficit figures above, but it did not itself restate updated Antarctic/Arctic/global recovery-year projections; those recovery years above are still sourced to the 2022 Scientific Assessment via NOAA's reporting, not a fresher 2025/2026 assessment, since the next full WMO/UNEP Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion (expected on its regular quadrennial cycle) was not locatable as a completed document during this research pass.

India's SO₂/NOx emission standards for thermal power plants

The clearest India-specific, central-government-sourced regulatory response to acid-rain-forming emissions is not a piece of acid-rain legislation as such, but the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change's revised emission standards for coal-based thermal power plants, notified on 7 December 2015 under the Environment (Protection) Rules, 1986. These standards tightened permissible SO₂, NOx, and particulate-matter emissions in three vintage-based tiers: plants installed before 31 December 2003 were held to particulate matter of 100 mg/Nm³, SO₂ of 600 mg/Nm³ (units below 500 MW) or 200 mg/Nm³ (500 MW and above), and NOx of 600 mg/Nm³; plants installed between 1 January 2003 and 31 December 2016 faced a tighter particulate limit of 50 mg/Nm³ and NOx cut to 300 mg/Nm³ (SO₂ unchanged); and plants installed from 1 January 2017 onward faced the strictest tier — particulate matter of 30 mg/Nm³, SO₂ of 100 mg/Nm³, and NOx of 100 mg/Nm³ — with existing (pre-2017) plants originally given two years from notification to comply.

That original two-year compliance window proved unworkable at fleet scale (installing flue-gas desulphurisation, or FGD, equipment across hundreds of generating units takes years), so MoEFCC subsequently staggered compliance by location. Per PIB's reporting of the ministry's notification of 5 September 2022, plants were sorted into three categories with separate SO₂-compliance deadlines: Category A (National Capital Region and million-plus-population cities) by 31 December 2024; Category B (critically polluted or non-attainment areas) by 31 December 2025; and Category C (all other locations) by 31 December 2026, with a graduated environmental compensation for non-compliance — ₹0.20, ₹0.30, and ₹0.40 per unit of electricity generated for delays of 0–180, 181–365, and 366-plus days respectively. As of a PIB update dated 27 July 2023, only 22 generating units, totalling 9,280 MW, across seven states, had actually installed and operationalised FGD systems — a useful, government-sourced indicator of how far implementation still lagged the original schedule.

These are verified emission-standard and compliance-deadline figures — a genuine India-specific, central-government-sourced regulatory response to SO₂/NOx, the acid-rain precursor gases discussed in §1 — but they are standards for what plants are permitted to emit, not ambient monitoring data showing acid-rain deposition or soil/water pH impact within India. A direct CPCB or MoEFCC data source for India-specific acid-rain deposition effects (as opposed to emission-standard compliance) remains the one item still not located across three research passes on this chapter; the Taj Mahal/marble-degradation caveat noted near §1 above stands unchanged by this addition.

(pib.gov.in, PRID 1943363, reporting the MoEF&CC notification of 5 September 2022 and a compliance update dated 27 July 2023).

5. The Taj Mahal and the Agra Trapezium Zone — the classic acid-rain case study

Section 1 flagged the Taj Mahal/marble-cancer link as illustrative pending a direct India-government source. This pass fetched the actual Supreme Court judgment text directly, which closes that gap: the case is M.C. Mehta v. Union of India & Ors. (the "Taj Trapezium Case"), decided 30 December 1996. The original writ petition alleged specific, visible deterioration of the marble — "the white marble has yellowed and blackened in places," with a "yellow pallor" and "ugly brown and black spots," worst in the inner chamber housing the original graves — attributed to foundries, chemical industries, the Mathura Refinery, brick-kilns, vehicular traffic, and diesel generator-sets operating in and around Agra. The Court itself stated the acid-rain chemistry in its judgment: "Sulphur-dioxide emitted by the Mathura Refinery and the industries when combined with Oxygen — with the aid of moisture — in the atmosphere forms sulphuric acid," which attacks the marble's calcium carbonate in two ways — by forming a crust that later exfoliates, and by gradual dissolution as rainfall washes away the reaction products.

The Court's directive was precise and dated: the 292 industries identified as coal/coke users in Agra were ordered either to switch to natural gas — applying for a connection by 15 February 1997, with GAIL required to decide on applications by 31 March 1997 and gas supply to commence by 30 June 1997 — or to relocate outside the zone by 31 December 1997 or shut down; any industry doing neither had to stop using coal or coke by 30 April 1997. This judgment is what legally created the Taj Trapezium Zone (TTZ): an area of roughly 10,400 square kilometres spanning Agra, Firozabad, Mathura and Hathras in Uttar Pradesh and Bharatpur in Rajasthan, protecting three UNESCO World Heritage Sites — the Taj Mahal, Agra Fort, and Fatehpur Sikri — along with over 40 other monuments of national importance. A dedicated Taj Trapezium Zone Pollution (Prevention and Control) Authority, set up in May 1998, zones industries by category (Orange/Red/White/Green), bans coal and coke use and new tree-felling/mining inside the TTZ, and has pushed battery-run and CNG vehicles in the immediate monument area.

Trap. The Taj Trapezium case is routinely tested as an acid-rain example, but the mechanism worth stating precisely is SO₂ (not NOx, and not particulate soot alone) reacting with atmospheric moisture to form sulphuric acid, which then attacks marble's calcium carbonate — the same general acid-rain chemistry introduced in §1, here with a named, dated Supreme Court source rather than a generic textbook claim.
The TTZ regulatory regime remains actively litigated in 2026, but current cases are about a different stressor than the original SO₂/marble issue: an NGT bench (Justice Prakash Shrivastava and Expert Member Dr A. Senthil Vel) issued notices on or around 5 January 2026 in Original Application No. 665/2025 over unauthorised construction, encroachment and tree-felling inside the Taj's heritage buffer — including work by the Agra Development Authority in Shahjahan Park and tree removal during metro construction — invoking a separate 1 May 2025 Supreme Court order requiring apex-court approval for any tree-felling within a 5 km aerial radius of the Taj Mahal. 6. India's ozone-depleting-substance phase-out history

India became a party to the Montreal Protocol in June 1992, as an Article 5 (developing-country) Party, and has marked World Ozone Day (16 September) every year since 1995. Per MoEFCC's own account, India "phased out Chlorofluorocarbons, Carbon tetrachloride, Halons, Methyl Bromide and Methyl Chloroform for controlled uses as on 1 January 2010" — i.e., the first-generation ozone-depleting substances covered by the original Montreal Protocol were eliminated on schedule over a decade ago, with one narrow, internationally-recognised exception: pharmaceutical-grade CFCs used in metered-dose inhalers (MDIs) for asthma and COPD patients, which continue under the Protocol's essential-use exemption pending viable alternatives.

The substances still being phased down are HCFCs — transitional refrigerants that are far less ozone-damaging than CFCs (see the ODP table in §2) but not ozone-safe, so the Protocol schedules their own elimination in stages. India's HCFC Phase-out Management Plan (HPMP) runs in three stages: Stage I (2012–2016), completed; Stage II (2017–2024), under which India achieved a 44% consumption reduction by 2020 against a 35% target, and under which India became "the first among the developing countries" to fully close out HCFC-141b — the specific HCFC used in rigid-foam manufacturing — with import licences for HCFC-141b prohibited and its use by the foam industry closed from 1 January 2020; and Stage III (2023–2030), which requires phasing out manufacture of new HCFC-using equipment by 31 December 2024 and complete phase-out of HCFCs for all controlled applications by 1 January 2030.

Trap. Three separate substance classes, three separate schedules — do not collapse them into one "ozone phase-out" date. CFCs/halons/carbon tetrachloride (first-generation ODS, Montreal Protocol core): phased out for controlled uses in India by 1 January 2010. HCFCs (transitional, still ozone-depleting but with low ODP): being phased down in stages through a target completion of 1 January 2030. HFCs (Kigali Amendment target, zero ODP but high-GWP greenhouse gases — see §3): not an ozone substance at all, on a much longer phase-down schedule running to 2047.

Source: PIB, Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, World Ozone Day press release (pib.gov.in, PRID 1957968); PIB, "India achieves complete phase out of one the most potent ozone depleting chemical..." (pib.gov.in, PRID 1600233, on the HCFC-141b closure).

7. Blue carbon ecosystems — mangroves, seagrass and India's MISHTI scheme

"Blue carbon" refers to carbon captured and stored by coastal and marine ecosystems — chiefly mangroves, seagrass meadows, and salt marshes — which sequester carbon not only in living biomass but, more importantly, in waterlogged, low-oxygen sediment where it can remain locked away for centuries, making their per-hectare sequestration rates far higher than an equivalent area of terrestrial forest. Blue carbon habitats line most of India's coast — Gujarat, Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, West Bengal, Puducherry, and the Andaman & Nicobar and Lakshadweep islands — and, distinct from their carbon role, mangroves in particular act as a physical buffer against storm surge and cyclone wind stress for the coastline behind them.

India's principal current scheme for this ecosystem is MISHTI — Mangrove Initiative for Shoreline Habitats & Tangible Incomes — announced in the Union Budget 2023-24 and formally launched by MoEFCC on 5 June 2023 (World Environment Day). As originally notified, MISHTI targets restoration/afforestation of about 540 square kilometres of mangroves over five years from FY 2023-24, across 11 States and 2 Union Territories, implemented not through a standalone MISHTI budget line but through convergence of existing schemes — principally State/National CAMPA (Compensatory Afforestation Fund) money, MGNREGS labour-days, and State Plan funds — alongside livelihood diversification and eco-tourism activity built around Self-Help Groups. A subsequent Lok Sabha reply from MoEFCC gives the clearest implementation snapshot located in this pass: as of 21 August 2025, "an area of 22,560.34 hectares has been taken up through convergence across 13 States/UTs for plantation and restoration of degraded mangroves," with Gujarat by far the largest contributor (19,220 ha), followed by Tamil Nadu (1,060 ha) and Andhra Pradesh (837.76 ha) — roughly 225.6 sq km against the five-year, 540 sq km target, i.e. progress running at somewhat under half the target area a little past the programme's two-year mark.

The per-hectare carbon-sequestration figures often quoted alongside blue carbon — such as a Kerala-based study's estimate of roughly 287 tonnes of carbon per hectare per year for undisturbed mangroves, and separate estimates that salt marshes, while sequestering a much smaller absolute 2 tonnes of carbon per hectare per year, still outperform terrestrial forests 43–55-fold per unit area once long-term sediment burial is counted — come from academic studies relayed via a science-media explainer (India Mongabay) in this pass, not from a primary government or IPCC document fetched directly, and should be treated as illustrative rather than exam-certain figures. Likewise, no dedicated central scheme comparable to MISHTI — i.e., one specifically targeting seagrass meadow or salt-marsh restoration rather than mangroves — was located in this research pass; MISHTI itself is a mangrove-specific programme.
Trap. MISHTI is a mangrove-restoration convergence programme, not a general "blue carbon scheme" — an answer should not describe it as covering seagrass or salt-marsh restoration, which (per this pass's research) lack an equivalent dedicated central scheme in India.

8. India's refrigerant-transition industry — natural refrigerants, HFOs, and a "Make in India" push

The Kigali Amendment's HFC phase-down (§3) is a demand signal the cooling industry has to answer with an actual substitute gas, and two competing/complementary families are in play. Natural refrigerants — propane (R-290), isobutane (R-600a), CO₂ (R-744), and ammonia (R-717) — occur naturally, carry very low or zero global warming potential, but are flammable or toxic and require redesigned, safety-adapted equipment before they can be used at scale. Hydrofluoroolefins (HFOs) — next-generation synthetic refrigerants such as HFO-1234yf — are manufactured fluorochemicals engineered to break down in the atmosphere within days rather than years, giving them a near-zero global warming potential and zero ozone-depletion potential despite still being "man-made" gases in the same broad family as the HFCs and HCFCs they are meant to replace.

India's own manufacturing-side response sits inside the India Cooling Action Plan (ICAP), released by MoEFCC on 14 March 2022, which sets an integrated vision — cutting cooling-related energy demand, transitioning refrigerants, and improving efficiency — running to 2037-38, and explicitly records that "the Department of Science and Technology, Government of India has funded a research project to the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR)-Indian Institute of Chemical Technology, Hyderabad" to develop indigenous, low-GWP refrigerant technology. That funded project produced a concrete result in July 2026: CSIR-IICT announced it had developed India's first bench-scale process technology for manufacturing HFO-1234yf domestically, which Director D. Srinivasa Reddy called "an important landmark towards achieving self-reliance," explicitly tying it to "Viksit Bharat 2047, Make in India, and Atmanirbhar Bharat." The technology is now being prepared for transfer to industry partners for commercial-scale production; consumer-market availability is projected within two to three years pending that industrial scale-up, with cost currently comparable to imported HFO-1234yf and expected to fall as domestic production scales.

The "industry" side of this transition is not only chemistry but workforce: ICAP records 43,450 refrigeration-and-air-conditioning service technicians trained under the National Skill Qualification Framework, plus a further 29,000 being trained specifically under the HCFC Phase-out Management Plans referenced in §6 — the technician base needed to service equipment as the refrigerant itself changes underneath it.

Trap. "Natural refrigerant" and "green/low-GWP refrigerant" are not synonyms — propane and ammonia are natural refrigerants; HFO-1234yf, the subject of CSIR-IICT's 2026 breakthrough, is not a natural refrigerant at all but a synthetic HFO. Both routes lower GWP relative to HFCs, but an answer naming CSIR-IICT's work should not call it a "natural refrigerant" development.
This pass located a well-documented, recent (2026), primary-sourced example of India's domestic refrigerant-manufacturing transition in the HFO-1234yf/CSIR-IICT case, but did not find a comparably specific central-government dataset on natural-refrigerant (propane/CO₂/ammonia) manufacturing capacity or scale-up in India — that side of the "refrigerant transition industry" is referenced qualitatively in policy documents (ICAP) but without figures as concrete as the HFO case above.

Montreal Protocol amendment cities, in order (London → Copenhagen → Montreal → Beijing → Kigali): "Lucky Cats Meet Big Kangaroos." The three substance-class end-dates (§6, §9.2): "Ten, Thirty, Forty-Seven — each newer gas buys 20 more years." CFCs/halons/carbon tetrachloride were out by 2010; HCFCs, the transitional substitute, get until 2030; HFCs, which are not even ozone-depleting, get until 2047 — the phase-out horizon stretches as each successive gas family is less immediately damaging to the ozone layer itself (even as, in the HFC case, it becomes more damaging to the climate instead).

India's Kigali four-step HFC cut (§3, §9.2): "10–20–30, then the big jump to 85." The first three steps rise by a steady 10 percentage points every five years (2032/2037/2042), then the schedule leaps straight to 85% by 2047 — the uneven final jump, not a smooth continuation to "40%," is exactly the kind of number a distractor option will get wrong.

10.1 Why the hole forms specifically over Antarctica, not just "where CFCs are" (UPSC Prelims 2011)

UPSC Civil Services Prelims, 2011 — official previous paper. "The formation of ozone hole in the Antarctic region has been a cause of concern. What could be the reason for the formation of this hole? (a) Presence of prominent tropospheric turbulence; and inflow of chlorofluorocarbons. (b) Presence of prominent polar front and stratospheric clouds; and inflow of chlorofluorocarbons. (c) Absence of polar front and stratospheric clouds; and inflow of methane and chlorofluorocarbons. (d) Increased temperature at polar region due to global warming." Correct answer: (b).

§2 explains that chlorine and bromine atoms catalytically destroy stratospheric ozone, but not why that destruction concentrates into a severe, near-total seasonal "hole" specifically over Antarctica rather than being spread evenly wherever CFCs have mixed into the stratosphere (which is, in fact, globally). The mechanism, per NOAA's 2022 Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion ("Twenty Questions" series, Q9), has three parts working together. First, the polar vortex — a band of very strong, cold westerly winds that forms in the winter stratosphere — isolates the air over Antarctica from the rest of the atmosphere for months; the Southern Hemisphere's vortex is markedly stronger and more stable than the Arctic's because the Northern Hemisphere's greater land/ocean/mountain contrast disturbs and warms its vortex, which is why the Arctic never develops ozone loss as severe as Antarctica's. Second, within that isolated, extremely cold air (average daily minimum stratospheric temperatures as low as −92 °C over Antarctica in July–August), polar stratospheric clouds (PSCs) form — ice and nitric-acid particles that exist only below about −78 °C. Reactions on the surfaces of these PSC particles convert chlorine from inert, harmless "reservoir" forms (hydrogen chloride and chlorine nitrate) into reactive chlorine monoxide (ClO), pushing the reactive-chlorine fraction from a small share of total stratospheric chlorine to more than half. Third, this reactive chlorine sits dormant through the dark polar winter — the catalytic ozone-destruction cycles need sunlight to run — and is only triggered into action when sunlight returns with the Antarctic spring (September–October), which is precisely why the hole opens up in spring rather than mid-winter despite winter being colder.

Trap. Option (d) of the 2011 question above — "increased temperature... due to global warming" — is the bait, and it inverts the actual mechanism: the Antarctic ozone hole forms because the stratosphere there is unusually cold (cold enough for PSCs to form), not because it is warming. Confusing stratospheric cooling (which favours ozone depletion) with tropospheric global warming (a separate phenomenon, and if anything one that cools the stratosphere further as greenhouse gases trap heat lower down) is a standing exam trap distinct from the good-ozone/bad-ozone altitude trap already flagged in §2.

10.2 Coral reefs beyond bleaching — where they are, and why they matter for biodiversity (UPSC Prelims 2018)

UPSC Civil Services Prelims, 2018 — official previous paper. "Consider the following statements: 1. Most of the world's coral reefs are in tropical waters. 2. More than one-third of the world's coral reefs are located in the territories of Australia, Indonesia and Philippines. 3. Coral reefs host far more number of animal phyla than those hosted by tropical rainforests. Which of the statements given above is/are correct? (a) 1 and 2 only (b) 3 only (c) 1 and 3 only (d) 1, 2 and 3." Correct answer: (d), all three.

§4's coral content (coral bleaching, acidification's effect on calcification, the 2023–24 bleaching event) assumes the reader already knows basic coral-reef distribution and biodiversity facts that this 2018 question tests directly and that the chapter has not stated anywhere. On statement 1: reef-building corals need warm, shallow, clear, sunlit water, which confines them almost entirely to tropical and subtropical latitudes (roughly between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn, plus a narrow fringe either side where warm currents extend the range, such as off southern Japan or South Africa). On statement 3: coral reef ecosystems are consistently described, including by NOAA's own ocean-literacy material, as hosting the highest biodiversity of any marine ecosystem and are often said to outscore tropical rainforests specifically on phylum-level diversity (reefs contain representatives of the great majority of known animal phyla, against the much narrower set found on land), even though reefs cover under 1% of the ocean floor and rainforests host more total species. 10.3 The Gothenburg Protocol — acid rain's actual binding treaty, and why it is not a Montreal-style global instrument (UPSC Mains 2022)

§9.1 already logged UPSC Civil Services Mains, 2022, GS Paper III — "Discuss in detail the photochemical smog emphasizing its formation, effects and mitigation. Explain the 1999 Gothenburg Protocol" — and flagged its second half as a named gap. §1 and §3 of this chapter describe acid rain's chemistry and the ozone treaty regime respectively, but neither covers the one treaty that actually targets SO₂/NOx/acid-rain-and-smog-forming emissions with binding national ceilings, which is what this Mains question is actually asking for.

The Gothenburg Protocol — full name, the Protocol to Abate Acidification, Eutrophication and Ground-level Ozone — was adopted on 30 November 1999 in Gothenburg, Sweden, as a protocol under the 1979 Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution (CLRTAP/LRTAP), itself administered by the UN Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE). It entered into force internationally on 17 May 2005. Unlike the single-pollutant framing common in this chapter's other material, Gothenburg is explicitly a multi-pollutant, multi-effect protocol: it sets legally binding national emission ceilings covering four pollutants together — sulphur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen oxides (NOx), volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and ammonia (NH₃) — because these same precursor gases drive three distinct environmental problems at once: soil/water acidification (§1's subject), eutrophication of water bodies from excess nitrogen, and ground-level/tropospheric ozone formation, i.e. photochemical smog (§2's "bad ozone," and the first half of the same 2022 Mains question). This multi-pollutant, multi-effect design is exactly why the Mains question pairs photochemical smog and the Gothenburg Protocol in one question rather than treating them as unrelated topics — the protocol is the regulatory response to the same precursor chemistry that produces both acid rain and smog. The protocol was substantially amended in 2012 (adding fine particulate matter, PM2.5, including a black-carbon component, as a fifth regulated pollutant, and setting new national emission-reduction commitments for 2020 onward measured against 2005 levels); the amended protocol entered into force on 7 October 2019.

Trap. The Gothenburg Protocol is a UNECE regional instrument, not a global one like the Montreal Protocol (§3) — its parties are overwhelmingly European and North American states (plus Canada and the United States, both UNECE members for this purpose), and India, which is not a UNECE member state, is not a party to CLRTAP or the Gothenburg Protocol. An answer should not describe it as a global acid-rain treaty analogous to Montreal's universal ozone coverage, and should not cite it as part of India's own binding international obligations the way the Kigali Amendment (§3) genuinely is.
India's non-party status to CLRTAP/the Gothenburg Protocol is stated here on the basis of the protocol's institutional structure (a UNECE-administered convention whose membership is drawn from UNECE's own 56 member states, which does not include India) rather than a located, explicit "India is not a party" statement from UNECE itself; no UNECE party list was directly fetched in this pass to confirm India's absence by name rather than by inference from institutional scope.

APPSC Mains answers score well when a candidate explicitly connects a topic to other parts of the same syllabus rather than treating each chapter as a sealed box. The two sections below trace concrete, mechanistic connections from this chapter's own chemistry and treaty material to two sibling chapters in this book's Environment-and-Health coverage — Chapter 5 (Disease Burden and Epidemic Preparedness) and Chapter 4 (Sustainable Development) — naming the specific facts and figures already established in each so that a reader could verify every link against the source chapter directly.

11.1 From stratospheric chemistry to India's disease burden (Chapter 5)

§2 of this chapter establishes that stratospheric ozone absorbs most of the sun's UV-B radiation before it reaches the surface, and that depletion thins this shield. The direct human-health channel this opens is skin cancer and cataracts, and the dose-response relationship is quantified precisely by WHO: "a 10 per cent decrease in ozone levels will result in an additional 300,000 non-melanoma and 4,500 melanoma skin cancer cases" worldwide per year. The US EPA's own Atmospheric and Health Effects Framework modelling gives the counterfactual scale at national level for the one country it has modelled in detail: full implementation of the Montreal Protocol (this chapter's §3) is projected to prevent approximately 443 million cases of skin cancer, 2.3 million skin cancer deaths, and 63 million cases of cataracts among Americans born 1890–2100 — a figure that illustrates the order of magnitude of the disease burden the treaty regime headed off, even though it is a US estimate rather than an India-specific one.

Chapter 5's own disease-burden architecture is where this channel actually lands administratively. §2 of that chapter describes the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases and Stroke (NPCDCS) as the national vehicle screening for cancer (among other NCDs) in the population aged 30 and above through Health & Wellness Centres; skin cancer sits inside that same "cancer" caseload NPCDCS is built to catch early, even though Chapter 5 does not itself break the figure out by cancer site. Read against this chapter's own ozone-recovery material — stratospheric chlorine down 11.5% from its 1993 peak and bromine down 14.5% from its 1999 peak, the 2025 Antarctic ozone hole ranking as the "fourth to fifth weakest" since 1992, and full recovery to 1980 benchmark values projected for around 2040 at global mid-latitudes (all per the material under "The Montreal Protocol's climate co-benefit" and "The 2025 Antarctic ozone hole," above) — the Montreal Protocol's compliance record is also, mechanistically, a containment story for exactly the UV-B-driven share of the cancer caseload Chapter 5's NPCDCS screens for: a shield that is thinning less each year, rather than thinning further, as the ozone layer continues its documented recovery.

The second channel runs through ocean acidification rather than ozone. §4 of this chapter documents that India's coastal waters are expected to acidify faster than the open-ocean baseline (per CMLRE's own research programme), and separately records the Fourth Global Coral Bleaching Event's toll on Indian reefs — 84.6% bleaching at Lakshadweep, the worst-affected system, with only partial recovery as of the Ministry of Earth Sciences' December 2024 Lok Sabha reply. Degraded reef and nearshore ecosystems bear directly on the roughly 30 million people in India whose livelihoods the fisheries sector sustains, "particularly...marginalised and vulnerable communities," per the Department of Fisheries' own Year-End Review 2023 (PIB); in at least one coastal state, the dependence is dietary as much as occupational — a separate PIB release on India's coastal development records that fish "is considered a staple diet for more than 90% of the population" in Goa, at an annual average per-capita consumption of 15–17 kg. Chapter 5's own §16 names India's "double burden of malnutrition" — child/adolescent undernutrition persisting alongside rising adult overweight and obesity, with NFHS-6 (2023-24) still recording 29.3% child stunting even after its "decline substantially from 35.5%." A fishery under compounding thermal and acidification stress is a plausible pressure on the protein/micronutrient side of that same double burden specifically for coastal and fishing communities, even where the national NFHS trend line is improving.

The fisheries-to-nutrition link in the paragraph above is stated as a mechanistic pathway, built from separately sourced facts (this chapter's own acidification/coral-bleaching data, the Department of Fisheries' livelihood figure, and Chapter 5's NFHS-6 malnutrition figures), not as a single study's measured finding. This research pass could not locate a primary Indian source that directly quantifies ocean acidification's effect on coastal-community protein intake, anaemia, or child-nutrition outcomes specifically (as opposed to fisheries yield or reef health in isolation) — readers should present this as a reasoned causal chain, not as an established epidemiological result, unless a future pass locates such a study.

11.2 Climate governance architecture — SDGs, planetary boundaries and the Montreal Protocol's success (Chapter 4)

Chapter 4's §5 introduces the planetary boundaries framework (proposed 2009 by 28 scientists led by Johan Rockström), which names both ocean acidification and stratospheric ozone depletion among its nine Earth-system processes, and states that, per the Stockholm Resilience Centre's most recent (2025) update, "seven of the nine boundaries have been transgressed — all except atmospheric aerosol loading and stratospheric ozone depletion — with ocean acidification confirmed as the seventh crossed boundary in 2025." This chapter's own §4 supplies the chemistry behind that crossing in far more depth than Chapter 4 itself states it: IPCC AR6 WGI Chapter 5's region-specific pH-decline rate (0.016–0.020 per decade in the subtropics, 0.002–0.026 per decade in subpolar/polar zones since the 1980s), the roughly 8.2-to-8.1 pH decline since pre-industrial times, and CMLRE's finding that India's own coastal waters are acidifying faster than the open-ocean baseline — together, the measured basis for exactly the boundary-crossing Chapter 4 reports as a bare fact.

The companion half of that same Chapter 4 sentence is at least as informative for this chapter's own subject: stratospheric ozone depletion is one of only two planetary boundaries not yet transgressed. This chapter's §3 and its "Montreal Protocol's climate co-benefit" and "2025 Antarctic ozone hole" subsections are the direct explanation for why that boundary has held — the Montreal Protocol and Vienna Convention's universal ratification (198 parties each, the only UN treaties to achieve this), the documented fall in stratospheric chlorine (-11.5% from its 1993 peak) and bromine (-14.5% from its 1999 peak), and the 2025 ozone hole's ranking as the "fourth to fifth weakest" since severe depletion began in 1992. Set beside Chapter 4's planetary-boundaries table, the contrast is exact and exam-usable: ocean acidification has no equivalent binding global treaty (only SDG 14.3's "enhanced scientific cooperation" language, §4 above) and has been transgressed; stratospheric ozone depletion has the Montreal Protocol's binding, universally-ratified phase-out schedules behind it and has not — making the Montreal Protocol, in Chapter 4's own terms, the reason one specific planetary boundary remains inside its safe operating space.

Chapter 4's §3 lists SDG 14 ("Conserve and sustainably use the oceans, seas and marine resources for sustainable development") among the seven SDGs it identifies as most relevant to this Environment-and-Health sub-topic, but states it only at the parent-goal level. This chapter's own §4 is where that goal becomes a specific, measurable commitment: SDG Target 14.3, "Minimize and address the impacts of ocean acidification, including through enhanced scientific cooperation at all levels," tracked via Indicator 14.3.1, "Average marine acidity (pH) measured at agreed suite of representative sampling stations." An answer drawing on Chapter 4's SDG table for Goal 14 is strengthened by citing this chapter's 14.3/14.3.1 detail rather than stopping at the goal's title.

Chapter 4's §8 reports that SDG 13 (Climate Action) is one of the fastest-moving goals in the SDG India Index 2023-24, its "overall composite score" rising "13 points...from 54 (Performer category) in SDG India Index 3 (2020-21) to 67 (Front Runner)" in 2023-24, alongside a rise in renewable electricity's generation share from 36.37% (2020) to 43.28% (2024). The Kigali Amendment material in this chapter's §3 and "Montreal Protocol's climate co-benefit" subsection is a direct, named contributor to the kind of national climate-mitigation trajectory SDG 13 tracks: India's ratified, four-step HFC phase-down (10% by 2032 to 85% by 2047) sits inside a global Kigali effort UNEP estimates could avoid up to 420 gigatonnes of CO₂-equivalent emissions by 2100, and the Montreal Protocol's ODS phase-out overall is already estimated to have avoided roughly 135 billion tonnes of CO₂-equivalent emissions between 1990 and 2010 — a climate-mitigation contribution running alongside, not instead of, the renewable-energy expansion Chapter 4's SDG 13 score explicitly credits.
