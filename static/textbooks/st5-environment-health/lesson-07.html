1. Ayushman Bharat — the umbrella scheme and PM-JAY

Ayushman Bharat was launched in 2018 as the umbrella national health protection initiative. The National Health Authority's own framing does not use the popular shorthand "two pillars" — it describes the scheme as built on "two inter-related components" (also phrased as a "two-pronged approach"): (1) Health and Wellness Centres (HWCs) — renamed Ayushman Arogya Mandir in November 2023 (covered in Chapter 6) — delivering comprehensive primary care including preventive, promotive, and curative services close to where people live; and (2) the Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM-JAY), a health assurance scheme providing hospitalisation cover.

Trap. Exam framings sometimes call Ayushman Bharat's two parts its "two pillars." NHA's own official language is "two inter-related components" / a "two-pronged approach," not "pillars." If a question quotes official phrasing, prefer "components."

PM-JAY provides a health cover of ₹5 lakh per family per year for secondary and tertiary care hospitalisation, cashless and paperless, at the point of service. It targets the bottom 40% of India's population by socio-economic deprivation criteria drawn from the Socio-Economic Caste Census (SECC) — India's largest government-funded health assurance scheme by this deprivation-based, rather than income-declared, targeting method.

Source: National Health Authority, PM-JAY official pages (pmjay.gov.in, nha.gov.in).

Ayushman Vay Vandana Card

The Ayushman Vay Vandana Card extends PM-JAY cover to senior citizens aged 70 and above, irrespective of their income — a deliberate break from PM-JAY's usual bottom-40%-by-deprivation targeting for this one age group. It was rolled out on 29 October 2024. Eligible seniors receive an additional top-up cover of ₹5 lakh per family per year, separate from and on top of any existing PM-JAY family cover.

Trap. Unlike the base PM-JAY scheme, eligibility for the Vay Vandana Card is by age alone (70+), not by SECC deprivation criteria or income — every citizen aged 70 and above qualifies regardless of existing insurance or economic status.

Source: PIB / NHA official announcements on the Ayushman Vay Vandana Card launch (29 October 2024).

2. Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM)

The Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission aims to build the digital backbone of India's healthcare system through interoperable digital health records. Its core building blocks are the Ayushman Bharat Health Account (ABHA) — a unique, voluntary digital health ID for every citizen; a set of registries (of healthcare professionals and facilities, among others) that verify and list participants in the digital health ecosystem; and the Unified Health Interface (UHI), an open protocol enabling different digital health applications and providers to interoperate, conceptually similar in design philosophy to the Unified Payments Interface (UPI) in the payments space.

Source: National Health Authority, ABDM official pages (abdm.gov.in).

3. Swachh Bharat Mission

The Swachh Bharat Mission (SBM) is India's national sanitation campaign, with rural and urban components (SBM-Gramin and SBM-Urban) run respectively under the Ministry of Jal Shakti and the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs. It is credited with driving India's rural sanitation coverage sharply upward and with the declaration of rural India as Open Defecation Free (ODF).

Audit update (second pass) — gap closed. SBM's launch date is now confirmed verbatim on the official SBM-Grameen portal (Department of Drinking Water & Sanitation, Ministry of Jal Shakti): "the Swachh Bharat Mission was launched on October 2, 2014, by the Hon'ble Prime Minister, with a focus on making India Open Defecation Free (ODF)." The page separately confirms the Mission's 10th anniversary was marked on 2 October 2024, independently corroborating the date.

Source: PIB coverage of Swachh Bharat Mission; SBM-Grameen official portal (swachhbharatmission.ddws.gov.in/about_sbm), launch date confirmed second pass.

4. Nutrition — POSHAN Abhiyaan, Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0

POSHAN Abhiyaan (the National Nutrition Mission) targets malnutrition, stunting, and anaemia among children, adolescent girls, pregnant women, and lactating mothers, using technology-enabled monitoring (the Poshan Tracker) and convergence across ministries. It has since been folded into Mission Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0, an umbrella integration of the erstwhile Anganwadi Services, POSHAN Abhiyaan, and the Scheme for Adolescent Girls, aimed at strengthening nutrition delivery and upgrading anganwadi infrastructure (including "Saksham Anganwadis" — upgraded centres with better infrastructure and early childhood care facilities) under the Ministry of Women and Child Development.

Source: Ministry of Women and Child Development, POSHAN Abhiyaan / Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0 official material.

The Anganwadi worker

The Anganwadi worker is a grassroots functionary of the Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) scheme, running the local anganwadi centre delivering supplementary nutrition, pre-school education, health check-ups, and referral services. A frequently-tested administrative point: ICDS, and therefore the Anganwadi worker, falls under the Ministry of Women and Child Development, not the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare — a distinction that matters because so much of the rest of this chapter's content does sit under MoHFW.

Trap. It is easy to assume every "health" frontline worker sits under MoHFW. ASHAs and ANMs do (see Chapter 6); Anganwadi workers do not — they are ICDS functionaries under the Ministry of Women and Child Development.

Source: Ministry of Women and Child Development, ICDS scheme material.

5. Mission Indradhanush and the Universal Immunisation Programme

The Universal Immunisation Programme (UIP) is India's routine childhood and maternal immunisation programme, one of the largest in the world by volume, providing vaccination against a defined set of vaccine-preventable diseases free of cost. Over the years the UIP schedule has been expanded to add newer vaccines, including the Pneumococcal Conjugate Vaccine (PCV) and Rotavirus vaccine, both phased into the routine schedule to address major causes of childhood pneumonia and diarrhoeal disease respectively.

Trap. The HPV (Human Papillomavirus) vaccine is not yet part of the routine UIP schedule, unlike PCV and rotavirus. Audit update (second pass) — now confirmed with launch specifics. PM Modi launched the nationwide HPV vaccination campaign on 28 February 2026 at Ajmer, Rajasthan, targeting girls aged 14 (with catch-up for those turning 15 within 90 days of the campaign), free at government facilities, using the imported Gardasil-4 (quadrivalent, types 6/11/16/18) procured via a GAVI partnership — around 1.15 crore girls were expected to receive it. PIB's own 17 March 2026 release explicitly calls this a "Nationwide HPV Vaccination Campaign (2026)," grouped under "Recent Vaccine Launches & Programmes" — and critically, PIB's own published National Immunisation Schedule table (birth through 16 years) does not list HPV among the routine antigens, confirming it still sits outside the formal UIP schedule as of this audit. One nuance worth flagging: the 28 February launch release does state that "thereafter, the vaccine will continue to be available on routine immunisation days" and frames GAVI's support as helping India ultimately "introduce the HPV vaccine... into the national routine immunisation schedule" — but this is worded as an aim/direction of travel, not a confirmation that formal UIP integration has already happened. India's own indigenous Cervavac is not the vaccine used in this campaign (only named as a prior-vaccination exclusion criterion alongside Gardasil-9 and Cervarix). A question that lists "PCV, rotavirus, and HPV" together as confirmed UIP additions is testing exactly this distinction — HPV should not be included as a confirmed routine UIP vaccine, even after the 2026 campaign.

Source: Drishti IAS current-affairs coverage (25 February 2026) on HPV vaccination status; MoHFW UIP schedule material for PCV and rotavirus; PIB, "Cervical Cancer Vaccination Campaign Launched" (28 Feb 2026); PIB, "Celebrating the Power of Vaccines" (17 Mar 2026).

Mission Indradhanush and Intensified Mission Indradhanush (IMI) — now at phase 5.0

Mission Indradhanush is the special catch-up immunisation drive supplementing the UIP, aimed at reaching children and pregnant women who have been missed by or are partially covered under routine immunisation. Intensified Mission Indradhanush 4.0 (IMI 4.0) was launched on 7 February 2022 by Union Health Minister Dr. Mansukh Mandaviya, run across three rounds (7 February, 7 March, and 4 April 2022), covering 416 districts — including all 75 districts identified under the Azadi Ka Amrit Mahotsav framework — across 33 States and Union Territories.

Audit update (second pass) — newer phase found. IMI 4.0 is not the most recent phase: Intensified Mission Indradhanush 5.0 (IMI 5.0) followed, run in three rounds — 7-12 August, 11-16 September, and 9-14 October 2023 — per PIB (12 October 2023). Two notable changes from earlier phases: IMI 5.0 was conducted across all districts in the country (rather than a targeted subset like IMI 4.0's 416 districts), and it expanded eligibility to children up to 5 years of age (earlier phases covered only up to 2 years). As of 30 September 2023, over 34.7 lakh children and 6.55 lakh pregnant women had received doses in the first two rounds. PIB frames IMI 5.0 as the 12th phase of Mission Indradhanush overall since the base programme's 2014 launch, with a cumulative 5.06 crore children and 1.25 crore pregnant women vaccinated under Mission Indradhanush to date. No IMI 6.0 or later phase was found in this pass — IMI 5.0 (2023) remains the most recent confirmed phase as of this audit (September 2026).

Source: PIB, IMI 4.0 launch press release (PRID 1796099, 7 February 2022); PIB, IMI 5.0 progress/conclusion release (PRID 1966931, 12 October 2023).

U-WIN

U-WIN is the digital immunisation registry built to track and manage routine immunisation for pregnant women and children under the UIP (and, per the HPV discussion above, the special HPV campaign as well), recording every vaccination event digitally and generating verifiable digital certificates.

U-WIN is frequently described in secondary/coaching-material sources as built "similar to CoWIN" (the COVID-19 vaccination platform) in its digital architecture. 7. Tele-MANAS

Tele-MANAS (Tele Mental Health Assistance and Networking Across States) is India's national tele-mental-health service, launched on 10 October 2022 (World Mental Health Day) under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, providing toll-free, 24/7 telephonic counselling and mental health support across the country through a network of State-level cells.

8. Maternal health programmes

Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY) and Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK)

Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY) is a conditional cash transfer scheme promoting institutional delivery among poor pregnant women, to reduce maternal and neonatal mortality linked to home births without skilled attendance. Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK) builds on JSY by entitling every pregnant woman delivering in a public health institution — and every sick newborn — to completely free services, including free delivery (including caesarean section), free drugs and consumables, free diagnostics, free diet during the hospital stay, free blood, free transport from home to institution and between facilities, and free transport back home, explicitly to eliminate out-of-pocket expenditure as a barrier to institutional delivery.

SUMAN — Surakshit Matratva Ashwasan

SUMAN, launched to build on JSY and JSSK, stands for "Surakshit Matratva Ashwasan" — this is the exact official spelling used in PIB's own material and should be used precisely rather than a phonetically "corrected" alternative spelling such as "Surakshit Matritva Aashwasan." SUMAN's objective is to guarantee assured, dignified, and quality healthcare, free of cost, to every pregnant woman and newborn, with zero tolerance for denial of services, extending JSY/JSSK's free-entitlements approach into a broader assured-care guarantee covering ante-natal, delivery, post-natal, and newborn care.

Trap. SUMAN's official expansion is "Surakshit Matratva Ashwasan" (per PIB) — note "Matratva," not "Matritva," and "Ashwasan," not "Aashwasan." Small spelling variants circulate; the PIB spelling is the one to rely on if a question turns on the exact expansion.

Source: PIB material on JSY, JSSK, and SUMAN.

Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana (PMMVY)

PMMVY is a maternity benefit programme providing cash incentives to pregnant and lactating mothers, but its history and current benefit structure are more layered than a single "launched in 2017" statement captures. The underlying scheme traces back to 2010, as the Indira Gandhi Matritva Sahyog Yojana (IGMSY); it was renamed and relaunched as the "Pradhan Mantri" scheme in 2017, implemented nationally in alignment with the National Food Security Act, 2013's maternity-benefit provision. On benefit amounts: the widely-cited figure of ₹5,000 in three instalments was the original 2017 cash-incentive structure; this has since been revised, and the current benefit is ₹6,000, paid in two instalments of ₹3,000 each, per the scheme's post-National-Food-Security-Act implementation structure. Both figures are correct for their respective time periods — a question should be read carefully for which framing (original 2017 structure vs. current structure) it is asking about.

Trap. Do not state PMMVY as simply "launched in 2017" without qualification — the scheme's lineage runs back to the 2010 Indira Gandhi Matritva Sahyog Yojana, with the "Pradhan Mantri" rename and relaunch coming in 2017. Similarly, do not state the benefit as a flat, unqualified "₹5,000 in three instalments" — this was superseded by the current ₹6,000-in-two-instalments structure.

Source: PMMVY scheme documentation (via secondary compilation cross-checked for internal consistency); primary MoWCD/PIB verbatim confirmation of the revised instalment structure not independently completed in this pass beyond the cited compilation.

10. Nutrition and fitness campaigns — Eat Right India, Fit India, Rashtriya Poshan Maah

Eat Right India is FSSAI's flagship movement to shift India towards safer, healthier, and more sustainable food, combining regulatory measures, capacity building, and public outreach. It was launched — as "The Eat Right Movement," bringing together three pre-existing FSSAI initiatives (the Safe and Nutritious Food Initiative, the Eat Right Campaign, and Food Fortification) into one platform — on 10 July 2018 in New Delhi.

11. National Deworming Day (NDD)

National Deworming Day (NDD) is a Ministry of Health and Family Welfare initiative, run through the National Health Mission, to periodically administer a single dose of Albendazole (400 mg) to every child and adolescent in the country to reduce the disease burden of soil-transmitted helminth (STH) infections (intestinal worms). Per the NHM's own NDD Operational Guidelines, the programme's stated aim is to "deworm all preschool and school-age children (enrolled and non-enrolled) between the ages of 1-19 years," delivered free of cost through government and private schools and anganwadi centres, with a separate "Mop-Up Day" held a few days later to cover children absent on the main day. NDD was launched on 10 February 2015 (first Mop-Up Day: 13 February 2015), initially across 12 States/Union Territories (including Assam, Bihar, Delhi, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, and Tamil Nadu), and has since scaled into a pan-India exercise held twice a year — one round in February and a second in August. The Operational Guidelines set a coverage target of "at least 90%" of the eligible population as the programme's key performance indicator.

Trap. NDD's 1-19-year age band is easy to confuse with RBSK's birth-to-18-years screening band (§8). The two are structurally different: NDD is a single-intervention, mass biannual drug-administration day (one Albendazole dose against worms), while RBSK is a continuous, comprehensive "4 Ds" health-screening programme delivered by Mobile Health Teams. Do not describe NDD as a "screening" programme, or RBSK as a "deworming" programme.
The most recent PIB press release with a specific national coverage figure located in this pass is for NDD's 10th round (10 February 2020): 19 States had reached "9.35 crore of the target population" on the day itself, with the round "expected to reach an estimated 30 crore of the target population" overall. A fetchable PIB figure for the most recent (2025/2026) rounds was not located in this pass — search results returned only coaching-site blogs and an NHM guidance PDF for the August 2025 round, none with a PIB-sourced coverage number. Treat the 2015 launch date, the biannual February/August schedule, and the 90% coverage-target framework as confirmed; do not cite a specific recent-round coverage figure as PIB-confirmed.

Source: National Health Mission, National Deworming Day Operational Guidelines (nhm.gov.in/images/pdf/NDD/Guidelines/NDD_Operational_Guidelines.pdf); PIB, National Deworming Day 10th-round coverage (PRID 1602706, 10 Feb 2020).

12. ABHA adoption — deepening the ABDM picture

Building on the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission's building blocks described in §2, PIB's 30 May 2026 release on ABDM's adoption milestone gives a concrete growth trajectory for the Ayushman Bharat Health Account (ABHA): cumulative ABHA accounts created rose from 14.7 crore (2021) to 30.4 crore (2022), 50.6 crore (2023), 72.2 crore (2024), 84.5 crore (2025), and crossed 90 crore by May 2026. The same release reports that women make up 49.75% of ABHA holders, and that four smaller States/UTs — Andaman & Nicobar Islands, Ladakh, Lakshadweep, and Dadra & Nagar Haveli/Daman & Diu — have reached full ABHA saturation, while Andhra Pradesh's own saturation rate stands at 98.5%, among the highest of any large State (Odisha: 91.9%; Rajasthan: 89.7%). Uttar Pradesh leads in absolute numbers with over 15.3 crore ABHAs created, ahead of Rajasthan and Maharashtra (7.1 crore each) and Bihar (6.3 crore). NHA CEO Dr. Sunil Kumar Barnwal framed the milestone as ABHA being "an important step towards empowering citizens with secure, consent-based access to their own health information."

Trap. Do not conflate ABHA (an individual's voluntary digital health-ID account, part of ABDM) with Ayushman Bharat/PM-JAY (§1, the ₹5-lakh hospitalisation insurance scheme) — sharing the "Ayushman Bharat" name, the two are frequently mixed up in objective-type questions. ABHA is an identity/records layer carrying no insurance entitlement by itself; PM-JAY is the insurance cover. Holding an ABHA ID does not make someone a PM-JAY beneficiary.

Source: PIB, "Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission Crosses Landmark Milestone of 90 Crore ABHAs" (PRID 2266979, 30 May 2026).

13. Poshan Tracker — the digital backbone of Poshan 2.0

Building on §4's coverage of POSHAN Abhiyaan and Mission Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0, PIB's 24 July 2026 release on Poshan Tracker describes it as a digital governance application enabling "real time monitoring and tracking of all Anganwadi Centres (AWCs), Anganwadi Workers (AWWs) and beneficiaries" — the technology layer that turns Poshan 2.0's convergence goals into a trackable, auditable system, run by the Ministry of Women and Child Development. Functionally, the app covers Anganwadi Centre operations and attendance; Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE) activities; child growth monitoring and nutritional measurements; Hot Cooked Meal and Take-Home Ration (THR) distribution; and beneficiary identity verification using a Facial Recognition System (FRS). As of the release, 99.88% of registered beneficiaries are Aadhaar-verified on the platform, and it hosts a sizeable ECCE content library (249 videos, 190 audio messages, and over 130 further learning resources, including 140 home-based activities for children aged 0-3). A Nominee Module lets a registered beneficiary designate someone else to collect their Take-Home Ration when they cannot attend the Anganwadi Centre in person.

Trap. Poshan Tracker (the monitoring app), POSHAN Abhiyaan/Poshan 2.0 (the nutrition mission it monitors), and Rashtriya Poshan Maah/Poshan Pakhwada (§10, the periodic mass-mobilisation campaigns run under that mission) are three distinct things sharing the "Poshan" name — a question naming one is not automatically about the other two.

Source: PIB, "'Poshan Tracker' digital application is an important governance..." (PRID 2288793, 24 Jul 2026).

14. Menstrual hygiene — the Scheme for Promotion of Menstrual Hygiene

Distinct from the Andhra Pradesh-specific "Sweccha"/"Balika Raksha" renaming covered in Chapter 8, India has run a national-level menstrual-hygiene scheme since well before that State initiative: the Scheme for Promotion of Menstrual Hygiene, run by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare under the National Health Mission "since 2011" per PIB's 29 July 2022 release, targeting adolescent girls aged 10-19 years. Its core mechanism is subsidised sanitary-napkin distribution: packs supplied through ASHA workers at ₹6 per pack, alongside a parallel channel of oxo-biodegradable napkins branded "Suvidha," sold for just ₹1 per pad through Jan Aushadhi Kendras (§6) under PMBJP. PIB's more recent 17 December 2025 release updates the Jan Aushadhi Kendra count for this channel to "over 16,000" outlets and gives a cumulative Suvidha sales figure of 96.30 crore pads (as of 30 November 2025). The same release cites NFHS data showing the share of women aged 15-24 using hygienic menstrual-protection methods rising from 57.6% (NFHS-4) to 77.3% (NFHS-5), and notes about 34.92 lakh adolescent girls received a monthly sanitary-napkin pack in 2021-22.

Trap. Three separate menstrual-hygiene initiatives can appear in the same question and should not be merged: (1) the Scheme for Promotion of Menstrual Hygiene (MoHFW/NHM, national, since 2011, ASHA-distributed napkins) described here; (2) the Menstrual Hygiene Policy for School-Going Girls (approved November 2024, Department of School Education, covering vending machines/incinerators in schools under Samagra Shiksha); and (3) Andhra Pradesh's own "Sweccha"/"Balika Raksha" State scheme (Chapter 8). Only the first distributes napkins through Jan Aushadhi's "Suvidha" ₹1 pads.

Source: PIB, "Scheme for Promotion of Menstrual Hygiene" (PRID 1846147, 29 Jul 2022); PIB, "Government takes appropriate measures to improve menstrual hygiene..." (PRID 2205104, 17 Dec 2025).

15. Tobacco Free Youth Campaign 3.0 (2025)

A genuinely new addition to the exam's current-affairs window is the Tobacco Free Youth Campaign 3.0, launched on 9 October 2025 jointly by the Ministry of Education and the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, aimed at building "a tobacco-free learning environment" across schools and higher-education institutions nationwide, run over a 60-day window. It specifically targets student tobacco use in the 13-15 years age band, citing the Global Youth Tobacco Survey (2019) finding that 8.4% of students aged 13-15 currently use tobacco products, against a backdrop of over 13 lakh tobacco-related deaths annually in India and an average tobacco-initiation age of around 10 years. Its core strategies include enforcement drives, capacity-building for teachers and student volunteers, cessation counselling, enforcing 100-yard tobacco-free zones around educational institutions, awareness competitions, and digital engagement via the MyGov platform — explicitly positioned as feeding into the "Viksit Bharat@2047" development vision.

Trap. Unlike most schemes in this chapter, Tobacco Free Youth Campaign 3.0 is jointly led by the Ministry of Education, not run solely by MoHFW — its delivery channel is schools and colleges, not health facilities, which distinguishes it from MoHFW's own longer-running National Tobacco Control Programme. The "3.0" in its name signals this is an iterated version of an earlier campaign, not a brand-new one-off initiative — do not describe it as India's first youth tobacco campaign.

Source: PIB, "Government of India Launches 'Tobacco Free Youth Campaign 3.0'" (PRID 2176322, 9 Oct 2025).

16. The National Digital Health Blueprint (NDHB) — the architecture behind ABDM

Building on §2's coverage of ABDM's building blocks, the National Digital Health Blueprint (NDHB) is the foundational architecture document that ABDM operationalises — not a scheme in itself, but the design report that recommended how India's digital-health ecosystem should be built. It was prepared by a committee chaired by J. Satyanarayana, former Chairman of UIDAI, under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, and released in 2019; a PIB release dated 29 October 2019 quotes then Health Minister Dr. Harsh Vardhan describing it as follows: "The National Digital Health Blueprint(NDHB) is just a beginning; it gives us a direction and a path to move towards strengthening healthcare delivery systems." The PIB release also confirms the NDHB was built to converge India's fragmented, siloed health-IT systems onto a common platform and explicitly draws on the "National Health Stack" concept earlier developed by NITI Aayog.

Per the National Resource Centre for EHR Standardisation (NRCeS, the MoHFW-recognised digital-health standards body), the NDHB's core building blocks include a Unique Health ID, federated Personal/Electronic Health Records ("Health Locker"), a Consent Manager giving citizens control over who accesses their data, an Anonymizer for privacy-preserving de-identification, a Health Information Exchange (HIE) for secure data-sharing, and health/facility/professional registries. Its design principles rest on a federated architecture — health data stays at the point of care rather than being centralised in one database — a minimalistic approach to ease adoption, and mandated open standards for interoperability (FHIR, SNOMED-CT, LOINC, ICD-10, DICOM), summed up in the Blueprint's own stated implementation strategy: "Think Big, Start Small, Scale Fast."

Trap. Do not conflate the NDHB (the 2019 architecture/design document, chaired by J. Satyanarayana) with ABDM (§2, the actual mission/programme — piloted 2020, launched nationwide 2021 — that implements the NDHB's recommendations, including ABHA, the registries, and the Unified Health Interface). A question asking "who chaired the committee behind India's digital health architecture" is asking about the NDHB, not ABDM as such.

Source: PIB, "National Digital Health Blueprint(NDHB) is just a beginning" (29 Oct 2019, release ID 194063); National Resource Centre for EHR Standardisation (NRCeS), National Digital Health Blueprint page (nrces.in).

17. Eat Right Station and Eat Right Campus — FSSAI's operational certifications

Building on §10's coverage of the Eat Right India umbrella movement, FSSAI runs several specific, auditable certification programmes under it — the two most exam-relevant being Eat Right Station and Eat Right Campus. Eat Right Station is run by FSSAI in partnership with Indian Railways, certifying food-hygiene and safety standards across every food-service point at a railway station — retail outlets and catering establishments, food plazas and food courts, petty food vendors and kiosks, and base-kitchen/warehouse facilities. Per FSSAI's own Eat Right Station FAQ, certification follows a three-stage process — a Railways-side self-assessment pre-audit, mandatory FoSTaC (Food Safety Training and Certification) of food-handling staff, and a post-audit by an accredited third-party agency — with a station required to score at least 65% to qualify.

Eat Right Campus extends the same "Eat Safe, Eat Healthy, Eat Sustainable" logic to institutional settings — colleges and universities, workplaces, hospitals, jails, and tea estates — evaluated on four parameters: food-safety compliance, provision of nutritious food, environmental sustainability, and consumer-awareness activities. Unlike Eat Right Station's pass/fail minimum-score model, Eat Right Campus uses a five-star rating scale following third-party audit; a campus scoring three stars or higher earns formal "Eat Right Campus" certification, valid for two years, with FSSAI's "Orange Book" serving as the implementation guide. A PIB release on FSSAI certifying nearly 100 prisons nationally illustrates the jails category of this same certification in practice.

Trap. Eat Right Station and Eat Right Campus are two of several distinct sub-certifications FSSAI runs under the single Eat Right India umbrella (others include Eat Right School and Eat Right Market) — they are not interchangeable names for the same certificate, and they use different rating mechanisms: Station is a minimum-score (65%) pass/fail audit, while Campus is a five-star scale requiring three-plus stars for certification.

Source: FSSAI, Eat Right Station FAQs (eatrightindia.gov.in/eatrightstation/faq.php); FSSAI, Eat Right Campus "About Us" (eatrightindia.gov.in/EatRightCampus/aboutUs); PIB, "Pioneering Food Safety in Jails, FSSAI Certifies Nearly 100 Prisons..." (PRID 2014551).

18. The Free Diagnostics Service Initiative (FDSI) — current national-level status

Distinct from Andhra Pradesh's own state-branded NTR Vaidya Pariksha (Chapter 8), the underlying scheme it implements is the national-level Free Diagnostics Service Initiative (FDSI), run by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare under the National Health Mission since July 2015, to make laboratory, radiology and imaging services free at every tier of the public health system and reduce out-of-pocket diagnostic spending. PIB's 12 August 2026 factsheet, "Affordable and Accessible Healthcare for All," confirms the scheme remains current and describes it as making "tests, X-rays, blood work free at every level of the public health system from sub-centres all the way up to district hospitals," with coverage "spanning haematology, radiology, biochemistry, and more." The tier-wise test-count structure it lists — 14 tests at Sub Centres, 63 at Primary Health Centres, 97 at Community Health Centres, 111 at Sub-District Hospitals, and 134 at District Hospitals — is identical to the figures given in PIB's earlier updates (5 August 2022 and 10 February 2023 Parliament replies), indicating no PIB-confirmed increase in the national tier-wise test counts through this September 2026 audit, even as the 2026 factsheet's own framing of the scheme has been refreshed.

A search for a more recent, higher national test-count figure (some secondary/coaching sources hint at scheme "expansion") did not turn up a PIB-sourced number above the 134-at-DH figure above. The 2022 update separately notes free CT scan service operational in 27 States/UTs and free tele-radiology in 13 States, and coverage across all 33 States/UTs via a mix of 11 PPP/hybrid and 22 in-house delivery models — these operational-model figures were not re-confirmed in a 2025/2026 PIB source in this pass and should be treated as last confirmed for 2022, not current.
Trap. FDSI is the pan-India NHM scheme; NTR Vaidya Pariksha (Chapter 8) is Andhra Pradesh's own state-branded implementation layered on top of it. A question naming the State scheme is not automatically asking about the national tier-wise test-count figures given here, and vice versa — treat the two as related but not synonymous.

Source: PIB, "Affordable and Accessible Healthcare for All" factsheet (id 150834, 12 Aug 2026); PIB, "Update on Free Drugs & Diagnostics Service Initiative" (PRID 1897986, 10 Feb 2023); PIB, "Update on Free Diagnostics Service Initiative (FDSI)" (PRID 1848807, 5 Aug 2022).

19. SAHI and BODH — India's Strategy for AI in Healthcare (2026)

A genuinely new addition for this exam's current-affairs window: in February 2026, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare unveiled the Strategy for AI in Healthcare for India (SAHI) alongside a companion validation platform, BODH (Benchmarking Open Data platform for Health AI). Per PIB's own release, SAHI is explicitly framed as "a recommendatory national framework on the way AI can be integrated into healthcare services," intended to guide policymakers, healthcare providers, and technology developers toward responsible AI adoption that aligns "with clinical needs, regulatory standards, equity considerations, and public trust," so as to make healthcare "more accessible, timely, high-quality and affordable." Its core pillars, per PIB, cover governance and evidence-generation standards, safe/ethical/transparent digital and data infrastructure, and workforce readiness — with the release stressing that AI's "adoption must be anchored in public interest, trust, and long-term system resilience." BODH, developed by IIT Kanpur in collaboration with the National Health Authority, is the companion mechanism for testing and validating Health AI solutions before deployment at scale, ensuring tools are "safe, reliable, and validated against real-world parameters" before being used on real patients.

Sourcing on the exact launch venue is not fully consistent across primary channels found in this pass. A PIB release frames SAHI/BODH as unveiled during the India AI Impact Summit 2026 (New Delhi, PIB-confirmed dates 16-21 February 2026), while a WHO South-East Asia Regional Office page separately describes a SAHI "launch" event in Geneva on 17 February 2026, attended by Union Health Minister J.P. Nadda, NHA CEO Dr. Sunil Kumar Barnwal, and IIT Kanpur's Prof. Manindra Agrawal. Both fall within the same short window and concern the same underlying document, so "launched in February 2026" is the safe, defensible exam fact; committing to one single ceremony or city as "the" launch is not fully supported by the sources located here.
Trap. SAHI is described in PIB's own material as a "recommendatory" framework — that is, guidance/policy, not a binding law or regulation — and it is a distinct, newer layer from both ABDM (§2, the digital-records/interoperability infrastructure) and the NDHB (§16, ABDM's underlying architecture blueprint): NDHB and ABDM are about health-data infrastructure and records, while SAHI is specifically about governing the use of AI on top of that infrastructure. Do not describe SAHI as "part of ABDM" or as replacing it.

Source: PIB, "Trust, Diversity and Inclusion: AI in Healthcare" (PRID 2235388); WHO South-East Asia Region, "Launch of the Strategy for AI in Healthcare for India (SAHI)"; PIB, "India AI Impact Summit 2026: Landmark Global Declaration and Major Announcements" (PRID 2234343) for summit dates.

Mnemonics

1. Swachh Bharat's split ministries — "Village cleans with Jal, City cleans with Housing." SBM-Gramin sits with the Ministry of Jal Shakti (the rural/water ministry); SBM-Urban sits with the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs. If a question's answer choice pairs Gramin with anything other than Jal Shakti, or Urban with anything other than Housing & Urban Affairs, it is wrong.

2. Jan Aushadhi's ministry — "Medicines are chemicals, so Jan Aushadhi lives with Chemicals, not Health." PMBJP sells generic medicines but is administered by the Department of Pharmaceuticals under the Ministry of Chemicals & Fertilizers, because pharmaceuticals manufacturing/pricing is a chemicals-ministry subject, not a health-ministry one.

3. Who runs the frontline worker — "WCD raises the child, MoHFW heals the body." Anganwadi workers (ICDS, POSHAN) sit under the Ministry of Women and Child Development; ASHAs and ANMs (immunisation, institutional delivery, PM-JAY) sit under MoHFW. A question mixing up which frontline worker belongs to which ministry is testing exactly this split (already flagged in §4).

4. The two launch-year clusters — "2014 is Swachh/Suraksha, 2018 is Ayushman/Abhiyaan." 2014 clusters the base-programme launches — Swachh Bharat Mission (2 Oct) and Mission Indradhanush (Dec). 2018 clusters a second wave — Ayushman Bharat/PM-JAY, POSHAN Abhiyaan, and Eat Right India (10 Jul) all launched that year. Anchoring a scheme to the wrong cluster-year is a common distractor in statement-based MCQs.

Gap 1 — FSSAI's own legal and institutional basis (UPSC Prelims 2018)

An official, confirmed UPSC Civil Services Prelims 2018 question asked respondents to evaluate two statements: (1) "The Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 replaced the Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954," and (2) "The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) is under the charge of Director General of Health Services in the Union Ministry of Health and Family Welfare." The correct answer is "1 only" — statement 1 is true, statement 2 is false. This chapter's §§10 and 17 cover FSSAI's campaigns (Eat Right India, Eat Right Station, Eat Right Campus) in detail but never establish FSSAI's own founding law or governance structure — a genuine gap, since this is exactly the kind of institutional fact PYQs return to.

Per the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006's own preamble (fetched from the Act's text on fssai.gov.in), the law exists "to consolidate the laws relating to food and to establish the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India for laying down science based standards for articles of food." FSSAI's own RTI disclosure document confirms the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare is FSSAI's administrative Ministry — but "administrative Ministry" is an oversight/budgetary relationship, not a chain-of-command one. FSSAI's own Chairperson-recruitment circular states the Chairperson is appointed by the Central Government and must be "holding or have held a position not below the rank of Secretary to the Govt. of India" — a political/administrative appointment in its own right, not a role filled by, or subordinate to, the Ministry's Director General of Health Services (DGHS), who is a separate technical-cadre post. That distinction is precisely what the 2018 question's false second statement trades on.

Trap. "FSSAI sits under MoHFW" (true, as its administrative Ministry) is not the same claim as "FSSAI is headed by/under the charge of the Director General of Health Services" (false). FSSAI's statutory head is its own Secretary-rank Chairperson, appointed by the Central Government — a distinct office from DGHS. A question conflating "administered by MoHFW" with "run by a MoHFW officer" is testing exactly this gap.

Source: Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, preamble (fssai.gov.in); FSSAI RTI disclosure document, Section 4(1)(b) (fssai.gov.in), confirming MoHFW as administrative Ministry; FSSAI Chairperson recruitment circular (fssai.gov.in), confirming Secretary-rank eligibility criterion and Central Government appointment; cross-checked against UPSC Civil Services Prelims 2018, General Studies Paper I.

Gap 2 — POSHAN Abhiyaan's four actual objectives, and what it is not about (UPSC Prelims 2017)

An official, confirmed UPSC Civil Services Prelims 2017 question (Question 23) asked which of four statements describe objectives of the "National Nutrition Mission": (1) creating awareness relating to malnutrition among pregnant women and nursing mothers; (2) reducing the incidence of anaemia among young children, adolescent girls and women; (3) promoting the consumption of millets, coarse cereals and unpolished rice; (4) promoting the consumption of poultry eggs. The correct answer is "1 and 2 only" — statements 3 and 4, about food-consumption promotion, are not POSHAN Abhiyaan objectives at all, despite sounding plausibly nutrition-related. This chapter's §4 already names malnutrition, stunting, and anaemia as target areas, but states them loosely as a list rather than as the Mission's own four defined objectives, and never flags that food-diversification promotion (millets, eggs) is a distractor rather than a POSHAN Abhiyaan goal — a real gap, since the 2017 question's entire design turns on that distinction.

Per a PIB backgrounder on Rashtriya Poshan Maah (dated September 2025, fetched directly), POSHAN Abhiyaan's own four stated objectives are: reducing stunting, reducing undernutrition (underweight), reducing low birth weight, and reducing anaemia — in children aged 0-6 years, adolescent girls, and women aged 15-49 years — anchored on protecting the "first 1,000 days" from conception to a child's second birthday as the critical window for breaking intergenerational malnutrition. The same document cites NFHS progress on these exact indicators: stunting among children under five fell from 38.4% (NFHS-4, 2015-16) to 35.5% (NFHS-5, 2019-21); underweight prevalence fell from 35.8% to 32.1%; wasting fell from 21.0% to 19.3% over the same period.

Trap. POSHAN Abhiyaan's four objectives are stunting, undernutrition, low birth weight, and anaemia reduction — not food-consumption promotion. Promoting millets/coarse cereals belongs to separate initiatives (the International Year of Millets 2023 and the "Shree Anna" push), and poultry-egg promotion is not a stated nutrition-mission objective at all. A question that lists these alongside the real four objectives, exactly as UPSC 2017 did, is testing whether the real objective list has been memorised precisely enough to reject the plausible-sounding extras.

Source: PIB, "Poshan Maah: Building a Healthier, Stronger Nation Through Nutrition" backgrounder (September 2025); cross-checked against UPSC Civil Services Prelims 2017, General Studies Paper I, Question 23.

Gap 3 — Swachh Bharat Mission's second pillar: ODF Plus and Solid & Liquid Waste Management (SLWM), and GOBARdhan

No officially-confirmed UPSC Prelims or Mains question naming Swachh Bharat Mission's Solid & Liquid Waste Management (SLWM) component specifically could be located in this pass — that search came up empty beyond coaching-site compilations of uncertain provenance. However, two confirmed-official other competitive-exam PYQs located during this search (IB ACIO Grade-II Executive, 17 January 2024, testing whether SBM "aims to eradicate open defecation and improve solid waste management"; and UGC NET Paper 1, 11 July 2022, testing which Ministries/Departments jointly implement the GOBARdhan scheme) both turn on a fact this chapter's §3 and §20 genuinely do not cover: that SBM's mandate extends well beyond toilet-building and ODF status into ongoing waste management — a gap worth closing given how directly it bears on how SBM is actually described in current government material, and how easily an ODF-only framing becomes outdated or incomplete as an answer.

Per PIB's own Swachh Bharat Mission (Grameen) Phase II factsheet (updated to 10 February 2026 figures), Phase II runs 2020-21 to 2026-27 with two explicit goals beyond ODF: sustaining ODF status (converting villages into "ODF Plus" / "ODF Plus Model" villages, defined by visual cleanliness and functioning waste-management systems) and extending Solid and Liquid Waste Management (SLWM) to all villages. As of that release, 4,96,180 of India's 5,86,944 villages (roughly 85%) had achieved ODF Plus status; 5,29,231 villages had solid-waste-management coverage and 5,46,487 had liquid-waste-management coverage; Uttar Pradesh led in ODF Plus villages (94,155), followed by Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan. Since 2014, SBM-Grameen has also built over 11.98 crore individual household latrines and 2.65 lakh community sanitary complexes (per the same PIB factsheet series).

The GOBARdhan scheme ("Galvanizing Organic Bio-Agro Resources Dhan") is the flagship delivery mechanism for the SWM half of this mandate: announced in the February 2018 Budget Speech and launched on 30 April 2018, it is hosted by the Department of Drinking Water and Sanitation, Ministry of Jal Shakti — the same Department that runs SBM-Grameen — and converts cattle dung and other organic/farm waste into compost, biogas, and bio-CNG, with financial assistance of up to ₹50 lakh per district supporting community/cluster biogas plants through 2026-27, per the scheme's own official "About Us" page (gobardhan.sbm.gov.in). GOBARdhan is explicitly described there as "an integral component of Solid Waste Management (SWM) under Phase II of SBM(G)" — i.e., it is not a free-standing scheme parallel to SBM, but a named sub-component inside it.

Trap. Describing Swachh Bharat Mission as being "about toilets and open defecation" alone is incomplete for SBM-Grameen Phase II (2020-21 to 2026-27), whose own stated goals are twofold: sustaining ODF status (the "ODF Plus" framework) and covering villages with Solid & Liquid Waste Management — with GOBARdhan as SWM's flagship delivery mechanism, administered by the Department of Drinking Water and Sanitation under the Ministry of Jal Shakti, not a separate ministry.
The UGC NET 2022 question's own answer key names a four-Ministry/Department combination for GOBARdhan (excluding the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change) that this pass could not fully cross-verify against a single primary GOBARdhan source beyond its home Department (Drinking Water and Sanitation) and its evident links to animal husbandry (cattle dung) and petroleum/renewable-energy sectors (biogas/bio-CNG, compressed biogas) implied by its own stated purpose — treat the precise multi-Ministry convergence list as indicative rather than independently confirmed here.

22. Cross-Chapter Linkages

APPSC Mains answers that score well on this sub-topic do not treat "recent public health initiatives" as a closed list of scheme names — they show how those schemes actually move through the delivery system described elsewhere in the syllabus, and what they are measured against. This closing section draws two such linkages explicitly, each anchored to specific facts and figures already established in this book's own Chapter 6 (Healthcare Delivery and Outcomes) and Chapter 4 (Sustainable Development), rather than asserted as generic connections. Neither sibling chapter was altered to write this section; every figure below is quoted or closely paraphrased from that chapter's own existing text, with its section number given so a reader can verify it directly.

22.1 The delivery backbone — how Chapter 6's infrastructure and workforce actually carry these initiatives to patients

This chapter's own §1 notes that Ayushman Bharat rests on "two inter-related components" and flags that the first — Health and Wellness Centres — was "renamed Ayushman Arogya Mandir in November 2023 (covered in Chapter 6)." That cross-reference is only half-exploited until it is read against Chapter 6's own figures: §2 of that chapter records 1,81,873 Ayushman Arogya Mandirs operationalised nationally as of 30 November 2025, each delivering an expanded 12-service package plus teleconsultation, with a cumulative footfall of 494.71 crore and 41.93 crore teleconsultations since inception. PM-JAY (this chapter's §1) is a pure financing instrument — it reimburses secondary/tertiary hospitalisation wherever it occurs — but the primary-care half of Ayushman Bharat is not an abstraction; it is literally this same, physically countable Ayushman Arogya Mandir network.

The frontline workforce Chapter 6 quantifies in its §3 is the actual delivery mechanism for several schemes this chapter names as if they were self-executing. ASHA workers, normed at one per 1,000 rural population (up to one per 2,500 in urban areas), are the explicitly-named delivery channel for this chapter's own Scheme for Promotion of Menstrual Hygiene (§14): subsidised sanitary-napkin packs are "supplied through ASHA workers at ₹6 per pack." That means the ASHA deployment density Chapter 6 documents — not the scheme's national budget or its Jan Aushadhi/"Suvidha" ₹1-pad channel (also §14) — is what actually determines how many adolescent girls in a given village can be reached. Similarly, ANMs, normed at two per Sub-Centre, each Sub-Centre serving roughly 5,000 people (Chapter 6, §3), are the staff who administer Universal Immunisation Programme doses and Mission Indradhanush catch-up rounds (this chapter's §5); the jump from IMI 4.0's 416 targeted districts to IMI 5.0's all-district, up-to-5-years coverage (§5) is a scale-up this chapter states in terms of districts and age bands, but its practical throughput ceiling is set by that same two-ANMs-per-Sub-Centre staffing norm.

Capital infrastructure tells the same story for diagnostics. Chapter 6's §4 details PM-ABHIM's district-level build-out — integrated public-health laboratories in every district and critical-care hospital blocks in every district with population over 5 lakh — reporting, as of its most recent audit, 744 Integrated Public Health Labs against a target of 730 and 621 Critical Care Hospital Blocks against a target of 602 (both already exceeded), funded from ₹33,081.82 crore of PM-ABHIM's ₹64,180-crore outlay already approved for release to states. This chapter's own Free Diagnostics Service Initiative (§18) promises a precisely tiered basket of free tests — 14 at Sub Centres, 63 at Primary Health Centres, 97 at Community Health Centres, 111 at Sub-District Hospitals and 134 at District Hospitals — but that tiered promise is deliverable only where the physical laboratory capacity to run those tests actually exists. PM-ABHIM's district-level lab build-out (Chapter 6, §4) is the capital-infrastructure precondition for FDSI's test-count ladder (this chapter's §18) being more than a published entitlement on paper.

The same pairing holds on the digital side: this chapter's §12 records ABHA crossing 90 crore accounts by May 2026, the demand-side (citizen-identity) half of the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission. Chapter 6's own §12 separately records the supply-side counterpart — the Health Facility Registry and Healthcare Professionals Registry having registered over 410,000 healthcare facilities and linked 671 million health records as of August 2025. An ABHA ID (this chapter's §2, §12) is only useful once it can actually exchange records with a registered facility; the 90-crore ABHA figure and the 410,000-facility figure are two halves of one interoperability system that neither chapter, read alone, states as explicitly linked.

Trap. Do not describe a scheme in this chapter as though it has its own independent delivery network. Ayushman Bharat's HWC component, UIP/Mission Indradhanush, FDSI, and the Scheme for Promotion of Menstrual Hygiene all ride on the same NHM facility tiers (Sub-Centre → PHC → CHC → District Hospital) and the same ASHA/ANM workforce that Chapter 6 quantifies — a question contrasting "how many schemes" versus "how much delivery capacity" India has is testing exactly this distinction.

22.2 The delivery vehicles — how these initiatives move Chapter 4's SDG 3 and SDG 6 index numbers

Chapter 4's goal-wise SDG India Index deep dive (§8) records SDG 3 — "Ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages," per that chapter's own official-title table in §3 — improving from a composite score of 52 (2018) to 77 (2023-24), with the underlying data citing a maternal mortality ratio of 97 per 100,000 live births and 97.18% of deliveries occurring in health institutions. Those two underlying numbers are not free-standing statistics this chapter can treat as someone else's concern: they are the measurable output of the maternal-health delivery chain described in this chapter's own §8 — Janani Suraksha Yojana's conditional cash transfer for institutional delivery, Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram's free-entitlements package (free delivery including caesarean section, free transport, free diagnostics, free blood), and SUMAN's "zero tolerance for denial of services" guarantee layered on top of both. A 97.18% institutional-delivery rate is the index-level signature of that same JSY/JSSK/SUMAN chain, not an independent fact about Indian hospitals.

Chapter 4's second health-adjacent composite, the National Multidimensional Poverty Index (§11), scores every household on a Health dimension built from "nutrition, child/adolescent mortality, maternal health" among its twelve indicators, weighted equally alongside Education and Standard of Living. POSHAN Abhiyaan and Mission Saksham Anganwadi/Poshan 2.0 (this chapter's §4), whose Poshan Tracker app monitors beneficiary nutritional status in real time (§13), work directly on that Health dimension's nutrition indicator; National Deworming Day's biannual Albendazole rounds, targeting a "at least 90%" coverage of the 1-19-years eligible population (§11), reduce the intestinal-worm burden that is itself a recognised driver of child undernutrition, feeding the same indicator from a different angle. NITI Aayog's own assessment that "India is likely to achieve SDG Target 1.2 [halving multidimensional poverty] much ahead of 2030" (Chapter 4, §11) is therefore not solely a hunger/income story — the Health dimension's one-third weight in that composite means this chapter's nutrition-delivery machinery is one of the three legs holding up that poverty-reduction trend.

On the sanitation side, Chapter 4's §8 records SDG 6 — "Ensure availability and sustainable management of water and sanitation for all" (Chapter 4, §3) — improving from a composite score of 63 (2018) to 89 (2023-24), the largest point-gain of the three goals that section highlights, attributed to "near-universal household toilet coverage and open-defecation-free status reported across districts." That is a direct description of this chapter's own Swachh Bharat Mission coverage (§3, §21 Gap 3): SBM-Gramin's rural ODF declaration, and the finding that Phase II (2020-21 to 2026-27) has pushed 4,96,180 of India's 5,86,944 villages (roughly 85%) to "ODF Plus" status with functioning solid-and-liquid-waste-management systems. Chapter 4's SDG 6 score movement is, in effect, the index-level readout of the same village-by-village SBM rollout this chapter tracks.

GOBARdhan, SBM-Gramin's flagship mechanism for converting cattle dung and farm waste into compost, biogas and bio-CNG (this chapter's §21, Gap 3), also sits inside the wider circular-economy architecture Chapter 4 documents in its §6: municipal solid/liquid waste is one of the eleven sector-specific committees NITI Aayog and the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change constituted in 2021 to shift India's waste streams away from a "take-make-dispose" linear model, that particular stream led by the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs. GOBARdhan's own financial-assistance ceiling of ₹50 lakh per district for community/cluster biogas plants is, in miniature, precisely the waste-to-resource conversion Chapter 4 frames as necessary given India's "only 2% of the world's landmass and 4% of freshwater resources" — a resource constraint Chapter 4 quotes PIB stating directly in its circular-economy discussion (§5).

Trap. Do not present a single scheme in this chapter as mechanically "causing" a specific point-increase in Chapter 4's SDG India Index scores — the index is a multi-indicator composite built from State/UT-reported data across the MoSPI National Indicator Framework (Chapter 4, §4), and no PIB release attributes a given score movement to one named scheme. What is established here is narrower and verifiable: the specific indicators Chapter 4 quotes as driving SDG 3 and SDG 6 (institutional-delivery rate, MMR, toilet coverage/ODF status) are the exact targets this chapter's JSY/JSSK/SUMAN and Swachh Bharat Mission coverage are designed to move — a scheme-to-indicator match, not a claimed statistical attribution.
