1. The idea of sustainable development

The term "sustainable development" was popularised by the report Our Common Future (1987), produced by the World Commission on Environment and Development (WCED) — a UN-convened body chaired by former Norwegian Prime Minister Gro Harlem Brundtland, and hence universally known as the Brundtland Report or the Brundtland Commission. The Commission itself was established four years earlier, in 1983, when UN Secretary-General Javier Pérez de Cuéllar appointed Brundtland as its chair, formalised by UN General Assembly Resolution 38/161 ("Process of Preparation of the Environmental Perspective to the Year 2000 and Beyond"), adopted 19 December 1983 — a detail worth keeping distinct from the 1987 publication date of its report. Its definition, quoted by the UN itself, is the single most exam-relevant line in this topic: development is sustainable when it is "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."

This one sentence contains two ideas the syllabus tests separately. The first is intergenerational equity — the principle that today's generation holds the planet's resources in trust for generations not yet born, and may not consume or degrade them in a way that forecloses future generations' own options. The second is carrying capacity — the idea, borrowed from ecology, that any given environment can sustain only a finite level of resource extraction and waste absorption before it degrades; development that ignores this ceiling is, by definition, not sustainable regardless of its short-term economic gains.

Trap. The Brundtland definition is about balancing present needs against future generations' needs — it is not, by itself, a statement about balancing economic growth against environmental protection (that three-way balance — economic, social, environmental — is a separate, later elaboration, covered in §2 below). Don't conflate the two when a question quotes the definition directly.

Source: UN CSD, "Framing Sustainable Development: The Brundtland Report – 20 Years On" (un.org/esa/sustdev/csd/csd15/media/backgrounder_brundtland.pdf).

3. The Millennium Development Goals and the 2030 Agenda

The MDGs (2000–2015) — the predecessor framework

The Millennium Declaration, signed by world leaders in September 2000, gave rise to eight Millennium Development Goals, each to be pursued by the target year 2015: (1) eradicate extreme poverty and hunger; (2) achieve universal primary education; (3) promote gender equality and empower women; (4) reduce child mortality; (5) improve maternal health; (6) combat HIV/AIDS, malaria and other diseases; (7) ensure environmental sustainability; and (8) develop a global partnership for development.

Trap. The MDGs applied chiefly as targets for developing countries, with only Goal 8 framed as a shared, partnership-based obligation on developed countries. The SDGs that replaced them in 2015 are explicitly universal — every country, developed or developing, is expected to pursue all 17 goals. This developing-country-specific versus universal contrast is the same structural shift seen between the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement (Chapter 3, §1).

The 2030 Agenda and its 17 Sustainable Development Goals

At the UN Sustainable Development Summit held in New York from 25 to 27 September 2015, member states adopted Transforming our World: the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, whose own Declaration states plainly: "The 17 Sustainable Development Goals and 169 targets which we are announcing today demonstrate the scale and ambition of this new universal Agenda." The new goals and targets, per the Declaration's own text, "will come into effect on 1 January 2016" and were to guide policy over the following fifteen years, i.e. to 2030.

The Agenda frames its ambition around five linked themes, commonly called the "5 Ps," each given its own subsection in the Declaration: People ("determined to end poverty and hunger, in all their forms and dimensions"), Planet ("determined to protect the planet from degradation... sustainably managing its natural resources and taking urgent action on climate change"), Prosperity ("determined to ensure that all human beings can enjoy prosperous and fulfilling lives"), Peace ("determined to foster peaceful, just and inclusive societies"), and Partnership ("determined to mobilize the means required to implement this Agenda through a revitalised Global Partnership"). Separately, the Declaration also commits to achieving development "in its three dimensions – economic, social and environmental – in a balanced and integrated manner." The older, commonly-quoted "three pillars" formulation traces to the 2005 World Summit Outcome document (UN General Assembly resolution A/RES/60/1, adopted 24 October 2005), whose paragraph 48 states verbatim: "These efforts will also promote the integration of the three components of sustainable development – economic development, social development and environmental protection – as interdependent and mutually reinforcing pillars." Note the document's own wording calls them "three components... interdependent and mutually reinforcing pillars," not a standalone fixed phrase "three pillars" — a nuance worth preserving if a question asks for the exact 2005 formulation rather than the popular shorthand.

4. India's SDG architecture

NITI Aayog is India's designated coordinating body for the SDGs — in its own words, "the nodal institution for achieving SDGs in the country, leading the 2030 Agenda with the spirit of cooperative and competitive federalism," with a "twin mandate to oversee the adoption and monitoring of the SDGs in the country and promote competitive and cooperative federalism among States and UTs." The statistical backbone sits with the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI), which developed and maintains the National Indicator Framework (NIF) — "MoSPI is responsible for the development of the National Indicator Framework (NIF) for measuring the progress of the SDGs and associated targets," while "NITI Aayog is facilitating and coordinating the implementation of SDGs in the country." The NIF, in its 2019 (Version 1.1) form, comprised 306 indicators spread across the 17 goals, overseen by a High Level Steering Committee chaired by the Chief Statistician of India that the Union Cabinet approved on 24 October 2018.

The SDG India Index

NITI Aayog's principal tracking tool is the SDG India Index, which scores every State and UT on a composite 0–100 scale using the MoSPI NIF indicators, and classifies them into bands including Front Runner (score 65–99). The fourth edition, SDG India Index 2023–24, was released by NITI Aayog on 12 July 2024 and reported that "the composite score for India improved from 57 in 2018 to 66 in 2020-21 to further to 71 in 2023-24," computed across 113 indicators spanning 16 of the 17 goals (Goal 14, on oceans, is excluded from the composite score because it applies only to India's nine coastal States). The number of Front Runner States/UTs rose to 32 in 2023-24, "up from 22 in the 2020-21 edition" — which itself had risen from just 10 Front Runners in 2019-20.

India presented its third VNR on 23 July 2025, delivered by NITI Aayog Vice Chairman Shri Suman Bery — PIB's release states this "marks India's third submission to the HLPF, reaffirming the country's sustained commitment to the UN 2030 Agenda for SDGs," with poverty alleviation (an estimated 248 million people having escaped multidimensional poverty) as its headline claim. 5. Measuring and governing sustainability — supporting concepts

Green GDP and natural capital accounting

India's own expert-committee work on this question reaches a conclusion that surprises many aspirants: an official Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation report, chaired by Prof. Sir Partha Dasgupta (not, as is sometimes assumed, C. Rangarajan) and submitted in March 2013, explicitly rejects the "green GDP" framing. Its own words: "The term 'green GDP' is thus an utter misnomer." Instead, the Dasgupta expert group argues that sustainability should be judged through a comprehensive measure of a nation's wealth — not a single adjusted GDP figure — stating that "the coin on the basis of which economic evaluation should be conducted is a comprehensive notion of wealth... not gross domestic product (GDP)," where wealth aggregates reproducible capital, human capital and natural capital (with natural-capital depletion counted as a deduction). This is the technical meaning of natural capital accounting as India's own official framework defines it.

Trap. Don't describe India's official position as "adjusting GDP for environmental depletion to calculate a Green GDP" — the government's own 2013 expert report explicitly disowns that framing by name and proposes inclusive wealth accounting instead. A question testing precise official positions, rather than the popular/textbook shorthand, should be answered with the Dasgupta report's own language.

Source: MoSPI, "Green National Accounts in India: A Framework," Report of the Expert Group chaired by Sir Partha Dasgupta, constituted 4 Aug 2011, submitted March 2013.

Circular economy

India's circular-economy push is coordinated through NITI Aayog and the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, which in 2021 constituted 11 sector-specific committees, each led by the relevant line ministry, to draft action plans for transitioning specific waste streams from a linear "take-make-dispose" model to a circular one. The eleven focus areas are municipal solid/liquid waste (Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs), ferrous and non-ferrous scrap metal (Ministry of Steel), electronic waste (MeitY), lithium-ion batteries (NITI Aayog itself), solar panels (MNRE), gypsum (DPIIT), toxic and hazardous industrial waste (Department of Chemicals and Petrochemicals), used oil (Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas), agricultural waste (Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers' Welfare), tyre and rubber recycling (DPIIT), and end-of-life vehicles (Ministry of Road Transport and Highways). PIB's framing of the motivation is a useful one-line justification for the exam: "with only 2% of the world's landmass and 4% of freshwater resources, a linear economy model of 'Take-Make-Dispose' would constrain India's manufacturing sector."

ESG reporting and SEBI's BRSR

Corporate sustainability reporting in India is governed by SEBI's Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR), introduced by circular SEBI/HO/CFD/CMD-2/P/CIR/2021/562 dated 10 May 2021. The circular states filing "shall be mandatory for the top 1000 listed companies (by market capitalization)... with effect from the financial year 2022-2023" (it was voluntary for FY 2021-22), and that BRSR "shall replace the existing BRR" (Business Responsibility Report), the older, thinner disclosure format SEBI had prescribed since 2015. BRSR reporting is framed around ESG (Environmental, Social and Governance) parameters, structured specifically as disclosures against the nine principles of the National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct (NGRBC), each split into mandatory "essential" and voluntary "leadership" indicators.

Planetary boundaries

The planetary boundaries framework was first proposed in 2009 by a group of 28 scientists led by Johan Rockström, identifying nine Earth-system processes whose limits define a "safe operating space for humanity": climate change, change in biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater change, modification of biogeochemical flows (nitrogen and phosphorus cycles), ocean acidification, increase in atmospheric aerosol loading, stratospheric ozone depletion, and the introduction of novel entities (chemical pollution). As of the framework's most recent (2025) update, per the Stockholm Resilience Centre, seven of the nine boundaries have been transgressed — all except atmospheric aerosol loading and stratospheric ozone depletion — with ocean acidification confirmed as the seventh crossed boundary in 2025.

Mission LiFE

Prime Minister Narendra Modi first floated the concept of "LiFE" (Lifestyle for Environment) at COP26 in Glasgow on 1 November 2021. The LiFE Global Movement itself was launched on 5 June 2022 (World Environment Day), and Mission LiFE was formally launched jointly by PM Modi and UN Secretary-General António Guterres at the Statue of Unity, Ekta Nagar, Gujarat, on 20 October 2022. PIB describes it as "an India-led global mass movement that will nudge individual and collective action to protect and preserve the environment," built on the "P3 model" — Pro Planet People — with the explicit objective "to mobilise at least one billion Indians and other global citizens to take individual and collective action... in the period 2022 to 2027," and a target that at least 80% of villages and urban local bodies become "environment-friendly" by 2028.

Sources: PIB, "Govt Driving Transition from Linear to Circular Economy" (18 Mar 2021); SEBI Circular SEBI/HO/CFD/CMD-2/P/CIR/2021/562 (10 May 2021); Stockholm Resilience Centre, "Planetary boundaries" (stockholmresilience.org); PIB, "PM & UNSG Launch Mission LiFE at Statue of Unity, Gujarat" (20 Oct 2022).

6. Circular economy — resource efficiency policy and Extended Producer Responsibility

§5 above named the eleven sector committees NITI Aayog and MoEFCC set up in 2021 to draft waste-stream action plans. This section goes one level deeper into the two instruments the syllabus most often tests within that circular-economy push: the still-pending national policy framework, and the Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) regime that is actually operating today, with real registration and recycling numbers.

The Draft National Resource Efficiency Policy — still a draft

MoEFCC released the Draft National Resource Efficiency Policy (NREP), 2019 for public comment on 25 July 2019. The draft "envisions a future with environmentally sustainable and equitable economic growth, resource security, healthy environment (air, water and land), and restored ecosystems with rich ecology and biodiversity," built on four guiding principles: reduction of primary resource consumption to sustainable levels (explicitly linked to achieving the SDGs); "creation of higher value with less material through resource efficient and circular approaches"; waste minimisation; and material security paired with employment and business models that serve environmental protection and restoration. PIB's own release on the consultation shows the process was still open-ended at that stage: the comment window, originally due to close on 31 August 2019, was extended to 24 September 2019 "to enable wider stakeholder consultation."

This research pass could not locate any subsequent PIB release, gazette notification or NITI Aayog document confirming that the NREP has been formally finalised and notified since its 2019 draft stage. Treat it, for exam purposes, as India's policy proposal for resource efficiency rather than a notified, binding policy, unless a source in hand states otherwise — checked as of September 2026.

EPR in numbers — plastic, e-waste and battery waste

Unlike the still-draft NREP, the EPR regime is a live, operating compliance system, run through the CPCB's centralised EPR portal. A Lok Sabha reply from MoEFCC (2 December 2024) gives a snapshot of registration under the Plastic Waste Management Rules, E-Waste (Management) Rules and Battery Waste Management Rules together: 44,659 registered Producers/Importers/Brand Owners (PIBOs) for plastic packaging waste, 7,050 for e-waste, 2,933 for battery waste, 179 for waste tyres and 8 for used oil, with the ministry noting that "the obligation of minimum level of recycling of plastic packaging waste, E waste, battery waste, waste tyre and used oil envisages strengthening of the waste management sector." A later Lok Sabha reply (23 March 2026), focused specifically on plastics, updates the registration count to 60,128 registered PIBOs and 3,012 registered Plastic Waste Processors, and states that "around 207 lakh tonnes of plastic packaging waste have been recycled, after the EPR Guidelines have come into force in 2022" — i.e. since the EPR Guidelines for plastic packaging (issued under the Plastic Waste Management Rules) took effect, distinct from the base Plastic Waste Management Rules, 2016 themselves. The same March 2026 reply gives CPCB-compiled state-reported plastic waste generation of 41,26,808 tonnes (2020-21), 39,01,802 tonnes (2021-22) and 41,36,188 tonnes (2022-23).

Trap. Do not treat "the Plastic Waste Management Rules" and "the EPR Guidelines for plastic packaging" as the same instrument with the same start date — the base Rules date to 2016, while the EPR Guidelines that created the registration/recycling-obligation system above "came into force" only in 2022, per the ministry's own reply.

NITI Aayog's 2026 roadmap for e-waste and lithium-ion battery circularity

In late January 2026, NITI Aayog released "Advancing Circular Economy of Waste Electronic and Electrical Equipment (E-waste) and Lithium-Ion Batteries in India," a report that supplies the specific figures this sub-topic had previously lacked. On e-waste: generation stood at 6.19 million metric tonnes (MMT) in 2024, projected to reach 14 MMT by 2030 at roughly 17% annual growth, making India the world's third-largest e-waste generator (about 7% of the global total) — yet only around 10% of this stream is currently recycled formally, with the informal sector handling an estimated 62% of it. By composition, computer equipment accounts for the largest share of the e-waste stream (65%), followed by large appliances and medical equipment (15%), telecom equipment (12%) and consumer electronics (8%). On lithium-ion batteries, the report projects demand rising from 16 GWh (2023) to 248 GWh by 2035 (about 26% annual growth), with end-of-life battery availability growing from 19 kilotonnes (2023) to 233 kilotonnes (2035) — against an infrastructure base of just 2,808 CPCB-authorised collection centres nationwide.

Sources: PIB, "Extension of timeline for public consultation on Draft National Resource Efficiency Policy" (26 Aug 2019); Draft National Resource Efficiency Policy, 2019 (MoEFCC, 25 Jul 2019); PIB, "Parliament Question: EPR Guidelines" (02 Dec 2024); PIB, "Parliament Question: Plastic Waste Management" (23 Mar 2026); NITI Aayog, "Advancing Circular Economy of Waste Electronic and Electrical Equipment (E-waste) and Lithium-Ion Batteries in India" (released week of 28 Jan 2026).

7. SEBI's BRSR — the 2025 assurance and value-chain expansion

§5 above described the original BRSR mandate (SEBI circular of 10 May 2021, mandatory for the top 1000 listed companies from FY 2022-23). Two further amendments — a SEBI board memorandum of 6 December 2024 and the operative circular that followed it — substantially extend that framework and are exam-relevant in their own right, since they turn BRSR from a disclosure-only exercise into one with third-party assurance.

The finalised changes were issued as SEBI's circular "Measures to facilitate ease of doing business with respect to framework for assurance or assessment, ESG disclosures for value chain, and introduction of voluntary disclosure on green credits," dated 28 March 2025. Two threads run through it. First, on assurance of BRSR Core: the underlying board note specifies "reasonable assurance for BRSR Core, beginning with top-150 listed entities (by market capitalization) from FY 2023-24 and gradually extended to the top 1000 listed entities by FY 2026-27" — but the March 2025 circular also gives companies a choice from FY 2024-25 onward, substituting the term "assurance" with "assessment or assurance," so a listed entity may now opt for the lighter-touch "assessment" route rather than mandatory third-party "assurance" for its BRSR Core disclosures. Second, on the value chain: applicable to the top 250 listed entities by market capitalisation, on a comply-or-explain basis, with value chain redefined as "the top upstream and downstream partners of a listed entity, individually comprising 2% or more of the listed entity's purchases and sales (by value), respectively" — while the entity "may limit disclosure of value chain to cover 75% of its purchases and sales (by value), respectively" in aggregate. The timeline for this value-chain layer was deferred by one year in the same circular: ESG disclosures for value chain partners now apply from FY 2025-26, and assessment/assurance of those disclosures from FY 2026-27. The circular's title also flags a linked but separate strand — introducing a voluntary disclosure on "green credits" alongside these changes.

Trap. "BRSR" (the mandatory disclosure filed by the reporting company itself) and "BRSR Core" (a smaller, assurance-eligible subset of high-priority ESG indicators within it) are not interchangeable — the phased assurance/assessment requirement above applies specifically to BRSR Core, not to the full BRSR filing. Similarly, "value chain" disclosure is about a listed entity's upstream/downstream partners, reported by the listed entity on their behalf on a comply-or-explain basis — it is not those partner companies filing their own BRSR.

Sources: SEBI, board memorandum "Facilitating ease of doing business with respect to Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR): Amendment to SEBI (LODR) Regulations, 2015" (agenda for meeting of 6 Dec 2024); SEBI circular, "Measures to facilitate ease of doing business with respect to framework for assurance or assessment, ESG disclosures for value chain, and introduction of voluntary disclosure on green credits" (28 Mar 2025).

8. Goal-wise progress under the SDG India Index — SDG 3, SDG 6 and SDG 13

§4 above gave India's composite SDG India Index score (57 → 66 → 71 across editions). The same 2023-24 release also breaks progress down goal-by-goal, and three of those goals sit squarely within this sub-topic's Environment-and-Health scope.

On SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-Being), PIB's release records that the goal's "overall score improved substantially from 52 in 2018 to 77 in 2023-24," with the underlying data citing a maternal mortality ratio of 97 per 100,000 live births and 97.18% of deliveries occurring in health institutions. 9. Localising the SDGs — State and District Indicator Frameworks

Beyond the national NIF (§4), NITI Aayog has pushed States and UTs to build their own indicator architecture, on the reasoning that most SDG-relevant subjects — health, water, sanitation, land — sit constitutionally with the States. NITI Aayog's own account of this, submitted to the UN's High-Level Political Forum in 2022, records that States were "encouraged to prepare their State Indicator Framework for each SDG based on the state vision documents, and national and global commitments."

The document tracks four progressive stages of localisation and, as of its writing, how many States/UTs had reached each: a State Vision/roadmap document (19 states and 3 UTs completed), a full State Indicator Framework (SIF) (25 states and 4 UTs), a District Indicator Framework (DIF) taking localisation down to the district level (16 states and 1 UT), and an operational SDG dashboard for public tracking (9 states and 1 UT) — a visibly narrowing funnel, with far more States able to publish a vision document than able to stand up a live, district-level dashboard. As a concrete illustration of the district tier, the same document cites the North-Eastern Region District SDG Index, which "compares 120 districts from the eight north eastern states across all sectors of the SDGs" using 84 indicators mapped to 50 targets — the most granular sub-state SDG comparison exercise the document names. Localisation was also extended to cities via the SDG Urban Index & Dashboard, covering 56 Indian cities across sectors including poverty reduction, health and nutrition, education and gender equality.

Treat the existence of an AP-specific portal as a lead for further research, not as a sourced claim.

10. NITI Aayog's 15-Year Vision, 7-Year Strategy and 3-Year Action Agenda

A frequently tested but often garbled fact concerns what NITI Aayog was actually asked to produce when it replaced the Planning Commission's Five Year Plans. Per the Comptroller and Auditor General's own audit (Report No. 8 of 2019, on India's preparedness for implementing the SDGs), NITI Aayog was directed by the Prime Minister's Office, in May 2016, to prepare three linked documents — a 15-Year Vision, a 7-Year Strategy, and a 3-Year Action Agenda — "inter-alia, taking into account SDGs."

The three documents did not arrive together, and did not engage with the SDGs equally. The Three Year Action Agenda (covering 2017-18 to 2019-20) was released first, on 1 August 2017 — its own preface states that "the Vision, Strategy and Action Agenda exercise represents a departure from the Five Year Plan process, followed with a handful of discontinuities until the fiscal year 2016-17," explicitly positioning it as the Five Year Plans' successor mechanism. Despite this, the CAG's audit found that the Action Agenda itself "does not specifically refer to SDGs," even though it substantively overlapped with 2030 Agenda themes. The Strategy document followed over a year later, released in December 2018 as Strategy for New India @75 (looking ahead to 2022-23) — and unlike the Action Agenda, the CAG found "each chapter has been mapped to relevant Goals/Targets," making the medium-term Strategy, not the shorter-term Action Agenda, the document that explicitly operationalised the SDG linkage. The 15-Year Vision document fared worst of the three: as of the CAG's 2019 audit, it remained unreleased, with NITI Aayog telling the auditors it would be "finalised by March 2020."

Trap. It is tempting to assume the shortest-horizon document (the 3-Year Action Agenda) would be the most tightly SDG-mapped, since 2017-2020 sits well within the SDG period. The CAG's audit found the reverse: the Action Agenda, released first and fastest, skipped explicit SDG mapping, while the slower, medium-term Strategy document did map itself to specific Goals and Targets.
This research pass could not confirm, via a fresh search, that NITI Aayog's 15-Year Vision document was ever released as a standalone document after its CAG-recorded March 2020 target slipped. Do not state a release date for it unless a source confirms one — checked as of September 2026.

Sources: CAG, Report No. 8 of 2019, "Preparedness for the Implementation of Sustainable Development Goals — Union Government (Civil)," Chapter II; NITI Aayog, Three Year Action Agenda 2017-18 to 2019-20 (1 Aug 2017).

11. NITI Aayog's National Multidimensional Poverty Index

Alongside the SDG India Index (§4), NITI Aayog maintains a second, narrower composite instrument aimed specifically at SDG Target 1.2 (halving multidimensional poverty by 2030): the National Multidimensional Poverty Index (National MPI), built with technical partners the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative (OPHI) and UNDP, and adapted from the global MPI framework to India-specific data and thresholds. NITI Aayog's own explainer note describes it as using the Alkire-Foster (AF) method to "assess acute poverty, providing a complementary perspective to conventional monetary poverty measures," scoring households on 12 indicators across three equally-weighted dimensions: Health (nutrition, child/adolescent mortality, maternal health), Education (years of schooling, school attendance) and Standard of Living (cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, assets, bank accounts) — the same three-dimension structure as the global MPI, drawing on National Family Health Survey (NFHS) rounds rather than the Global MPI's own data sources. The baseline explainer, released 27 November 2021 using NFHS-4 (2015-16) data, also situated India internationally at the time: "According to Global MPI 2021, India's rank is 66 out of 109 countries."

Two progress reports, two different comparison windows

NITI Aayog has since issued two distinct progress reports, each comparing a different pair of years, and exam answers should keep the two apart. The first, National Multidimensional Poverty Index: A Progress Review 2023, compared the two completed NFHS survey rounds directly — NFHS-4 (2015-16) against NFHS-5 (2019-21) — and found the national headcount ratio falling from 24.85% to 14.96%, with "about 135.5 million persons" (13.55 crore) having escaped poverty over that four-to-five-year window; rural poverty fell faster in relative terms (32.59% to 19.28%) than urban (8.65% to 5.27%). The second, a discussion paper titled Multidimensional Poverty in India since 2005-06 and released with a PIB statement on 15 January 2024, extended the series further, reporting that a cumulative 24.82 crore individuals escaped multidimensional poverty over nine years, with the national headcount ratio falling from 29.17% (2013-14) to 11.28% (2022-23) — "a reduction of 17.89 percentage points" — and noting the pace of decline itself accelerated, from an annual rate of 7.69% (2005-06 to 2015-16) to 10.66% (2015-16 to 2019-21). This same release states plainly why the index matters for this sub-topic: "India is likely to achieve SDG Target 1.2 (reducing multidimensional poverty by at least half) much ahead of 2030."

The 15 January 2024 release's 2013-14 starting point falls between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 survey rounds and is therefore not a year with its own direct NFHS observation; this research pass could not confirm, from the landing page at niti.gov.in for the underlying discussion paper, the precise interpolation or back-casting method used to generate that 2013-14 figure. Treat "24.82 crore over nine years" as NITI Aayog's own headline claim, but do not assert the technical basis for the 2013-14 baseline itself unless a source confirms it.

State-wise comparison

By absolute numbers escaping poverty over the nine-year window (2013-14 to 2022-23), the largest states dominate simply by population size: Uttar Pradesh (5.94 crore) led, followed by Bihar (3.77 crore), Madhya Pradesh (2.30 crore) and Rajasthan (1.87 crore). This is a different ranking from the headcount-ratio comparison — the share of each state's own population still poor — where, per the original 2021 baseline exercise, Bihar recorded the country's highest ratio at 51.91%, ahead of Jharkhand (42.16%) and Uttar Pradesh (37.79%), while Kerala recorded the lowest at 0.71%, ahead of Goa (3.76%) and Sikkim (3.82%).

Trap. Do not treat "the state where the most people escaped poverty" and "the state with the highest remaining poverty ratio" as the same ranking. Uttar Pradesh tops the first list purely because of its population size; Bihar tops the second because a larger share of its (smaller) population remains poor even after its own large absolute improvement. A question naming a specific state and figure should be checked against which of the two measures — absolute numbers escaping, or the remaining headcount ratio — it is actually asking about.

Sources: PIB, "24.82 crore Indians escape Multidimensional Poverty in last 9 years" (15 Jan 2024); NITI Aayog, "Multidimensional Poverty in India since 2005-06" (niti.gov.in, Jan 2024); PIB, "Explainer Note on National Multidimensional Poverty Index" (27 Nov 2021); NITI Aayog/OPHI/UNDP, "National Multidimensional Poverty Index: A Progress Review 2023."

12. India's Human Development Index ranking

The Human Development Index (HDI), published annually (with occasional gaps) by UNDP in its Human Development Report, is a composite of three dimensions — a long and healthy life (life expectancy at birth), knowledge (expected and mean years of schooling) and a decent standard of living (Gross National Income per capita, PPP-adjusted) — distinct from both the SDG India Index (§4, domestic and State/UT-wise) and the National MPI (§11, deprivation-based rather than an averaged composite). UNDP released the latest edition, Human Development Report 2025: A Matter of Choice — People and Possibilities in the Age of AI, on 6 May 2025, using 2023 data. India's press materials for the release record it as ranking 130th out of 193 countries, with an HDI value of 0.685, placing it in the "Medium Human Development" band. This is an improvement from the prior edition's ranking of 133rd (HDI value 0.676 for 2022 data), and UNDP's own framing situates the change over the longer run too: "India's HDI value has increased by over 53% since 1990, growing faster than both the global and South Asian averages." The underlying 2023 component figures were life expectancy of 72 years ("highest since index inception" per UNDP's India materials), mean years of schooling of 6.88 years (up from 6.57), and GNI per capita of $9,046.76 (up from $8,475.68).

Trap. India's HDI value of 0.685 remains in the Medium Human Development band (broadly 0.550–0.699 in UNDP's banding); the next band up, High Human Development, begins at 0.700. A rising HDI value and an improving rank do not by themselves mean India has crossed into the "High" category — it has not, as of the 2025 report's 2023 data.

The same report also carries an Inequality-adjusted HDI (IHDI), which discounts the headline HDI for within-country inequality across its three dimensions; UNDP's India press materials note that "inequality reduces India's HDI by nearly a third" (30.7%), described as "one of the highest losses in the region" — a reminder that the plain HDI rank alone understates how unevenly its underlying gains are distributed within the country.

Trap. The Inequality-adjusted HDI is not a separate ranking exercise with its own country rank — it is a downward adjustment applied to the same HDI components for the same country, reported as a percentage loss (30.7% for India). Don't describe it as "India's IHDI rank," since HDR press summaries report it as a loss percentage rather than a distinct global rank table entry.

Sources: UNDP, "India's human development continues to make progress, ranks 130 out of 193 countries" (undp.org/india, 6 May 2025); UNDP, Human Development Report 2025: A Matter of Choice — People and Possibilities in the Age of AI (Statistical Annex, HDI Table).

13. Gender Budgeting and SDG 5

India's fiscal instrument for operationalising commitments to gender equality is Gender Budgeting, formally institutionalised in the Union Budget from 2005-06 onward, building on the groundwork laid by the National Policy for the Empowerment of Women, 2001. The Ministry of Women and Child Development's own account describes it as "a fiscal strategy for translating the Government's commitments towards [gender equality and women's empowerment], across various sectors, into budgetary commitments," implemented through mandatory Gender Budgeting Cells set up across central ministries and departments, and made visible each year through the Gender Budget Statement (GBS) appended to the Union Budget.

The GBS splits reported allocations into three parts: Part A, schemes that are 100% targeted at women and girls; Part B, schemes where 30-99% of the allocation benefits women; and Part C, schemes with a women-benefiting share below 30% (a category added in more recent GBS editions). For 2025-26, the Ministry of Finance's own release records total gender-budget allocations of Rs. 4.49 lakh crore — a 37.25% rise over 2024-25's Rs. 3.27 lakh crore — made up of Part A (Rs. 1,05,535.40 crore, 23.50% of the total), Part B (Rs. 3,26,672.00 crore, 72.75%) and Part C (Rs. 16,821.28 crore, 3.75%). As a share of the overall Union Budget, this took the gender budget from 6.8% in 2024-25 to 8.86% in 2025-26, with 49 Ministries/Departments and 5 Union Territories reporting allocations against it — "the highest number of reporting by the Ministries/Departments in the GBS since its inception," per the same release, up from 38 reporting Ministries/Departments the previous year.

Trap. The Gender Budget is not a separate pool of money spent exclusively on women — only Part A is 100% women-specific spending. Parts B and C are ordinary scheme allocations (PMAY, MGNREGA-type programmes, health and education schemes, etc.) whose reported share is estimated to benefit women, not additional money layered on top of those schemes' regular budgets. A question describing "the gender budget" as if it were one dedicated women's-welfare fund is oversimplifying the GBS structure.

Gender Budgeting is the fiscal mechanism through which India pursues SDG 5 (Gender Equality) commitments in practice, but the two are tracked by different instruments and — on NITI Aayog's own numbers — moving at different speeds. The SDG India Index 2023-24 (the same release covered in §4/§8) records Goal 5's composite score rising only from 36 (2018) to 49 (2023-24) — its own wording: "Overall score improved significantly from 36 in 2018 to 49 in 2023-24" — which keeps Goal 5 in the lowest-scoring "Aspirant" band (score below 50) even as the Gender Budget's rupee value and Union Budget share have both grown substantially over the same period. The index's Goal 5 indicators span sex ratio at birth, female-to-male earnings ratios, female labour-force participation, family-planning access and women's participation in household decision-making.

This research pass could not locate a Ministry of Finance or NITI Aayog document that explicitly states, in so many words, that the Gender Budget Statement is designed to operationalise SDG 5 — the GBS release itself does not reference the SDGs, and the SDG India Index release does not reference gender budgeting. The pairing above rests on two separately sourced facts (rising GBS allocation share; stagnant-low Goal 5 Aspirant-band score) juxtaposed for exam relevance, not on an official document that links the two instruments by name. Treat the causal/operational link as the standard textbook framing rather than a verbatim government claim.

14. The Environmental Performance Index and India's official critique

The Environmental Performance Index (EPI) is a global ranking jointly produced by Yale University's Center for Environmental Law & Policy (YCELP) and Columbia University's Center for International Earth Science Information Network (CIESIN), scoring countries across dozens of indicators grouped under three policy objectives — Environmental Health, Ecosystem Vitality, and Climate Change. India has ranked consistently and controversially near the very bottom of this table across its recent editions. In the 2024 edition, India placed 176th of 180 countries, described in Indian coverage as "5th from bottom," with weak category scores including 177th on Air Quality, 172nd on Projected [2050] Emissions and 178th on Biodiversity and Habitat. In the 2026 edition, published in August 2026 for the index's 25th anniversary, India again placed 176th, now out of 177 countries, with a score of 22.46 — the second-lowest score in the entire table, ahead of only Laos, and seventh of eight South Asian countries ranked.

India's official rejection of the EPI's methodology

India's government has not accepted these rankings at face value. Following the 2022 edition (in which India was ranked last, 180th of 180), the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) issued a formal rebuttal on 8 June 2022, stating plainly: "The Ministry of Environment does not accept its analysis and conclusions." The rebuttal's specific technical objections are precise and worth retaining for the exam rather than a generic "India disagreed" summary:

  • the EPI's 2050 "Projected GHG Emissions" indicator was built by extrapolating "the average rate of change in emission of the last 10 years," without accounting for India's renewable energy capacity additions, carbon sinks or energy-efficiency gains;
  • India's forests and wetlands — both significant carbon sinks — "have not been factored in" when computing that projected-emissions trajectory;
  • indicator weights were reduced, without explanation, for measures on which India performs comparatively well — the rebuttal cites Black Carbon as an example where India's performance improved yet the indicator's weight fell;
  • the index gives very low weightage to the principle of equity, understating the Common But Differentiated Responsibilities (CBDR) principle central to India's position in global climate negotiations (Chapter 3);
  • its Air Quality sub-index leans on Copernicus satellite-modelled data that "have higher uncertainty in regions with less extensive monitoring networks"; and
  • the index omits indicators India considers material, including water quality, water-use efficiency, waste generation per capita, agro-biodiversity, soil health, and food loss and waste.

For the 2026 edition, secondary Indian reporting records that "the Union Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) and Indian experts questioned the EPI methodology," again on grounds that it "disregards India's development stage, population size, historical emissions, and the principle of Common but Differentiated Responsibilities" — substantively the same equity/CBDR objection as 2022, though within this research pass a dedicated 2026-specific PIB press release could not be located and confirmed directly.

The 2026-edition MoEFCC response above is sourced to secondary current-affairs coverage rather than a directly located PIB release specific to the 2026 EPI (unlike the 2022 rebuttal, which is confirmed from PIB itself). Treat the 2026 response as consistent with, but not verbatim-confirmed in the same way as, the 2022 PIB rebuttal quoted above.
Trap. The EPI (Yale/Columbia, a purely environmental composite on which India ranks near the very bottom) is a different instrument from both the domestic SDG India Index (§4/§8, on which India's own composite score has been rising, 57→71) and the Climate Change Performance Index (a separate climate-specific ranking). A question citing "India's poor global environmental ranking" without naming the index is most likely pointing at the EPI specifically — don't substitute the SDG India Index's much more favourable domestic trend line as a rebuttal to an EPI-specific question.

Sources: PIB, "Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change Rebuts the Environment Performance Index (EPI) 2022" (8 Jun 2022); The Print, "India ranks 5th from bottom in Environment Performance Index 2024" (2024); Columbia Climate School / CIESIN, "New Environmental Performance Index Highlights Sustainability Gains and the Challenges Ahead" (Jul 2026); Yale EPI, epi.yale.edu/country/2024/IND.

Memory aids for exam day

1. The 17 SDGs by theme cluster — ride the "5 Ps" (§3). Rather than memorising 17 goals as a flat list, group them by the Declaration's own five themes: People (Goals 1–5: poverty, hunger, health, education, gender), Prosperity (Goals 7–11: energy, work, industry, inequality, cities), Planet (Goals 6, 12–15: water, consumption, climate, oceans, land), Peace (Goal 16: justice and institutions), and Partnership (Goal 17: means of implementation). A question naming a goal number can be placed in its cluster even if its exact wording is forgotten.

2. The three dimensions/pillars (§1, §3) — "Grows, Shares, Sustains." Economic development grows the pie, social development shares it fairly, environmental protection sustains the base it is grown and shared from. This keeps the 2005 World Summit Outcome's "economic development, social development and environmental protection... interdependent and mutually reinforcing pillars" (§3's exact-quote trap) anchored to three distinct verbs rather than three interchangeable nouns.

3. SDG India Index bands, in ascending order (§4) — "Aspirants Perform, then Front-Run to Achieve." The sentence's own four capitalised words give the four official bands in increasing order of score: Aspirant (<50) → Performer (50–64) → Front Runner (65–99) → Achiever (100).

4. The conference chronology (§2) — "Stock up, Rio, Jo'burg, Rio again, then Thirty." Stockholm (1972) → Rio Earth Summit (1992) → Johannesburg/Rio-10 (2002) → Rio+20 (2012) → 2030 Agenda adopted in 2015. The gap between each milestone is a clean decade (1972→1992 is two decades, then 1992→2002→2012→2015 settles into roughly ten-year steps), which doubles as a check on any year a question quotes.

Closing the gap: the Global MPI's ten indicators, against the National MPI's twelve

§11 listed the National MPI's twelve indicators across Health, Education and Standard of Living, including maternal health and having a bank account, without stating whether those two also belong to the Global MPI. They do not. OPHI's own description of the global index lists exactly ten indicators across the same three equally-weighted dimensions: Health — nutrition, child mortality (two indicators, not three); Education — years of schooling, school attendance (two indicators, matching the National MPI); and Standard of Living — cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, assets (six indicators, not seven). A person is identified as "MPI poor" globally if deprived in at least one-third of these ten weighted indicators. Maternal health (within Health) and a bank/post-office account (within Standard of Living) are the two India-specific additions that take the National MPI from ten to twelve indicators — they were added because NITI Aayog adapted the Alkire-Foster framework to India's own NFHS data and policy priorities (maternal health ties to India's own maternal-mortality reduction goals; financial-inclusion access ties to the Jan Dhan Yojana-era push for universal bank accounts), not because the global index also tracks them.

Trap. A statement that pairs "Maternal Health" or "Bank Account" with the Global MPI, or implies the two indices share all twelve indicators, is incorrect even though both indicators are real and correctly described as part of India's National MPI. Always check which index — National (12) or Global (10) — a statement is actually naming before marking a maternal-health or bank-account claim correct.

Closing the gap: why a circular economy lowers greenhouse gas emissions

§6 documented India's circular-economy institutions (the eleven sector committees), the still-draft National Resource Efficiency Policy, and live EPR registration/recycling numbers — but never stated the mechanism connecting any of this to climate mitigation, the exact link the 2025 Prelims item tests. The Economic Advisory Council to the Prime Minister's (EAC-PM) working paper "India's Tryst with a Circular Economy" (July 2023) supplies it directly: "as much as 70% of [global] greenhouse gas emissions are tied to material handling and use," meaning the extraction, processing and disposal of raw materials — not only their end-use combustion — is itself a dominant emissions source. The same paper estimates that circular-economy approaches could "reduce up to 30% of total material used," and models the downstream climate effect of that reduction as GHG emissions "23% lower in 2030 and 44% lower in 2050 compared with the current development scenario." This is precisely the chain the PYQ's three statements describe: lower material inputs (II) and lower production wastage (III) both cut emissions (I) because material handling itself, not just fuel combustion, is responsible for the large majority of emissions in the first place.

Sources: OPHI, "What is the global MPI?" (ophi.org.uk/what-global-mpi); EAC-PM, "India's Tryst with a Circular Economy" (working paper, Jul 2023, eacpm.gov.in).

Two further traps worth one line each

Trap. "Limits to Growth" is not an alternate name for the Brundtland Report. It is a separate, earlier 1972 report commissioned by the Club of Rome (lead author Donella Meadows), using systems-dynamics modelling to warn that unchecked growth in population, industrial output and resource use would hit ecological limits within a century — a full fifteen years before Brundtland's 1987 Our Common Future (§1) and conceptually closer to the limits-based "carrying capacity" idea in §1 than to Brundtland's own needs-based definition.
Trap. The Human Development Index's three dimensions are income (GNI per capita), education and life expectancy (§12) — "condition of environment" is not one of them and never has been, a common distractor option. A country's environmental performance is tracked by an entirely separate instrument, the EPI (§14), on which India and its HDI ranking can diverge sharply — India's HDI has risen for years (§12) while its EPI rank has stayed near the bottom (§14), precisely because the two indices measure different things.

17. Cross-Chapter Linkages

APPSC Mains answers that treat "Sustainable Development" as a sealed topic lose marks a candidate who connects it outward does not. This closing section makes three such connections explicit and mechanistic — not "related to," but how and through what instrument — to three sibling chapters of this same book: Chapter 3 (international climate finance), Chapter 7 (public health schemes), and Chapter 8 (Andhra Pradesh's own SDG performance). Each subsection below names the specific fact in the sibling chapter being drawn on, by section number, so the link can be checked against that chapter directly rather than taken on faith.

17.1 International climate finance (Chapter 3) — feeding, and now overtaking, SDG financing

The 2030 Agenda's own financing target for climate action is not a vague aspiration; it is a numbered target with a specific, dated figure. SDG Target 13.a, as adopted in 2015, commits developed-country Parties "to a goal of mobilizing jointly $100 billion annually by 2020 from all sources... and fully operationalize the Green Climate Fund through its capitalization as soon as possible." That $100-billion figure is not an SDG-chapter abstraction — it is the exact same figure Chapter 3, §4 tracks as the climate-finance goal that COP29's New Collective Quantified Goal (NCQG) explicitly replaced: at Baku in November 2024, Parties agreed to "triple finance to developing countries, from the previous goal of USD 100 billion annually, to USD 300 billion annually by 2035," nested within a wider ambition to scale finance "to the amount of USD 1.3 trillion per year by 2035" (the "Baku to Belém Roadmap," further referenced at COP30's Belém Package, Chapter 3 §7, which separately committed to tripling adaptation finance by 2035). The SDG framework's own founding financing target is therefore already obsolete in its headline number, superseded by a climate-specific negotiating track that moves on its own COP-by-COP timeline rather than the SDGs' 2030 horizon — a mechanism worth citing precisely if a question asks how climate finance commitments have evolved since 2015, since quoting the $100-billion SDG target without flagging its COP29 replacement would date an answer to 2015-era knowledge.

The Green Climate Fund, the second instrument SDG 13.a names by requiring its "capitalization," is detailed in Chapter 3, §13: established under the 2010 Cancun Agreements as an operating entity of the UNFCCC's financial mechanism, it has to date committed over USD 20 billion across 134 developing countries, split roughly evenly between mitigation and adaptation. This is SDG Target 13.a's own operationalisation clause made concrete — but it sits alongside, and now competes for donor attention with, a newer instrument the SDG framework's 2015 text never anticipated: the Loss and Damage Fund (Chapter 3, §9), operationalised only at COP28 (December 2023), hosted by the Republic of the Philippines, with roughly USD 700 million pledged in its first days (led by the UAE's USD 100 million) against GCF's much larger USD 20-billion base. Three climate-finance channels — GCF (mitigation/adaptation, SDG-13.a-linked), the NCQG (the post-2020 successor to 13.a's $100bn figure), and the Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage (compensatory, outside the SDG target architecture altogether) — now draw on the same finite pool of developed-country climate pledges, a fragmentation the original single-target SDG 13.a text did not contemplate.

This financing picture also supplies the mechanism behind a pattern this chapter's §8 already documents without explaining: India's SDG India Index Goal 13 (Climate Action) score rose from 54 (2020-21, Performer) to 67 (2023-24, Front Runner), alongside renewable electricity's generation share climbing from 36.37% (2020) to 43.28% (2024). That domestic renewable build-out is financed overwhelmingly through India's own public and private capital, not through GCF or NCQG disbursements — a distinction that matters because India's own climate-diplomacy position, grounded in the CBDR-RC equity principle (Chapter 3, §8, and explicitly invoked in India's own 2031–35 NDC text, Chapter 3 postscript to §2: "global climate ambition must be matched by equitable support from the developed countries"), is precisely that rising domestic SDG 13 performance should not be read as substituting for the international finance flows SDG 13.a and its COP29 successor were supposed to deliver. A candidate citing India's Goal 13 score improvement as evidence that "climate finance is working" would be conflating domestically-financed mitigation progress with the international transfer the target was actually about.

17.2 National flagship health schemes (Chapter 7) — the delivery vehicles for SDG 3

This chapter's §8 records that the SDG India Index's Goal 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) composite score rose from 52 (2018) to 77 (2023-24) — the largest point-gain of the three Environment-and-Health goals tracked there, with the 2023-24 release citing a maternal mortality ratio of 97 per 100,000 live births and 97.18% institutional deliveries as the underlying data. Those two numbers are not abstractions the index invents; they are the direct, countable output of named Chapter 7 schemes. SDG Target 3.1 commits to "reduce the global maternal mortality ratio to less than 70 per 100,000 live births" by 2030 — the exact metric the 97/100,000 figure is being measured against — and Chapter 7, §8 names the scheme cluster mechanistically responsible for driving it down: Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY), the conditional cash transfer for institutional delivery; Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK), which entitles every pregnant woman delivering in a public facility to free delivery, free transport, free drugs, free diagnostics and free blood specifically to remove out-of-pocket cost as a barrier to institutional birth; and SUMAN (Surakshit Matratva Ashwasan), which extends that same free-entitlement logic into a zero-tolerance-for-denial guarantee across ante-natal, delivery and newborn care. The 97.18% institutional-delivery figure this chapter's §8 cites is, mechanistically, the JSY/JSSK/SUMAN cluster's own key performance indicator restated as an SDG India Index input.

SDG Target 3.8 — "achieve universal health coverage, including financial risk protection... for all" — is likewise not met by composite-score movement in the abstract; it is met, concretely, by Ayushman Bharat and PM-JAY (Chapter 7, §1), which provides a ₹5-lakh-per-family-per-year hospitalisation cover, cashless and paperless, targeted at the bottom 40% of India's population by SECC deprivation criteria — the financial-risk-protection mechanism Target 3.8 names explicitly. The digital infrastructure that makes this entitlement trackable and auditable is the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM): Chapter 7, §12 records cumulative ABHA (Ayushman Bharat Health Account) enrolments crossing 90 crore by May 2026, up from 14.7 crore in 2021 — the same digital identity layer that, in principle, lets MoSPI's National Indicator Framework (this chapter's §4) actually count UHC-relevant service delivery rather than estimate it. Beyond UHC and maternal health, Mission Indradhanush/IMI 5.0 (Chapter 7, §5 — run across all districts, extending catch-up immunisation coverage to children up to age 5) and Swachh Bharat Mission (Chapter 7, §3 — rural India declared Open Defecation Free, launched 2 October 2014) do the equivalent work for SDG 3's child-survival targets and for this chapter's own Goal 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation) score, which §8 records rising from 63 (2018) to 89 (2023-24) — sanitation coverage SBM itself produced. POSHAN Abhiyaan and Poshan 2.0 (Chapter 7, §4), with its Poshan Tracker digital monitoring layer (Chapter 7, §13), is the parallel delivery vehicle for the stunting/undernutrition indicators that feed both SDG 2 and SDG 3's child-health targets.

Trap. The SDG India Index's Goal 3 score (52→77) and Goal 6 score (63→89) are outputs, not mechanisms — a question asking "how has India improved its SDG 3/6 performance" should name the delivery schemes (PM-JAY, JSY/JSSK/SUMAN, Mission Indradhanush, Swachh Bharat Mission) from Chapter 7, not merely restate the index numbers from this chapter's §8. Citing the score movement alone, without the scheme that produced it, answers only "what changed," not "how" — and APPSC Mains questions on this theme typically ask for the latter.

17.3 Andhra Pradesh's own SDG India Index performance and localisation (Chapter 8)

This chapter's own §16 closed a long-standing cross-chapter gap for the 2020-21 edition specifically: per PIB's restatement of that edition's state-wise results, Andhra Pradesh scored a composite 72, placing it in a four-way tie for joint-4th rank nationally with Goa, Karnataka and Uttarakhand — behind Kerala (75, rank 1) and a Himachal Pradesh/Tamil Nadu tie (74, rank 2) — against that edition's national average of 66. Chapter 8, §17 is the fuller record of the parallel, still-unresolved search for AP's score in the current, more-cited 2023-24 edition: the topic inventory's claimed figure (74, rank 10th, tied with Gujarat and Telangana, named a Goal 7 top performer) remains unconfirmed there after three independent research passes, including a direct fetch of PIB's own 2023-24 release that, as Chapter 8 §17 documents, contains no state-by-state score table and no mention of Andhra Pradesh at all. The two chapters' gaps are the same gap, cross-referenced in both directions: this chapter's §16 explicitly notes "Chapter 4, §4 flags this same unresolved gap" is answered from Chapter 8's side, and Chapter 8's §17 cross-references back here. A question asking for "Andhra Pradesh's SDG India Index score" should be answered precisely by edition — 72/joint-4th is sourced and defensible for 2020-21; 74/10th is not yet sourced for 2023-24, and should be flagged as such rather than stated as settled fact.

Trap. Do not carry the confirmed 2020-21 figure (72, joint-4th) forward as if it were Andhra Pradesh's current score — the SDG India Index composite rose nationally from 66 (2020-21) to 71 (2023-24) over the same period (this chapter's §4), so an unchanged state score would itself represent a relative decline. Treat "AP's score is 72" as edition-specific and dated, exactly as this chapter's own §16 does.

Beyond the composite score itself, Chapter 8 documents Andhra Pradesh actually doing the sub-national localisation work this chapter's §9 describes in the abstract (State Vision documents, State Indicator Frameworks, District Indicator Frameworks, SDG dashboards — a "visibly narrowing funnel" nationally, per NITI Aayog's own account). AP's concrete instance of that funnel, specific to this chapter's Environment-and-Health theme, is the State Action Plan on Climate Change and Human Health (SAPCCHH), 2022–27 (Chapter 8, §18) — prepared by the state's own Department of Health & Family Welfare with NCDC technical support, organising AP's climate-sensitive health burden into five priority areas (air-pollution disease, heat-related illness, extreme-weather health impacts, vector-borne disease, and water/food-borne disease) and setting a target of 100% district-level awareness-campaign implementation by 2024-25. This is localisation precisely at the intersection this sub-topic is named for — not a generic SAPCC, but one fused with the health delivery apparatus Chapter 7 describes nationally. AP's six National Clean Air Programme non-attainment cities (Chapter 8, §19 — Chittoor, Nellore, Ongole, Guntur, Eluru and Rajahmundry, each with its own PM10 reduction target against a FY 2017-18 baseline) are the city-level equivalent of this chapter's §9 "SDG Urban Index & Dashboard" (56 Indian cities nationally) operating at the exact sub-state granularity that index was designed to track. And Mission Haritha Andhra Pradesh (Chapter 8, §22 — launched 5 June 2025, targeting a rise in green cover from 30.05% to 50% by 2047) is AP's own state-level analogue of the 15-Year-Vision-style long-horizon target architecture this chapter's §10 describes at the national level, applied specifically to SDG 15 (land) rather than left as an un-localised national aspiration.

Chapter 7, §12 separately records Andhra Pradesh's ABHA (digital health ID) saturation rate at 98.5% — among the highest of any large State, against Odisha's 91.9% and Rajasthan's 89.7% — which, if read alongside Chapter 8's unresolved 2023-24 SDG India Index gap, would suggest AP is performing strongly on at least the SDG 3-linked digital-health-infrastructure indicator even while its overall 2023-24 composite rank cannot yet be confirmed. This is offered as a plausible, source-grounded observation, not as a substitute for the missing composite score — ABHA saturation is one input among many the SDG India Index's Goal 3 indicators would draw on, not the goal score itself, and should not be cited as if it resolved the open 2023-24 gap.
