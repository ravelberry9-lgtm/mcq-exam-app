1. The shape of India's disease burden

India is mid-way through what public-health literature calls the epidemiological transition: a shift, as a population's health improves and life expectancy rises, from a burden dominated by communicable, maternal, neonatal and nutritional diseases toward one dominated by non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and injuries. The single most authoritative Indian data source for this shift is the India State-Level Disease Burden Initiative, a collaboration between the ICMR, the Public Health Foundation of India and the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, launched in October 2015 with support from the Union Health Ministry and first published in 2017 using Global Burden of Disease Study methodology. Its headline finding: "61% [of DALYs] was due to communicable, maternal, neonatal, and nutritional diseases... in 1990, which dropped to 33% in 2016. There was a corresponding increase in the contribution of non-communicable diseases from 30%... in 1990 to 55%... in 2016, and of injuries from 9% to 12%." The transition is uneven across India — the report notes the year each state group crossed the 50% mark for infectious-disease share of DALYs "ranged from 1986 to 2010" — which is why India is described as carrying a dual/double burden: communicable and non-communicable disease coexisting at high levels, and undernutrition coexisting with rising overweight and obesity, rather than one cleanly replacing the other.

Trap. "Epidemiological transition" describes the changing balance between disease categories over time (communicable declining in share, NCDs rising); "dual/double burden" describes the resulting coexistence at a single point in time. A question naming one term should not be answered with the other's definition.

Source: ICMR/PHFI/IHME, "India: Health of the Nation's States — The India State-Level Disease Burden Initiative" (2017), Executive Summary.

2. Non-communicable diseases and their national programmes

The national programme addressing the NCD share of this burden is the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases and Stroke (NPCDCS) — sometimes referred to in shorthand as NP-NCD — which states its own rationale as: "cardiovascular diseases, cancer, chronic respiratory diseases, diabetes and other NCDs are estimated to account for 63% of all deaths." It operates on a 60:40 Centre-State funding split (90:10 for North-Eastern and hill states) and screens the population aged 30 and above through Health & Wellness Centres.

The topic inventory names "the four shared risk factors: tobacco, alcohol, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity" as part of NP-NCD. This is the standard WHO framing for NCD risk factors generally, but the specific NPCDCS "About Us" page fetched for this chapter does not state that exact four-item phrase anywhere in its own text (its content covers financing, screening age and infrastructure, not risk factors) — treat the four-factor list as a general public-health fact rather than a verbatim NPCDCS quotation until a page stating it directly is found.

Tobacco is addressed by its own vertical programme, the National Tobacco Control Programme (NTCP), launched in 2007-08 to implement the Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act, 2003 (COTPA). COTPA's own scope, per the Health Ministry's own FAQ, is regulatory rather than prohibitory: it "regulates the consumption, production, supply and distribution of the tobacco products by imposing restrictions on advertisement, promotion and sponsorship of tobacco products; prohibiting smoking in public places; prohibiting sale to and by minors; and prohibiting sale of tobacco products within a radius of 100 yards of educational institutions," plus mandatory pictorial health warnings — but, in the FAQ's own words, "it doesn't ban tobacco products per se."

Mental health is governed by two distinct instruments that are easy to conflate: the National Mental Health Programme (NMHP), the older service-delivery scheme, and the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 (Act No. 10 of 2017), the newer rights-based law — passed by both Houses of Parliament in March 2017, assented to on 7 April 2017, and brought into force on 29 May 2018. Its central reform, in its own supporters' framing, is that it "effectively decriminalised attempted suicide, which was a punishable offence under Section 309 of the Indian Penal Code," redirecting a person who attempts suicide toward "opportunities for rehabilitation rather than criminal trial and punishment." The Act also restricts electroconvulsive therapy to emergency use, bars its administration without anaesthesia, and bans it outright for minors, and requires insurers to cover mental illness "on the same basis as for physical illness."

Two independent secondary sources (Centre for Mental Health Law & Policy commentary) now corroborate the substance of the decriminalisation provision in near-identical language to what is commonly quoted: "an individual who attempts suicide should be presumed to be under severe stress and should not be prosecuted." 3. Malnutrition, anaemia and the latest health survey data

WHO defines the three standard child-anthropometric indicators precisely, and the distinction matters for the exam: stunting (low height-for-age) "is the result of chronic or recurrent undernutrition," reflecting long-term deprivation; wasting (low weight-for-height) "usually indicates recent and severe weight loss," reflecting an acute shock; and underweight (low weight-for-age) is a composite measure — a child "may be stunted, wasted or both." India's flagship anaemia programme, Anaemia Mukt Bharat (AMB), launched in 2018, follows a declared "6x6x6" strategy: six target beneficiary groups (children 6-59 months, children 5-9 years, adolescents 10-19 years, pregnant women, lactating women, and women of reproductive age 15-49 years), six interventions (iron-folic-acid supplementation, behaviour-change communication, dietary diversification, deworming, antenatal management, and testing/treatment), and six institutional mechanisms, with a stated goal of a national anaemia prevalence below 5% by 2030.

A separate, disease-specific mission — the National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission, inaugurated by the Prime Minister on 1 July 2023 at Shahdol, Madhya Pradesh — targets elimination of sickle cell anaemia in India by 2047, through universal screening of 7 crore people aged 0-40 in affected tribal areas; as of July 2025, PIB reported 6 crore screened, 2.15 lakh people diagnosed with the disease and 16.7 lakh identified as carriers.

NFHS-6 (2023-24) versus NFHS-5 (2019-21)

The sixth round of the National Family Health Survey, conducted in 2023-24 by the International Institute for Population Sciences and released by the Union Health Ministry on 29 May 2026, shows broad improvement over NFHS-5. The topic inventory's headline figures are confirmed exactly: child stunting "declined substantially from 35.5% to 29.3%," full immunisation coverage "increased from 83.8% to 87.1%," and health-insurance/scheme coverage "expanded significantly from 41.0% to 60.2%." Other confirmed movements: institutional deliveries rose from 88.6% to 90.6%; antenatal care coverage rose from 92.6% to 95.9%; total fertility rate "remains at 2.0"; and severe wasting fell from 7.7% to 5.2% (a 32% reduction) — though underweight fell only marginally, from 32.1% to 31.8%.

4. Vector-borne diseases and elimination targets

The National Centre for Vector Borne Diseases Control (NCVBDC) administers the umbrella National Vector Borne Diseases Control Programme (NVBDCP) for six diseases: malaria, dengue, chikungunya, kala-azar (visceral leishmaniasis), lymphatic filariasis and Japanese encephalitis. Of these, NCVBDC itself distinguishes three that are formally "targeted for elimination" — malaria, lymphatic filariasis and kala-azar — from three that are "outbreak prone and climate sensitive" — dengue, malaria (which appears in both categories) and Japanese encephalitis.

Elimination-target years are a frequent source of confusion because several have been repeatedly missed and reset. Measles-rubella elimination was originally targeted for 2015, "later pushed to 2020," and "again revised for 2023 during the Covid-19 pandemic period" — the current target is 2026. India's official elimination definition is precise: "the absence of endemic measles transmission in a defined geographical area for at least 12 months, which has to be verified again after it has been sustained for at least 36 months in the presence of a high-quality surveillance system." Leprosy was declared eliminated "at the national level" in 2005, once national prevalence fell below the WHO elimination threshold, following NLEP's launch in 1983.

Audit correction (second pass). A secondary source used to flag this gap in the first pass suggested kala-azar and lymphatic filariasis had "both been eliminated in 2024" — this is contradicted by WHO's own fact sheets, checked directly in this pass. WHO's kala-azar page shows only Bangladesh validated for visceral-leishmaniasis elimination, in October 2023 — India is not on that list. WHO's November 2024 list of 21 countries validated for lymphatic-filariasis elimination likewise does not include India. NCVBDC's own 2024 national case data (436 kala-azar cases, 27 deaths) directly confirms kala-azar has not been eliminated nationally. The likely source of the "2024" confusion: WHO did validate India for trachoma elimination in 2024 (a different disease and programme entirely) — that milestone appears to have been conflated with kala-azar/filariasis in the secondary source originally consulted. Do not answer any question on kala-azar or lymphatic-filariasis elimination status with "2024" or "achieved" — as of the sources checked, both remain targeted-but-not-yet-eliminated nationally. District/sub-national leprosy elimination target years remain unconfirmed from any source checked in either pass.

A newer addition to this cluster is the National Action Plan for Prevention and Control of Snakebite Envenoming (NAPSE), launched 12 March 2024, with the explicit vision "to prevent and control snakebite envenoming in order to halve the numbers of deaths and cases of disability that it causes by 2030," using a One Health framework that brings in human, wildlife, tribal and animal-health stakeholders. PIB frames the scale of the problem starkly: "around 50,000 deaths occur of an estimated 3-4 million snakebites annually" in India, "half of all snakebite deaths globally," with roughly 90% caused by the "big four" — common krait, Indian cobra, Russell's viper and saw-scaled viper.

Sources: NCVBDC "About Us" (ncvbdc.mohfw.gov.in); PIB/ThePrint reporting on measles-rubella target reset; Wikipedia, "National Leprosy Eradication Program"; PIB, NAPSE launch release (12 Mar 2024).

5. Tuberculosis — the National TB Elimination Programme

India's TB control effort — first organised in 1962, reorganised as the Revised National TB Control Programme (RNTCP) in 1997, and renamed the National TB Elimination Programme (NTEP) with effect from 1 January 2020 "with Government of India's commitment to achieve the END TB targets 5 years earlier" than the global timeline — reports genuine, sourced progress: per the WHO Global TB Report 2025, cited by PIB, TB incidence in India "reduced by 21% – from 237 per lakh population in 2015 to 187 per lakh population in 2024," a decline PIB describes as running "almost double the pace of the decline observed globally at 12%." Treatment coverage rose from 53% (2015) to over 92% (2024), and the treatment success rate reached 90%, "ahead of [the] global treatment success rate of 88%."

Trap. A 21% fall in incidence is real progress but is nowhere near the roughly 90% reduction that "elimination" requires under WHO's End TB Strategy definitions — don't infer from the PIB release's positive framing that India is on track to formally eliminate TB by its own accelerated target; no source checked in this pass states that the target will be met, only that incidence and mortality are both falling faster than the global average.

The current push is the 100-day intensified TB-Mukt Bharat Abhiyan, launched 7 December 2024. A February 2025 progress update (roughly 10 weeks in) reported "over 5.1 lakh notifications... across India," "more than 10 crore vulnerable individuals... screened," and over 10 lakh community outreach camps ("Ni-kshay Shivirs") organised — later, later-stage figures for the full 100-day run are commonly cited elsewhere as higher (around 5.6 crore screened), but that specific combined figure was not found in the single progress-update page checked for this chapter and should be re-verified before being quoted as a campaign-final total.

Nutritional support for TB patients runs through Nikshay Poshan Yojana (launched April 2018), which since 2024 provides ₹1,000 per month per patient for the full treatment duration — up from ₹500 — plus Energy Dense Nutritional Supplementation for underweight patients (BMI below 18.5). Ni-kshay Mitra is the complementary community/donor scheme, under which individuals or organisations "adopt the household contacts of TB patients for distribution of food baskets with a view to improve the immunity of the family members of TB patients."

Sources: PIB, "TB incidence in India drops by 21%..." (12 Nov 2025); PIB, "Update on TB-Mukt Bharat Abhiyan" (22 Feb 2025); PIB, "Union Health Minister Unveils Key Initiatives to boost Nutrition Support for TB Patients" (7 Oct 2024); Wikipedia, "National TB Elimination Program (India)."

6. Other communicable-disease control programmes

Four further named programmes round out India's vertical disease-control architecture. The National AIDS Control Programme (NACP), launched in 1992, is now in its fifth phase, running 1 April 2021 to 31 March 2026 with a total outlay of ₹15,471.94 crore, explicitly tied to "United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals 3.3 of ending the HIV/AIDS epidemic as a public health threat by 2030." India's adult HIV prevalence stands at 0.22%, with roughly 14.2 lakh people living with HIV currently on free antiretroviral therapy.

The National Leprosy Eradication Programme (NLEP) (see §4 above) declared national-level elimination in 2005. The National Viral Hepatitis Control Programme (NVHCP), launched in July 2018 under the National Health Mission, aims to "combat hepatitis and achieve countrywide elimination of Hepatitis C by 2030," explicitly tied to SDG 3.3 and India's 2016 commitment at the 69th World Health Assembly. And the National Rabies Control Programme, alongside the dedicated National Action Plan for Dog-Mediated Rabies Elimination (NAPRE) launched on World Rabies Day, 28 September 2021, targets elimination of dog-mediated human rabies deaths by 2030 through a One Health approach — a particularly high-stakes target given the Health Ministry's own framing that "rabies is 100% fatal but 100% vaccine preventable," and that "33% of global rabies deaths are recorded in India."

Trap. Three different 2030 targets appear in this chapter for three different diseases — AIDS (NACP, SDG 3.3), Hepatitis C (NVHCP, SDG 3.3) and rabies (NAPRE) — plus a 2030 target for snakebite deaths (NAPSE, §4) and TB's own accelerated national target (5 years ahead of the global schedule, i.e. effectively an "NTEP" internal target distinct from these SDG-anchored ones). Don't let "2030" become a generic placeholder in an answer — match the disease to its own named plan.

Sources: PIB, NACP Phase-V Cabinet approval (21 Mar 2022); PIB, NVHCP rollout release (24 Feb 2019 update); PIB, NAPRE launch release (28 Sep 2021); Wikipedia, "National Leprosy Eradication Program."

7. One Health and zoonotic threats

India's National One Health Mission, implemented by ICMR, frames its rationale plainly: "India, with its diverse wildlife, one of the largest livestock populations and high density of human population, carries heightened risks for inter-compartmental spread of diseases," citing COVID-19, Lumpy Skin Disease outbreaks in cattle, and the recurring threat of avian influenza as evidence of "the need to address diseases across all sectors" — the underlying logic of zoonotic spillover, where pathogens jump from animal populations into humans. The Mission's governance has a specific, exam-relevant structure that is easy to get backwards: its Executive committee is chaired by the Union Health Minister (MoHFW), with the Principal Scientific Adviser (PSA) serving only as vice-chair; a separate Scientific Steering Committee is chaired by the PSA alone, providing scientific — not executive — direction. The National Institute of One Health, the Mission's anchor institution, is located at Nagpur.

Trap. It is tempting to assume the Principal Scientific Adviser — India's top science-policy official — chairs the Mission's top governing body. The Mission's own governance page states the opposite: the Union Health Minister chairs the Executive committee (the policy-guidance body), and the PSA chairs only the Scientific Steering Committee (the science-advisory body). Reverse this pairing and the answer is wrong.

Recent outbreaks illustrate the zoonotic risk directly. Kerala has recorded nine Nipah virus outbreaks since 2018: the largest, in 2018, had 23 cases and a 91% case-fatality rate; the most recent WHO-reported event, between 17 May and 12 July 2025, produced four confirmed cases (two deaths) across Malappuram and Palakkad districts — the first-ever outbreak recorded in Palakkad. Nipah's case-fatality rate across Bangladesh, India, Malaysia and Singapore "typically range[s] from 40% to 100%," among the highest of any known human pathogen.

Audit update (second pass). Mpox in India is now confirmed with case-level detail: India recorded a clade IIb mpox case with one death (Kerala, 30 July 2022), and separately a clade Ib cluster from the newer 2023-26 global outbreak — 18 cases, 0 deaths, the first of which was reported 24 September 2024 (case count as of roughly March 2026). H5N1 avian influenza, by contrast, is confirmed only in animal/poultry outbreaks in India — recurring since the Navapur, Maharashtra outbreak of 2006 — and no confirmed human H5N1 case in India was found in any source reached in this pass; this is recorded as an access/search gap rather than a confirmed absence of human cases, since not every possible source could be checked. Zika's periodic occurrence in India (e.g. earlier outbreaks in Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh) is well documented in general but was not independently re-verified with a fresh source in this pass either.

Sources: Office of the Principal Scientific Adviser, "National One Health Mission" (psa.gov.in); WHO Disease Outbreak News, "Nipah virus infection – India" (6 Aug 2025).

8. Surveillance and antimicrobial resistance

Disease surveillance runs through the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP), whose digital backbone was upgraded on 5 April 2021 with the launch of the Integrated Health Information Platform (IHIP) — described at launch as "the next generation highly refined version of the presently used" IDSP, expanding real-time tracking from 18 diseases to 33 and eliminating paper-based reporting; it was called "the world's biggest online disease surveillance platform" at launch. The lead institution for both surveillance and outbreak response is the National Centre for Disease Control (NCDC), Delhi — whose own institutional lineage traces back to the Central Malaria Bureau, established at Kasauli in 1909, renamed the Malaria Institute of India after moving to Delhi in 1938, and renamed again as the National Institute of Communicable Diseases (NICD) on 30 July 1963; it took its current name, NCDC, in 2009.

India's most recent National Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance (NAP-AMR) — the second iteration, "NAP-AMR 2.0," covering 2025-29 — was launched on 18 November 2025, the first day of WHO's World AMR Awareness Week, revising the original 2017-2021 plan. The Health Ministry's own framing of what changed: NAP-AMR 2.0 "addresses the gaps identified in the first NAP-AMR by increasing the ownership of AMR-related efforts, strengthening inter-sectoral coordination and ensuring stronger engagement with the private sector," backed by ministry-specific action plans with budgets and timelines across more than 20 concerned ministries and departments.

Sources: PIB, IHIP launch release (5 Apr 2021); NCDC "Mandate"/institutional history pages (ncdc.mohfw.gov.in); PIB, NAP-AMR 2.0 launch release (18 Nov 2025).

9. Global health governance — IHR 2005 and the WHO Pandemic Agreement

Two separate, complementary instruments of global pandemic governance were both updated in 2024-25, and the exam-relevant distinction is that they are different legal tracks. The International Health Regulations (2005) were amended at the Seventy-seventh World Health Assembly on 1 June 2024 (resolution WHA77.17, adopted by consensus), entering into force 19 September 2025 for most States Parties (24 months later, 19 September 2026, for the four States that had rejected an earlier 2022 amendment round). The single most significant substantive change: the amendments "introduced a 'pandemic emergency,' which constitutes the highest level of global alert that the Director-General may issue" — a new alert tier sitting above the existing Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC), triggered when a PHEIC also carries a high risk of wide geographic spread, of overwhelming health-system capacity, and of substantial social/economic disruption, and requires "rapid, equitable and enhanced coordinated international action." WHO is explicit that this new power remains non-binding: "WHO will have no ability to impose any health measure, including lockdowns or other restrictions."

Separately, the WHO Pandemic Agreement was adopted by consensus at the Seventy-eighth World Health Assembly on 20 May 2025 — "the second international legal agreement negotiated under Article 19 of the WHO Constitution," after the Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (2003). It sets out cooperative "principles, approaches and tools" for pandemic prevention, preparedness and response, including commitments toward equitable access to vaccines, therapeutics and diagnostics, while explicitly denying WHO or its Director-General any power "to direct, order, alter or otherwise prescribe" national law or to "mandate or otherwise impose" lockdowns or vaccination requirements. Its most consequential unfinished business, exactly as the topic inventory frames it, is the Pathogen Access and Benefit-Sharing (PABS) system: this annex was not finalised at adoption and was instead sent to an Intergovernmental Working Group, with its outcome originally due to be considered at the following year's Assembly (WHA79, May 2026) — only once the PABS annex is itself adopted does the Agreement open for signature and, after 60 ratifications, enter into force.

Audit update (second pass). The PABS annex was not resolved at WHA79 as this chapter's first pass anticipated. Per WHO's own 25 September 2026 statements, negotiations remain unfinished and the target has slipped to WHA80 (May 2027). 10. COVID-19 — India's response, and its legal architecture

India fielded four principal domestically-relevant vaccines during its national drive, which began 16 January 2021: Covishield (Serum Institute of India's licensed version of the Oxford-AstraZeneca vaccine); Covaxin (Bharat Biotech, with ICMR and the National Institute of Virology, granted interim approval before completing Phase 3 trials — recipients had to sign a consent form); ZyCoV-D (Zydus Cadila), "the world's first DNA plasmid-based COVID-19 vaccine," administered needle-free; and Corbevax (Biological E), India's first protein-subunit COVID vaccine. Registration and digital certification ran through CoWIN ("Winning over COVID-19"). Separately, the Department of Biotechnology's Mission COVID Suraksha (₹900 crore, under the Atmanirbhar Bharat 3.0 package) funded four indigenous vaccines specifically — ZyCoV-D, Corbevax, GEMCOVAC-19 (world's first mRNA vaccine outside the mRNA-1273/BNT162b2 pair) and iNCOVACC (world's first intranasal COVID-19 vaccine) — and does not, per its own PIB release, count Covishield among its outputs, since Covishield was an imported-technology license rather than a Mission-funded indigenous development. India's international vaccine-sharing initiative was branded Vaccine Maitri.

Two distinct laws underpinned India's legal response, and conflating them is a common exam trap. The colonial-era Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897 (enacted to fight bubonic plague in Bombay) was invoked from 11 March 2020, when the Cabinet Secretary directed all states/UTs to use its Section 2 powers; it was separately amended on 22 April 2020 (as an Ordinance, later an Act) to add specific protection for healthcare workers, making attacks on them a cognizable, non-bailable offence punishable by up to seven years' imprisonment, plus double the market value of any damaged property as compensation. The actual legal instrument behind the nationwide lockdown declared on 24 March 2020, however, was the Disaster Management Act, 2005 — the order was issued by the National Disaster Management Authority under Section 6(2)(i) of that Act, with the Home Secretary (as Chairperson, National Executive Committee) issuing implementation guidelines under Section 10(2)(l), and penal provisions for non-compliance drawn from Sections 51-60 of the same Act (up to two years' imprisonment).

Trap. The Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897 is the law protecting healthcare workers and enabling disease-control regulations at the state level; the Disaster Management Act, 2005 is the law that actually authorised and enforced the nationwide lockdown itself, through the National Disaster Management Authority. Both were invoked during COVID-19, but they are not interchangeable, and a question about "the legal basis for the lockdown" specifically wants the 2005 Act.

The pandemic also exposed concrete gaps that remain part of India's preparedness discussion: oxygen supply and distribution (most acutely during the April-May 2021 second wave), ICU bed capacity, the lag in real-time disease surveillance that IHIP (§8) was built to close, and pharmaceutical/medical-supply-chain resilience more broadly — themes that connect directly back to why the National One Health Mission, the revised IHR pandemic-emergency tier, and the WHO Pandemic Agreement (§9) were all pursued in the years that followed.

Sources: Wikipedia, "COVID-19 vaccination in India" (used for structured vaccine-rollout facts, cross-checked against PIB); PIB, Mission COVID Suraksha release (27 Jan 2023); Wikipedia, "Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897"; iPleaders, "Disaster Management Act 2005 with respect to COVID-19" (secondary legal-analysis source, flagged as such).

11. Air pollution as a disease burden

Air pollution is not merely an environmental issue in the exam syllabus's separate sense — it is one of India's single largest categories of disease burden, and the two major evidence bases on it (the GBD India state-level study and the more recent Lancet Countdown series) are worth distinguishing carefully, since they cover different study years and are easy to mix up. The Global Burden of Disease India study, published in The Lancet Planetary Health, found that in 2017, "1·24 million (1·09–1·39) deaths in India... were attributable to air pollution," split into "0·67 million (0·55–0·79) from ambient particulate matter pollution and 0·48 million (0·39–0·58) from household air pollution." This represented 12.5% of all deaths in India that year, and 38.7 million DALYs (8.1% of India's total DALYs). Crucially for the exam's framing of NCDs as a "lifestyle" issue, more than half of these deaths were not among the elderly: "51.4%... of the deaths attributable to air pollution... were in people younger than 70 years." The study also converts the burden into a life-expectancy figure that is unusually concrete: "If the air pollution level in India were less than the minimum causing health loss, the average life expectancy in 2017 would have been higher by 1·7 years (1·6–1·9)."

State-level variation

Air pollution's health toll is sharply uneven across India, and the pattern the same study found runs counter to the assumption that this is purely a "Delhi problem": the worst-affected states by life-expectancy loss were Rajasthan (2·5 years lost), Uttar Pradesh (2·2 years) and Haryana (2·1 years) — a north-Indian belt where ambient particulate levels are highest.

The more recent picture — 2022 data

The Lancet Countdown on Health and Climate Change's India-specific data sheet, released October 2025, updates the picture to 2022 and shows the burden still rising: "There were over 1,718,000 deaths attributable to anthropogenic air pollution (PM₂.₅) in 2022 in India, an increase of 38% since 2010." Of these, fossil-fuel combustion specifically accounted for "752,000 (44%) of these deaths," with coal alone responsible for 394,000 deaths, "primarily from its use in power plants (298,000 deaths)." The same data sheet monetises the toll: "The monetised value of premature mortality due to outdoor air pollution in India amounted to US$339.4 billion... 9.5% of gross domestic product" in 2022 — and separately, household air pollution from polluting cooking fuels was "associated with 113 deaths per 100,000" that year.

Trap. Do not quote the 2017 GBD India study's 1.24 million figure and the 2025 Lancet Countdown's 1.718 million figure as if they were the same number restated — they are two different studies, for two different years (2017 vs 2022), using related but not identical methodologies. If a question specifies a year or names a source, answer with that source's own figure rather than whichever number comes to mind first.

Sources: Pandey et al., "Health and economic impact of air pollution in the states of India," The Lancet Planetary Health (2019), reporting 2017 GBD India estimates; Lancet Countdown on Health and Climate Change, "India — 2025 Data Sheet" (lancetcountdown.org, Oct 2025).

12. How far apart India's states really are — deepening the disease-burden picture (extends §1)

Section 1 above establishes the national-level epidemiological transition; the ICMR/PHFI/IHME India State-Level Disease Burden Initiative's own November 2017 press release quantifies just how large the gap between India's best- and worst-performing states remains, and this state-variation angle is a frequent source of exam questions in its own right. On life expectancy, the release states that between 1990 and 2016 national female life expectancy at birth rose "from 59.7 years... to 70.3 years," and male life expectancy "from 58.3 years to 66.9 years" — but behind that average sat "a range of 66.8 years in Uttar Pradesh to 78.7 years in Kerala for females, and 63.6 years in Assam to 73.8 years in Kerala for males in 2016," a gap of roughly 12 years for women and 10 years for men between the best- and worst-placed major states.

The same pattern holds for overall disease burden: "there was an almost two-fold difference in this disease burden rate between the states in 2016, with Assam, Uttar Pradesh, and Chhattisgarh having the highest rates, and Kerala and Goa the lowest rates." Under-5 mortality shows an even starker spread: "a four-fold difference in this rate between the highest in Assam and Uttar Pradesh as compared with the lowest in Kerala in 2016." Disease-specific gaps were wider still — the release records "nine-fold" variation for diarrhoeal disease, "seven-fold" for lower respiratory infections, and "nine-fold" for tuberculosis between India's highest- and lowest-burden states in 2016. The Lancet paper underlying this release adds a summary figure for the syllabus's purposes: of India's ten leading causes of disease burden in 2016, "five causes had at least a five-times difference between the highest and lowest state-specific DALY rates for individual causes."

Trap. Kerala and Goa repeatedly appear at the "good" end of these state comparisons (lowest disease burden, highest life expectancy), while Uttar Pradesh, Assam and Chhattisgarh repeatedly appear at the "high burden" end — but the exact multiple differs by indicator (roughly 2-fold for overall disease burden, 4-fold for under-5 mortality, up to 9-fold for specific diseases like TB and diarrhoeal disease). Don't answer a question asking for "the" gap between states with a single number without checking which indicator is being asked about.

Source: ICMR/PHFI/IHME, "The First Comprehensive Estimates and Trends of Disease Burden in the States of India" (press release, 14 Nov 2017), and Dandona et al., "Nations within a nation: variations in epidemiological transition across the states of India," The Lancet (2017).

13. Mental health — the economic burden and the treatment gap (extends §2)

Section 2 above covers the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 and the NMHP as instruments; the underlying scale of the problem they were built to address is itself a distinct data point worth knowing. The National Mental Health Survey (NMHS) 2015-16, conducted by NIMHANS with WHO backing, found current mental-disorder prevalence in India (excluding tobacco use) at 10.6%, with lifetime prevalence at 13.7% — implying, in the survey's own words, that "nearly 150 million Indians are in need of active interventions." Within this, current depression affected 2.7% ("1 in 40") and lifetime depression 5.2% ("1 in 20"); substance use disorders affected 22.4% of the population above 18; and 0.9% of the population showed high suicidal risk.

The treatment gap the survey found is severe and, importantly, varies sharply by disorder rather than being a single number: the NMHS reported gaps of 86.3% for alcohol use disorders, 85.2% for major depressive disorder, 83.2% for neurosis, 75.5% for psychotic disorders and 70.4% for bipolar affective disorder — summarised in the survey's own words as ranging "from 28% to 83% for mental disorders and 86% for alcohol use disorders." Out-of-pocket cost compounds the access problem: the survey found "families had to spend nearly INR 1000-1500 a month mainly for treatment and travel to access care."

A more recent Health Ministry document, "Advancing Mental Healthcare in India" (PIB, Feb 2025), restates the burden using different framings worth knowing for the exam: it puts India's mental-health disease burden at "2443 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) per 10000 population," the age-adjusted suicide rate at "21.1 per 100000 population," and — using a single blanket figure rather than the NMHS's disorder-specific range — states that "70% to 92% of people with mental disorders do not receive proper treatment." The same document quantifies India's workforce shortfall precisely: "India has 0.75 psychiatrists per 100,000 people, whereas WHO recommends at least 3 per 100,000" — a fifth of the WHO benchmark. It also cites a global economic-loss estimate for mental health conditions specifically applied to India's context: "USD 1.03 trillion" in cumulative economic loss over the period 2012-2030.

Trap. Two different official-adjacent sources give two different "treatment gap" figures — NMHS 2015-16's disorder-specific range (28% to 86%, depending on the disorder) versus the 2025 PIB document's single blanket figure (70% to 92% "of people with mental disorders"). These are not contradictory so much as differently scoped; don't average them into one number, and if a question names a specific disorder (e.g., "the treatment gap for alcohol use disorders"), answer with the NMHS disorder-specific figure, not the blanket one.

Sources: NIMHANS/WHO, "National Mental Health Survey of India, 2015-16: Summary"; PIB, "Advancing Mental Healthcare in India" (Feb 2025).

14. Antimicrobial resistance — the scale of the disease burden itself (extends §8)

Section 8 above covers NAP-AMR 2.0 as a policy response; the disease-burden figures that make AMR a national-security-adjacent health priority in the first place come from the Global Research on Antimicrobial Resistance (GRAM) project, a University of Oxford-IHME collaboration. Its foundational paper, published in The Lancet on 20 January 2022 ("Global burden of bacterial antimicrobial resistance in 2019: a systematic analysis"), estimated that globally in 2019, "an estimated 1.27 million deaths worldwide" were directly attributable to bacterial AMR, while a much larger "estimated 4.95 million deaths" were associated with (i.e., occurred alongside, though not necessarily solely caused by) a drug-resistant infection. For South Asia specifically — the region India dominates by population — the same study found "deaths caused directly by AMR were estimated at 22 deaths per 100,000 population," while "AMR was associated with 77 deaths per 100,000" — among the highest regional rates in the study, reflecting both high infection burden and high resistance prevalence in the region's bacterial isolates.

Trap. "Attributable" (or "directly caused by") AMR deaths and "associated with" AMR deaths are not the same statistic, and the second is always larger than the first — for South Asia, 22 per 100,000 versus 77 per 100,000, roughly a 3.5-fold gap. An exam question asking for "deaths due to AMR" is ambiguous unless it specifies which of the two counterfactual definitions it means; when in doubt, note both.
Widely-reported India-specific absolute figures for 2019 — commonly cited as roughly 2.1 lakh deaths directly attributable to bacterial AMR and roughly 6.9 lakh deaths associated with the five leading resistant pathogens, plus a much larger figure (around 29.9 lakh) for deaths from or with sepsis in which AMR-linked organisms played a role — are reported by multiple Indian news outlets (theprint.in, gulfnews.com) as drawn from the GRAM project's country-level estimates. This chapter could not independently verify these specific India figures against a primary GRAM/IHME document in this pass: the IHME's own India-specific country brief (healthdata.org/sites/default/files/2023-09/India.pdf) returned an access error in every attempt. Treat the India absolute numbers above as secondary-sourced and directionally indicative rather than as confirmed, citable statistics, pending a primary source.

Sources: Murray et al., "Global burden of bacterial antimicrobial resistance in 2019: a systematic analysis," The Lancet (20 Jan 2022); IHME, press release on the same study (healthdata.org).

15. Climate change and health — India's evidence base (connects §1, §4 and Chapter 1)

India's institutional response to climate-sensitive health risk runs through the National Centre for Disease Control's Climate Change and Human Health programme. Per NCDC's own programme page, a National Action Plan for Climate Change and Human Health (NAPCCHH) "developed in 2019 and revised in 2021... outline[s] health adaptation strategies against climate-sensitive illnesses," with a dedicated National Action Plan on Heat-Related Illnesses launched in 2021. Surveillance for heat-related illness and death has run "on the digital platform (IHIP)" — the same platform covered in §8 — "since 2023," and every state and union territory has since developed its own State Action Plan for Climate Change and Human Health (SAPCCHH), tailored to local risk profiles (heatwaves, vector-borne disease, extreme weather, air pollution).

The measured scale of the risk — 2025 Lancet Countdown data

The same India-specific Lancet Countdown data sheet cited in §11 quantifies the climate-health link directly. On heat: "people in India were exposed to 19.8 heatwave days each" on average in 2024, of which "6.6 days... would not have been expected to occur without climate change." Compared with the 1990s baseline, Indians faced "366 more hours during which ambient heat would have posed a moderate or higher risk." The economic translation is large: heat-driven "potential income lost from labour capacity reduction... was US$194 billion in 2024," against "247 billion potential labour hours" lost per year — "124% more than in 1990-1999."

On vector-borne disease — directly relevant to §4's coverage of dengue and the NVBDCP — the data sheet finds climate change is measurably expanding transmission potential: "The average R₀ for dengue transmission by Aedes albopictus mosquitoes increased from 0.86 to 1.60 in 2015-2024, crossing from less than to more than 1," meaning the mosquito vector's climatic suitability for sustaining dengue transmission (an R₀ above 1 implies a single infection can, on average, generate more than one further infection) has newly crossed the epidemic threshold across the period studied. Coastal water-borne disease risk is rising too: "the total coastal area environmentally suitable for Vibrio transmission was 46% greater in 2024, compared with the 1982-2010 baseline." Separately, on direct climate exposure rather than disease per se, "over 18 million people were living less than 1m above sea level in India" in 2024, a population directly exposed to future sea-level-rise risk.

Trap. The NAPCCHH (2019, revised 2021) and the National Action Plan on Heat-Related Illnesses (2021) are related but distinct instruments — the former is the umbrella climate-health plan covering heat, vector-borne disease, extreme weather and air pollution together; the latter is the heat-specific sub-plan under it. Don't use the two names interchangeably in an answer that asks for one specifically.

Sources: NCDC, "Centre for Environmental and Occupational Health" programme page (ncdc.mohfw.gov.in); Lancet Countdown on Health and Climate Change, "India — 2025 Data Sheet" (Oct 2025).

16. The nutrition transition — India's double burden of malnutrition

Section 3 above covers child anthropometric indicators; a parallel and increasingly exam-relevant trend concerns adults. India's own health ministry now names this explicitly as a distinct policy problem: the NFHS-6 (2023-24) release itself flags "emerging challenges such as rising non-communicable diseases, lifestyle-related risks and the dual burden of undernutrition and rising overweight/obesity among adults" — undernutrition among children and adolescents persisting alongside a rising tide of adult overweight and obesity, sometimes within the very same households. Two independent secondary sources that reviewed the NFHS-6 microdata (dataful.in and The Print) converge on identical figures for the scale of this shift: adult overweight/obesity rose from 24.0% to 30.7% among women and from 22.9% to 27.3% among men between NFHS-5 (2019-21) and NFHS-6 (2023-24), with women showing "both a higher prevalence and a steeper increase." The same two sources converge on identical high-blood-sugar figures as well: 17.8% of women and 20.9% of men in NFHS-6 had high/very-high blood sugar or were on medication for it, up from roughly 13-16% in NFHS-5.

The exact overweight/obesity and blood-sugar percentages above could not be independently confirmed against a primary IIPS/MoHFW report page or table in this pass — the PIB release fetched directly for this chapter states the qualitative "dual burden" finding in the quoted language above but does not itself publish the underlying percentages. The figures are reported here because two independent secondary sources, reviewing what they describe as the same NFHS-6 dataset, converge on identical numbers to one decimal place; treat them as well-corroborated but not primary-confirmed.

The clinical/metabolic counterpart of this shift is diabetes. The ICMR-INDIAB study, India's first nationally representative, all-state-and-UT cross-sectional diabetes survey, is cited directly by the Health Ministry in a Parliament reply: "the prevalence of diabetes is 10.1 crores" (101 million) people nationally. The government's programmatic response runs through the NP-NCD infrastructure already described in §2 — 724 District NCD Clinics, 210 District Cardiac Care Units, 326 District Day Care Centres and 6,110 Community Health Centre NCD Clinics — with population-based screening for diabetes, hypertension and common cancers delivered through Ayushman Bharat Health & Wellness Centres for those aged 30 and above, and free insulin and other essential NCD medicines available through the Free Drugs Service Initiative and the Pradhan Mantri Bhartiya Janaushadhi Pariyojana.

Trap. India's public-health literature uses "double/dual burden" in two different, easily confused senses. Section 1 above uses it for the coexistence of communicable and non-communicable disease categories at the population level. This section uses the same phrase for the coexistence of undernutrition and overnutrition — often called the "double burden of malnutrition" specifically, a narrower, nutrition-specific usage that can appear even within a single household (an undernourished child and an overweight parent). A question that says "double burden" without further qualification should be read for which of the two senses it is testing, from context.

Sources: PIB, "Union Health Ministry Releases National Family Health Survey – 6" (29 May 2026); dataful.in, "Obesity and High Blood Sugar Rise Across India, NFHS-6 Shows"; The Print, "Gaining weight, losing health..."; PIB, "Update on treatment of Diabetes" (Rajya Sabha reply, citing ICMR-INDIAB study, 2023).

17. Occupational health — silicosis and the 2024 Supreme Court intervention

Silicosis — in the Supreme Court of India's own words, "an incurable occupational lung disease caused by prolonged inhalation of silica dust" — is India's most exam-relevant occupational disease, concentrated among workers in mining, stone-cutting/quarrying, construction, sandblasting and Gujarat's agate industry, and manifesting in chronic, accelerated and acute forms depending on exposure intensity. Unlike TB, malaria or the NCDs covered earlier in this chapter, silicosis has no single national prevalence survey or centralised surveillance system: a 2025 scoping review of the peer-reviewed literature found "there is no single paper reporting the overall prevalence in India," and its own pooled meta-analysis of eleven available prevalence studies — nine of which came from only two states, Gujarat and Rajasthan — yielded a pooled prevalence of 25.98% among the specific worker populations those studies sampled, explicitly not a national figure. The same review projects that by 2025 India will account for "approximately 25% of the global working population," with "about 92% engaged in the informal sector," where most silica-dust-exposed occupations sit outside routine labour-inspection coverage.

The 2024 Supreme Court judgment

The single most significant recent development is judicial rather than administrative. In People's Rights and Social Research Centre (PRASAR) & Ors. v. Union of India & Ors. (2024 INSC 582; Writ Petition (Civil) No. 110 of 2006), decided on 6 August 2024, the Supreme Court held that the state's failure to prevent and manage silicosis violates the right to life with dignity under Article 21, and separately invoked the Directive Principles under Articles 39(e) and 42. The judgment converted what had been discretionary, state-by-state relief schemes into enforceable national obligations: it directed the constitution of a national-level coordination committee for detection and management of the disease; directed timely compensation and rehabilitation to be administered jointly by state chief secretaries and the Employees' State Insurance Corporation; placed compensation-delivery oversight with the National Human Rights Commission; and authorised the National Green Tribunal to ensure environmental/workplace-safety compliance. The Court's own case record cites illustrative, not national, casualty figures — for example 238 silicosis deaths in Godhra, Gujarat, and 334 silicosis-affected individuals identified in Madhya Pradesh — underscoring the same absence of aggregated national data flagged by the scoping review above.

A figure of "over 52 million workers currently... exposed" to silica dust nationally (up from 11.5 million in 2015-16) is widely repeated in recent journalism covering the PRASAR judgment (e.g. Human Rights Journal, September 2025). This chapter could not trace that figure to a primary ICMR/NIOH/DGMS document in this pass — the Supreme Court judgment itself, fetched directly, does not state a comparable national exposure total — so it is flagged here as an unconfirmed secondary figure rather than a citable statistic.
Trap. Do not answer a question on "India's National Silicosis Policy" by naming a single central Act or scheme — no primary source checked in this pass confirms one exists at the Union level. What exists is a patchwork of state-level policies (Rajasthan's Pneumoconiosis Policy being the most cited example) plus, since August 2024, Supreme Court-mandated coordination and compensation-oversight duties assigned to existing bodies (NHRC, NGT, state chief secretaries, ESIC) rather than a new dedicated national authority or portal.

Sources: Supreme Court of India, PRASAR v. Union of India, 2024 INSC 582, W.P.(C) No. 110/2006 (6 Aug 2024); "Silicosis prevalence and related issues in India: a scoping review," PMC (2025); Human Rights Journal, "Preventing Silicosis is Now a Human Rights Duty in India" (Sep 2025, secondary, flagged as such above).

18. Water-borne and food-borne disease burden

Diarrhoeal disease is the headline indicator here, and India's own long-run trend is a genuine public-health success story that is easy to understate. A June 2026 ICMR-authored study (Kanungo et al., New Microbes and New Infections) tracking India's diarrhoeal-disease burden from 1990 to 2023 found absolute deaths fell 68.3%, from 1.46 million in 1990 to 0.46 million in 2023, with the age-standardised death rate down 84.5% and total DALYs down 81.3%. The improvement is sharpest exactly where it matters most for child-health indicators covered in §3: neonatal deaths from diarrhoea fell 94.1%, and deaths among children aged 2-4 years fell 98.0% over the same period. The picture is not uniform across India — 2023 crude death rates varied 6.5-fold across regions, with Meghalaya recording the steepest decline (-88.6%) and Kerala the slowest (-42.8%) — echoing the state-variation pattern already documented for TB and other causes in §12. The same study's pathogen breakdown is worth knowing precisely: in 2023, norovirus was the leading cause of diarrhoeal disease across all ages, while among children under five specifically, adenovirus led, followed by rotavirus.

Surveillance — a rising outbreak count, against a falling burden

India's outbreak-level surveillance for water-borne and food-borne disease runs through the same IDSP mechanism covered in §8, using its three-tier "S" (suspected), "P" (presumptive) and "L" (laboratory-confirmed) case classification. NCDC's own published IDSP figures show the number of outbreaks detected nationally rising sharply and fairly steadily: 554 in 2020, 728 in 2021, 1,027 in 2022, 1,862 in 2023, 3,020 in 2024, and 2,285 in 2025. NCDC's own description of what these outbreaks consist of is direct: "the majority of outbreaks reported were of Acute Diarrhoeal Disease (ADD), Food poisoning, Chickenpox, Measles, Dengue, Chikungunya, Malaria & Cholera," with ADD and food poisoning consistently the two largest single categories.

Trap. Do not read the rising IDSP outbreak count (554 → 3,020 across 2020-2024) as evidence that water-/food-borne disease burden in India is worsening — it directly contradicts the Kanungo et al. mortality/DALY trend above, which is falling. The two series measure different things: IDSP's count reflects outbreak-detection capacity (more reporting units, better real-time surveillance following the IHIP upgrade covered in §8), while the GBD-based mortality study reflects total disease burden. Better surveillance finding more (mostly small, non-fatal) outbreaks is consistent with — not contradictory to — a genuinely falling death toll.
This chapter searched for a specific, recent (2024-26) large-scale Indian cholera outbreak to name as a case study, as the topic inventory requests. The only detailed outbreak account located in this pass (Odisha/Kerala, Rayagada and Malappuram districts) turned out on direct verification to be dated July 2016, not a recent event, despite appearing in a current search — it is not used above for that reason. No confirmed 2024-26 India-specific cholera outbreak was located from a primary source in this pass; readers should treat "cholera" in the IDSP outbreak-category list above as an ongoing background category rather than evidence of one recent named outbreak.

Sources: Kanungo et al., "Trends and burden of diarrhoeal diseases across India, 1990-2023," New Microbes and New Infections, Vol. 71 (Jun 2026); NCDC, "IDSP Key Activities & Achievements" (ncdc.mohfw.gov.in).

19. Road traffic injuries as a disease-burden category

Road traffic injuries belong in a disease-burden chapter because global-health accounting treats them exactly like a disease category with its own GBD cause code, and India's trend runs contrary to the rest of the world. The WHO Global Status Report on Road Safety 2023 found global road deaths fell roughly 5% in the period it covers, while, per reporting on the same report, India's fatalities rose 15%, from 1.34 lakh in 2010 to 1.54 lakh in 2021 — moving in the opposite direction from the global trend during a period when India, as a signatory to the 2015 Brasilia Declaration, had committed to SDG 3.6: halving road traffic deaths by 2030. The legal response — the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019

India's principal legislative response is the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019, which entered into force from 1 September 2019 and, per the Ministry's own framing, "provides for strict penalties for ensuring compliance and enhancing" traffic-rule enforcement through technology. Its substantive changes include steep hikes in penalties for traffic violations backed by electronic/camera-based monitoring; computerisation and automation of vehicle-fitness and driving tests; a defective-vehicle recall power; and a streamlined third-party insurance and compensation regime, under which hit-and-run compensation now stands at ₹2,00,000 for death and ₹50,000 for grievous injury. It sits alongside a wider basket of engineering and enforcement measures the Ministry has rolled out since — mandatory front-passenger airbags, seat-belt reminders for driver and co-driver, anti-lock braking systems, speed-limiting devices on transport vehicles, and a reduced four-laning threshold for national highways (from 15,000 to 10,000 passenger-car-units) meant to reduce head-on and overtaking-related fatalities specifically.

Trap. The steep one-year fall in accidents and deaths between 2019 and 2020 in the table above (roughly 18% and 13% respectively) is not evidence that the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019 was dramatically effective — 2020 was the year of India's COVID-19 lockdowns (see §10), which suppressed traffic volumes nationally for months. The more telling comparison is 2019 → 2022: deaths rose from 1,58,984 to 1,68,491 despite the Act being in force throughout, which is why India's road-safety trajectory is described as off-track for the SDG 3.6 target of halving deaths by 2030, not on track because of the 2019 law.

21. Primary healthcare delivery architecture — the National Health Mission and ASHA (extends §1, §2, §16)

Every programme named earlier in this chapter — NPCDCS screening (§2), Anaemia Mukt Bharat (§3), AB-HWC-based diabetes/hypertension screening (§16) — is delivered through a specific institutional pyramid that the chapter has so far assumed rather than stated, and that pyramid is itself a frequent, precise subject of PYQs. The National Rural Health Mission (NRHM), launched 2005-06, was subsumed in 2013 into the broader National Health Mission (NHM) when the parallel National Urban Health Mission (NUHM) was added, giving NHM two sub-missions covering rural and urban India respectively. The rural facility tiers below the district hospital carry official population norms that are frequently tested and frequently misquoted: a Sub (Health) Centre is normed for 5,000 people on the plains and 3,000 in hilly/tribal/desert areas; a Primary Health Centre (PHC) for 30,000 on the plains and 20,000 in hilly/tribal/desert areas; and a Community Health Centre (CHC) — the first tier with specialist care — for 1,20,000 on the plains and 80,000 in hilly/tribal/desert areas. The lower figure in each pair is not a rounding convenience: it is a deliberate policy adjustment for terrain and population dispersal, and a question that quotes only the plains figure for a hill-state scenario is testing exactly this distinction.

ASHA — scope exactly as UPSC has tested it

The Accredited Social Health Activist (ASHA) scheme began with NRHM in 2005-06 for rural India and was extended to urban India with NUHM from 2013. An ASHA is normed at one per 1,000 population in rural areas (one per 2,000 in urban areas), relaxed to one per habitation in tribal, hilly and desert areas where population is too dispersed for a strict ratio to work; she is selected by the local community/Gram Sabha, must be a literate, married/widowed/divorced woman resident of the village she serves, preferably aged 25-45. As of June 2022, PIB put the national total at over 10.52 lakh ASHAs across all states/UTs except Goa — by most official framings, the world's largest community health worker programme. Her duties are precisely bounded, and a well-attested UPSC question tests exactly where that boundary falls: she accompanies women to the health facility for antenatal-care checkups, uses pregnancy-test kits for early detection of pregnancy, and provides information on nutrition and immunisation — but she does not conduct the actual delivery of a baby, which remains the job of a skilled birth attendant (typically the ANM, Auxiliary Nurse Midwife, stationed at the Sub-Centre) or a facility-based team.

22. Vector-borne disease transmission nuances — Zika virus (extends §4, §7)

Section 4 and §7 cover NVBDCP's six diseases and zoonotic spillover generally; Zika virus disease sits adjacent to both — it is Aedes-borne like dengue and chikungunya, but it is also the one vector-borne disease in this chapter's scope with a confirmed non-vector transmission route, which is precisely what a well-attested PYQ tests. WHO's own fact sheet states Zika is transmitted "primarily... through the bite of an infected Aedes mosquito, mainly Aedes aegypti but also Aedes albopictus; these species also transmit dengue, chikungunya and urban yellow fever" — the same vector species this chapter already names for dengue and chikungunya in §4. Critically, WHO adds that "Zika virus is also transmitted from mother to fetus during pregnancy, as well as through sexual contact, laboratory exposure, transfusion of blood and blood products" — making it, among this chapter's named vector-borne diseases, the one with a confirmed sexual-transmission route. The principal health risk is not acute severity in the infected adult (symptoms are typically mild) but downstream harm: WHO states "Zika virus infection during pregnancy is a cause of microcephaly and other congenital malformations in the infant," and separately "is associated with Guillain-Barré syndrome, neuropathy and myelitis in adults and children."

India has recorded confirmed Zika transmission on more than one occasion: a 2018 outbreak centred on Rajasthan (Jaipur), and — per WHO's own Disease Outbreak News reporting — a 2021 cluster spanning Kerala (Thiruvananthapuram district, first confirmed case 8 July 2021 in a pregnant woman, with a reported 70 positive RT-PCR results, 11.9% of samples tested, including four more pregnant women; smaller case numbers in Ernakulam and Kottayam) and Maharashtra (Pune district, Belsar village, first confirmed case 31 July 2021). Government figures reported separately put India's case count at 13 confirmed cases nationally through mid-2024, including a cluster in Pune.

Trap (official previous paper). UPSC Prelims 2017: "Consider the following statements: 1. In tropical regions, Zika virus disease is transmitted by the same mosquito that transmits dengue. 2. Sexual transmission of Zika virus disease is possible. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?" The correct answer is both 1 and 2 — both statements are true, which is itself the trap, since a test-taker primed to expect "not all vector-borne disease facts are symmetrical" (a reasonable instinct, given this chapter's own trap notes elsewhere) may wrongly eliminate one of the two correct statements looking for an asymmetry that, here, does not exist. Separately, UPSC Prelims 2013, Q.96 tested the opposite distinction on tattoo-transmissible diseases: of chikungunya, Hepatitis B and HIV-AIDS, only the latter two (both blood-borne) are tattoo-transmissible, not chikungunya (purely mosquito-borne, with no known blood-contact transmission route) — a useful pairing with this section's Zika facts, since both questions test whether a student can correctly separate a vector-borne disease's transmission route from a blood-/body-fluid-borne one.

23. Antimicrobial resistance — the regulatory and surveillance mechanisms (extends §8, §14)

Sections 8 and 14 cover NAP-AMR 2.0 as policy and the GRAM project's mortality burden; a 2014 Mains question asks specifically for something neither section names directly — the actual "available mechanisms for monitoring and control" of antibiotic misuse. Three concrete, named mechanisms answer that question. First, the regulatory lever: antibiotics are controlled as prescription-only drugs under Schedule H and Schedule H1 of the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945 — Schedule H1 (added in 2013) applies to a specified list of higher-risk antibiotics and other habit-forming drugs, requiring the dispensing chemist to retain a copy of the prescription, maintain a separate register recording the prescriber and patient, and not sell the drug without a registered medical practitioner's prescription. Second, the public-awareness lever: the Red Line Campaign, run by the Health Ministry, asks the public not to buy or consume, without a doctor's prescription, any medicine marked with a red vertical line on its packaging — the visual marker Schedule H1 itself mandates.

Two distinct AMR surveillance networks — easy to conflate

Third, and most exam-relevant because it is routinely conflated into one network when it is actually two: India runs two separate, differently-governed AMR surveillance networks. The ICMR Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance and Research Network (AMRSN), launched in 2013, operates through tertiary-care academic medical institutes with a research orientation, aiming (per ICMR's own description) at "strengthening surveillance and rationalizing exploratory research" and generating resistance-mechanism data, with ICMR's own page noting it "plans to scale up... to embrace... the One Health approach." Separately, the National Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network (NARS-Net), coordinated by the NCDC under the National Programme on AMR Containment (launched during the 12th Five-Year Plan, 2012-17), is the public-health surveillance arm: it monitors 9 priority pathogens for defined drug-bug combinations across a network that has grown from 30 government medical college laboratories in 24 states/UTs (as of March 2021) to 60 medical colleges and hospitals nationally (per a PIB release dated 28 March 2025). Budget allocations for the AMR Containment Programme that funds NARS-Net were reported at ₹666.89 lakh (2021-22), ₹777.81 lakh (2022-23) and ₹919 lakh (2023-24).

Trap (official previous paper, plus a chapter-internal distinction). UPSC Mains 2014, GS Paper III, Q.11: "Can overuse and free availability of antibiotics without Doctor's prescription, be contributors to the emergence of drug-resistant diseases in India? 24. Population ageing and the geriatric health burden (extends §1, §2)

Section 1's epidemiological-transition framing describes the shift toward NCDs in aggregate; a 2022 Mains question asks about one specific driver of that shift that this chapter has not yet named directly — rising life expectancy itself creating a distinct, growing health-burden category. India's own Longitudinal Ageing Study of India (LASI) Wave-1 (IIPS, released 2021) found that 12% of India's population is already aged 60 and above, a share the Health Ministry's own October 2025 document projects will reach roughly 230 million (about 15% of the population) by 2036 and 319 million by 2050, growing at an annual rate of "approximately 3%" — faster than the population as a whole. The health-burden data behind this shift is specific and exam-usable: LASI found 34.6% of those aged 60+ report a cardiovascular-disease diagnosis, 32% hypertension and 14.2% diabetes, and — directly naming the multimorbidity concept this chapter's NCD sections (§2, §16) treat only implicitly — 23% of those aged 60+ have been diagnosed with two or more chronic conditions simultaneously (multimorbidity), compared with 13% among the 45-59 age group; regional variation is sharp, multimorbidity reaching 52% among Kerala's elderly against a national 23%. Functional decline compounds the disease burden: LASI recorded 24% of the 60+ population with at least one activities-of-daily-living (ADL) limitation and 48% with at least one instrumental-ADL (IADL) limitation such as managing money or medication independently.

India's institutional response is the National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly (NPHCE), launched in 2010-11, which operates on two tiers: a primary/secondary tier delivered through the same NHM facility pyramid described in §21 (District Hospitals, CHCs, PHCs and Sub-Centre/Health & Wellness Centres, now screening for NCDs at age 30+ under §16's AB-HWC infrastructure), and a dedicated tertiary tier of 19 Regional Geriatric Centres (RGCs) at medical colleges across 18 states plus 2 National Centres of Ageing — one at AIIMS New Delhi, one at Madras Medical College, Chennai.

Trap (official previous paper). UPSC Mains 2022, GS Paper III, 10 marks: "The increase in life expectancy in the country has led to newer health challenges in the community. What are those challenges and what steps need to be taken to meet them?" A strong answer connects three chapter threads explicitly rather than restating §1's epidemiological-transition language generically: the NCD/multimorbidity burden quantified by LASI above, the dedicated NPHCE institutional response (not NPCDCS, which targets NCDs generally rather than the elderly specifically), and the functional/caregiving dimension (ADL/IADL limitations) that a purely disease-counted NCD answer would omit.

25.1 Climate trends (Chapter 1) worsening specific disease burdens, beyond §15

Section 15 above already uses the Lancet Countdown's 2025 India data sheet to link climate change to heatwave days, dengue's rising R₀, and Vibrio-suitable coastal area. Chapter 1 supplies three further, more structurally exam-relevant links that this chapter has not yet drawn. First, Chapter 1 §10's CEEW Climate Vulnerability Index (2021) ranks Andhra Pradesh second nationally for climate vulnerability — immediately after Assam, and ahead of Maharashtra, Karnataka and Bihar — with 59% of India's eastern-coastal-zone districts (the zone AP sits in) facing extreme cyclone vulnerability. For an APPSC Group 2 answer, this is the fact that gives §15's generic statement — "every state... has developed its own SAPCCHH, tailored to local risk profiles" — real stakes: Andhra Pradesh's own State Action Plan for Climate Change and Human Health is not a template exercise but a response to the second-highest climate-vulnerability ranking of any Indian state, covering precisely the heatwave, vector-borne-disease and extreme-weather risk categories CEEW's index flags.

Second, Chapter 1 §11 documents a concrete, named 2025 disaster inside this ranking: Severe Cyclonic Storm Montha made landfall near Kakinada, Andhra Pradesh, on 28 October 2025, with the Ministry of Home Affairs recording ₹6,355.60 crore in total estimated damage and 1.61 lakh hectares of crop loss. A cyclone of this scale is also a disease-burden event in its own right, not only an infrastructure one: post-cyclone flooding and waterlogging create exactly the conditions behind the rising background rate of the outbreak categories NCDC's own IDSP data names in §18 above — "Acute Diarrhoeal Disease (ADD), Food poisoning,... Dengue, Chikungunya, Malaria & Cholera" — meaning Montha's district-level relief effort and the IDSP's three-tier (S/P/L) outbreak surveillance described in §18 are two halves of the same post-disaster public-health response, not separate systems.

Third, Chapter 1 §7's urban heat island findings add an equity dimension this chapter's heat coverage (§15's heat-related-illness action plan) has not stated: UNEP's NDMA-workshop figures put Indian cities "up to 10°C hotter" than their rural surroundings, while only about 9% of Indian households have air conditioning — and the same source notes the urban poor have both the least access to natural cooling (shade, vegetation, water) and the lowest air-conditioning penetration of any group. This is precisely the population the National Action Plan on Heat-Related Illnesses (launched 2021, §15) and IHIP's heat-surveillance data (running since 2023, §15) most need to reach, and a strong exam answer should name the UHI/AC-penetration equity gap as the reason heat-related illness is not a uniformly distributed risk within a city, even where ambient heatwave-day counts (Lancet Countdown's 19.8 days/year, §15) are the same for everyone in it.

Trap. Don't collapse Chapter 1's climate-vulnerability evidence into one undifferentiated "AP is climate-vulnerable" line. CEEW's CVI rank (2nd nationally) is a composite, multi-hazard index; Cyclone Montha is a single dated extreme-weather event within that broader ranking; and the urban heat island effect is a distinct, city-scale mechanism layered on top of both. A question asking specifically about AP's cyclone risk, heat risk, or overall climate vulnerability wants the matching fact, not whichever Chapter 1 statistic comes to mind first.

25.2 Ozone depletion and ocean acidification (Chapter 2) as upstream drivers of disease and nutrition

Chapter 2 §2 establishes that stratospheric ozone "absorbs most of the sun's harmful ultraviolet-B (UV-B) radiation, protecting life on Earth's surface" — a protective function this chapter's NP-NCD cancer-screening coverage (§2) has not yet connected to a disease outcome. WHO's own UV-radiation fact sheet, fetched directly for this section, quantifies that protective function's stakes globally: in 2020, "over 1.5 million cases of skin cancers were diagnosed and over 120,000 skin cancer-associated deaths were reported," split into roughly 1.2 million non-melanoma cases (64,000 deaths) and 325,000 melanomas (57,000 deaths); separately, of the 15 million people WHO estimates are blind from cataracts, "some 10% may be due to exposure to UVR" — roughly 1.5 million cases. The US EPA's own health-effects page for ozone-layer depletion makes the ozone-treaty link to this burden explicit in counterfactual terms: full implementation of the Montreal Protocol (the treaty Chapter 2 §3 covers) "is expected to prevent approximately 443 million cases of skin cancer, 2.3 million skin cancer deaths, and 63 million cases of cataracts" among people born in the United States between 1890 and 2100 — figures for one country alone, illustrating the scale of a single ozone-protection treaty's disease-prevention effect. Mechanistically, EPA also notes that in Antarctica "the amount of UV-B measured at the surface can double during the annual ozone hole" — connecting Chapter 2's own tracking of the shrinking 2025 Antarctic hole (18.71 million km², "fifth-smallest since 1992," per Chapter 2) directly to a measurable UV-B exposure swing, not merely an atmospheric-science data point.

Framed against this chapter's own NP-NCD coverage (§2), the connection is a genuine gap worth naming explicitly: NP-NCD's screening mandate targets "cardiovascular diseases, cancer, chronic respiratory diseases, diabetes and other NCDs," and the Health & Wellness Centre-based screening model (§2, §16) covers common cancers (oral, breast, cervical) for the population aged 30 and above — but skin cancer, the cancer category most directly shielded by the ozone layer, is not named among NP-NCD's screened cancers in any source this chapter has checked. The Montreal Protocol and Kigali Amendment (Chapter 2 §3) therefore function as an upstream, atmospheric layer of cancer prevention operating ahead of — and largely independent of — India's own domestic NCD-screening infrastructure, which has not yet built a corresponding downstream detection pathway for the specific cancer category ozone protection targets.

Ocean acidification supplies a second, nutrition-side link. Chapter 2's own CMLRE research programme (under the Ministry of Earth Sciences) states that India's coastal waters are expected to acidify faster than the open-ocean baseline, naming "agricultural fertilizers, industrial emissions, domestic and industrial sewages and... upwelling" as compounding local stressors — and the same chapter's Lok Sabha-reply data on the Fourth Global Coral Bleaching Event (2023-24) records Lakshadweep at 84.6% bleaching, the worst-affected Indian reef system, with only partial recovery as of the reply date. Lakshadweep's own Fisheries Department states plainly that "a huge part of the population in Lakshadweep depends on the fishery resources which is also the main livelihood of the inhabitants of the islands" — meaning reef degradation of this scale bears directly on the protein and micronutrient base that this chapter's own anaemia and double-burden-of-malnutrition sections (§3's Anaemia Mukt Bharat, §16's nutrition transition) implicitly assume continues to exist for India's reef-dependent coastal and island populations, a coastal-nutrition vulnerability neither §3 nor §16 currently names.

Ocean acidification's chemical mechanism (falling pH lowering carbonate-ion availability, impairing shell/skeleton-building organisms) is established in Chapter 2 §4 for corals specifically, via IPCC AR6 and CMLRE; the same mechanism is understood generally to extend to other calcifying fishery species (molluscs, some crustacean larval stages), but a quantified India-specific fishery-catch decline attributable to acidification specifically — as distinct from the thermal-stress-driven coral bleaching figures cited above — was not located in either chapter's research passes and is not asserted here. Similarly, no primary WHO or EPA source fetched for this pass gave a single "X% ozone loss equals Y% more skin cancer" dose-response figure; the global/US burden-and-prevention totals quoted above are the best-confirmed, directly-fetched substitute.

25.3 How this chapter's disease burdens are shaping Chapter 6's health-system design

Chapter 6 §15 describes Health Technology Assessment in India (HTAIn)'s role as "evidence-based gatekeeping for where public health rupees go," and its own "Completed HTA Studies" listing reads almost as a direct index of this chapter's disease-burden sections. HPV DNA testing for cervical-cancer screening and, in 2026, HPV vaccination for India's immunisation programme map onto this chapter's NP-NCD cancer mandate (§2); COPD screening at Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (2024) maps onto the 1.24-million-death (2017, GBD India) and 1.718-million-death (2022, Lancet Countdown) air-pollution disease burden this chapter documents in §11; influenza vaccination among the elderly (2024) and Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement cost-effectiveness (2024) map onto the population-ageing multimorbidity burden this chapter's §24 quantifies via LASI (34.6% of those 60+ reporting a cardiovascular diagnosis, 23% with two or more chronic conditions); and typhoid conjugate vaccination (2026) maps onto the water- and food-borne disease burden this chapter's §18 tracks through the Kanungo et al. diarrhoeal-disease study and IDSP's own "Acute Diarrhoeal Disease, Food poisoning... & Cholera" outbreak categories. HTAIn's own prioritisation list is, in effect, this chapter's disease-burden data converted into a health-technology procurement agenda.

Insurance-coverage design shows the same mechanical link on the financing side. This chapter's §1 records that NCDs' share of India's DALYs rose from 30% (1990) to 55% (2016), and §16 cites the ICMR-INDIAB study's finding that diabetes alone affects 10.1 crore Indians — the demand-side facts behind both the NP-NCD screening infrastructure §16 itemises (724 District NCD Clinics, 210 District Cardiac Care Units, 6,110 CHC NCD Clinics) and Chapter 6 §11's AB PM-JAY design, which extends ₹5 lakh per family per year in cashless secondary- and tertiary-care cover to roughly 12 crore poorer households specifically because NCD and cancer care — unlike a single acute episode — typically means repeat, long-horizon hospitalisation. Chapter 6 §1's own headline financial-protection indicator, out-of-pocket expenditure falling from 64.2% to 43.4% of total health spending, is being tracked as a proxy for whether India's growing NCD burden is being absorbed by insurance and public financing rather than pushing households into catastrophic spending — the same dynamic this chapter's §13 illustrates at the household level, where NMHS 2015-16 found families spending "nearly INR 1000-1500 a month mainly for treatment and travel" for mental illness alone.

Workforce and infrastructure planning complete the link. Chapter 6 §4 records PM-ABHIM funding five new Regional NCDCs and public-health units at 32 airports, 11 seaports and 7 land border crossings — infrastructure that answers directly to this chapter's own zoonotic-spillover findings in §7 (Kerala's nine Nipah outbreaks since 2018, the confirmed mpox clade IIb/Ib cases, the National One Health Mission's own framing of "inter-compartmental spread of diseases") and to the surveillance-lag gap COVID-19 exposed per §10. Separately, Chapter 6 §3's nursing shortfall (1:670 against a WHO norm of 1:300) and its stalled Public Health Management Cadre reform (§16, still substantially incomplete three years past the 13th Central Council of Health and Family Welfare's 2022 target) sit directly downstream of specific workforce gaps this chapter names rather than merely implies: the AMR surveillance network NARS-Net growing from only 30 to 60 government medical-college laboratories nationally (§23), and the psychiatrist shortage this chapter's §13 quantifies precisely — "India has 0.75 psychiatrists per 100,000 people, whereas WHO recommends at least 3 per 100,000." Both are specific, named instances of the generic workforce-planning gap Chapter 6 documents structurally but without this chapter's underlying disease-burden numbers attached.

Trap. A Mains answer on Chapter 6's health-system reforms (HTAIn, PM-JAY, PM-ABHIM, the PHMC debate) scores poorly if it describes them as generic "strengthening the health system" moves. Each has a specific, nameable disease-burden figure from this chapter driving it — HTAIn's study list tracks §2/§11/§18/§24's disease categories one-for-one; PM-JAY's design responds to §1's 55%-NCD-DALY-share and §16's diabetes prevalence; PM-ABHIM's border health units respond to §7's named zoonotic outbreaks. Naming the specific figure behind the specific reform, rather than asserting the connection generically, is what separates a cross-chapter-linkage answer from a vague "everything is connected" gesture.
