1. National Health Policy 2017 and health financing

The National Health Policy (NHP) 2017 set India's central financing benchmark: then-Health Minister J.P. Nadda's own framing of the commitment was that NHP 2017 "commits to achieving a target of raising public health expenditure to 2.5 percent of the GDP in a time-bound manner by 2025." Actual performance, per the Health Ministry's own tenth National Health Accounts (NHA) report, released in May 2026 for FY 2022-23 (confirmed in this audit pass as still the latest available NHA round — no FY2023-24 NHA estimate has been released as of September 2026), falls well short of that target: Government Health Expenditure (GHE) as a share of GDP stood at 1.43% in 2022-23 — up from 1.15% in 2013-14, but still nearly a full percentage point below the 2.5% goal with the target year already passed. The same release notes that, recalculated under the newer GDP series with base year 2022-23, this figure becomes 1.48% — a base-year revision, not a change in the underlying spending.

The same report shows genuine progress on the equity side. Out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE) — the share of total health spending paid directly by patients at the point of care, and the standard indicator of financial-protection failure — "declined significantly by nearly 21 percentage points, from 64.2% to 43.4%" between 2013-14 and 2022-23 (it dipped further, to 39.4%, during the pandemic-spending peak of 2021-22, before settling back up). Government's own share of total health expenditure correspondingly rose from 28.6% to 43.7% over the same decade, and per-capita government health spending rose "nearly 2.7 times, from Rs. 1,042 to Rs. 2,786."

Trap. Don't conflate the 2.5%-of-GDP target (still unmet, at 1.43%, or 1.48% under the revised GDP base, for 2022-23) with the out-of-pocket-expenditure trend (which has genuinely and substantially improved, from 64.2% to 43.4%). A question about NHP 2017's "success" needs both halves of this picture — spending targets missed, but the equity indicator moving the right way. Separately, a Lok Sabha reply (per the Economic Survey 2023-24, cited 9 Aug 2024) put GHE at 1.9% of GDP in 2023-24 — this is a genuinely different, larger figure from a different methodology (Economic Survey) than the NHA/SHA-2011 series this chapter otherwise uses; don't quote the two figures interchangeably or treat 1.9% as an update to the NHA's 1.43%/1.48%.

2. National Health Mission and the health infrastructure tiers

The National Rural Health Mission (NRHM) was launched on 12 April 2005 to address weak public-health indicators in 18 identified states. On 1 May 2013, the Union Cabinet approved a new, parallel National Urban Health Mission (NUHM) and folded both sub-missions under a single overarching National Health Mission (NHM) — the umbrella structure that has governed India's public-health delivery architecture since.

Facility-level care runs through a standard tier hierarchy — Sub-Centres, Primary Health Centres (PHCs), Community Health Centres (CHCs) and District Hospitals — with Sub-Centres and PHCs upgraded into comprehensive primary-care hubs under Ayushman Bharat. These were originally branded Health and Wellness Centres (HWCs); in November 2023, the Union Health Ministry "communicated [a] decision to all states and Union territories" renaming them Ayushman Arogya Mandir, with the tagline "Arogyam Paramam Dhanam" (health is the supreme wealth).

The benchmark for what each tier must provide — staffing, equipment, infrastructure and services — is set by the Indian Public Health Standards (IPHS), first published in 2007 under NRHM, revised once in 2012, and revised again with a release date of 16 April 2022. The 2022 revision is organised into four separate volumes matched to facility tier: Volume I (Sub District Hospital and District Hospital), Volume II (Community Health Centre), Volume III (Health and Wellness Centre-Primary Health Centre), and Volume IV (Health and Wellness Centre-Sub Health Centre).

As of 30 November 2025, per the Health Ministry's own "Initiatives & Achievements-2025" document, 1,81,873 Ayushman Arogya Mandirs have been operationalised nationally with an expanded package of 12 services and teleconsultation facilities, recording a cumulative footfall of 494.71 crore and 41.93 crore teleconsultations since inception.

3. The frontline health workforce

India's community-level health workforce has a clear division of labour that exam questions frequently test. ASHAs (Accredited Social Health Activists) are unpaid community volunteers — "part-time, trained community health volunteers, who work as an interface between the community and the public health [system]" — normed at one per 1,000 rural population (up to one per 2,500 in urban areas), compensated through task-based monetary incentives rather than a salary. ANMs (Auxiliary Nurse Midwives), by contrast, are trained, salaried paramedical staff stationed at Sub-Centres (norm: two ANMs per Sub-Centre, each Sub-Centre serving roughly 5,000 people), qualified in midwifery and maternal/child health, and holding a supervisory role over both ASHAs and Anganwadi Workers (the third frontline category, who sit under the Ministry of Women and Child Development rather than Health).

On staffing ratios, the government's own December 2022 reply to the Rajya Sabha put India's doctor-population ratio at 1:834 — better than the "WHO norm of 1:1,000" — based on 13.08 lakh allopathic doctors registered with State Medical Councils and the NMC as of June 2022, plus 5.65 lakh AYUSH doctors, and assuming 80% practical availability. Nursing supply lags further behind its own WHO benchmark: the 15th Finance Commission cited "a nurse-to-population ratio of 1:670 against the WHO norm of 1:300."

Trap. India's doctor-population ratio (1:834) is frequently misquoted as worse than the WHO norm; per the government's own December 2022 parliamentary reply it is in fact better than the WHO's 1:1,000 benchmark. It is the nursing ratio (1:670 against a WHO norm of 1:300) that falls well short — don't apply the same "better than WHO norm" framing to both professions.

Sources: IDR, "Who are India's frontline health workers and what do they do?"; ThePrint, "India's doctor-patient ratio better than WHO norm of 1:1000, government tells Rajya Sabha" (20 Dec 2022).

4. PM-ABHIM — the pandemic-response infrastructure mission

The Pradhan Mantri-Ayushman Bharat Health Infrastructure Mission (PM-ABHIM) was announced in the FY 2021-22 Budget speech on 1 February 2021 (initially under the name "Prime Minister Atmanirbhar Swasth Bharat Yojana"), with Cabinet approval following on 15 September 2021, and a total outlay of about ₹64,180 crore over six years (through FY 2025-26). Its stated purpose is "developing capacities of health systems and institutions across the continuum of care at all levels... and preparing health systems in responding effectively to the current and future pandemics/disasters" — a direct policy response to the infrastructure gaps COVID-19 exposed (Chapter 5, §10). Key deliverables include support for 17,788 rural and 11,024 urban Health and Wellness Centres, 3,382 Block Public Health Units, integrated public-health labs in every district, critical-care hospital blocks in every district with population over 5 lakh, five new Regional NCDCs, and public-health units at 32 airports, 11 seaports and 7 land border crossings. PM-ABHIM operates "in addition to the National Health Mission," not as a replacement for it.

Trap. PM-ABHIM is often mis-dated to October 2021 (when some state rollout ceremonies occurred) — its actual origin is the 1 February 2021 Budget announcement, with Cabinet approval on 15 September 2021. Use the Budget-announcement date if a question asks specifically when the scheme was "announced."

5. Outcome indicators — MMR, IMR, U5MR and TFR

India's core maternal and child health outcome indicators come from the Registrar General of India's Sample Registration System (SRS). The Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR), per the latest SRS Special Bulletin (2022-24), has declined to "nearly 87" maternal deaths per lakh live births — continuing a steady fall from 130 (2014-16) to 93 (2019-21) recorded in the previous bulletin round. PIB's own framing: "India has already achieved the National Health Policy target of reducing MMR below 100 and is steadily progressing towards the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target of reducing MMR to below 70 by 2030," with six States — Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Gujarat — having already reached the SDG threshold individually.

For child mortality and fertility, the most recent confirmed SRS figures show: Infant Mortality Rate (IMR) down to 27 per 1,000 live births (2021) from 39 in 2014, falling further to 25 in the 2023 SRS Statistical Report, and to 24 per 1,000 in the SRS Statistical Report 2024 (released 20 May 2026 by the Registrar General of India); Neonatal Mortality Rate at 19 per 1,000 (2021), down from 26 in 2014; Under-Five Mortality Rate (U5MR) at 31 per 1,000 (2021), down from 45 in 2014, and now confirmed at 28 per 1,000 in the SRS Statistical Report 2024 (down from 29 the preceding year); and Total Fertility Rate (TFR), which fell below the replacement level of 2.1 to reach 1.9 in the 2023 SRS report and holds at 1.9 in the 2024 report (having held at 2.0 in 2021, itself down from 2.3 in 2014). The Sex Ratio at Birth improved from 899 (2014) to 917 (2021-23 average, per the 2023 report) to 918 (2022-24 average, per the 2024 report).

6. Drug regulation — CDSCO and NPPA

Two separate bodies regulate India's pharmaceutical sector along different axes. The Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO), headed by the Drugs Controller General of India (DCGI) and functioning under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, is "India's national regulatory body for cosmetics, pharmaceuticals and medical devices" — approving new drugs and clinical trials, setting manufacturing/quality standards, and conducting inspections through zonal offices, advised by the Drug Technical Advisory Board (DTAB) and Drug Consultative Committee (DCC).

The National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA), constituted on 29 August 1997 under the Department of Pharmaceuticals, does something different: it "controls the prices of pharmaceutical drugs in India," implementing and enforcing the Drug Price Control Order (DPCO) — the current version, DPCO 2013, sets price ceilings for roughly 384 scheduled drugs — and setting ceiling prices for medicines on the National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM), alongside monitoring drug availability and shortages nationally.

Trap. CDSCO regulates whether a drug can be sold at all (safety, efficacy, manufacturing standards, clinical trial approval); NPPA regulates what it can be sold for (price ceilings under DPCO/NLEM). A question about drug approval wants CDSCO/DCGI; a question about medicine affordability or price caps wants NPPA/DPCO.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation"; Wikipedia, "National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority."

7. Digital health, transplant coordination and data systems

eSanjeevani, the Health Ministry's national telemedicine service, operates in two forms: eSanjeevani AB-HWC, a doctor-to-doctor hub-and-spoke service run through roughly 50,000 Health and Wellness Centres, and eSanjeevaniOPD, a direct patient-to-doctor service — together they had crossed 3 crore tele-consultations by 25 March 2022 (with a single-day record of 1.7 lakh consultations), built by C-DAC Mohali as a "Make in India" platform, and PIB credits the service with contributing "considerably during COVID-19."

NOTTO (National Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisation), based at Safdarjung Hospital in Delhi under the Directorate General of Health Services, is the apex coordinating body for organ and tissue donation and transplantation, supported by Regional Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisations (ROTTOs) — five per a 2021 parliamentary reply — and State Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisations (SOTTOs) — fourteen per the same 2021 reply, with new SOTTOs for Delhi, Meghalaya, Sikkim, Tripura and Arunachal Pradesh operationalised during 2025-26 per NOTTO's own Annual Report 2025-26, though no updated aggregate ROTTO/SOTTO count was found; the broader National Organ Transplant Programme (NOTP) also funds transplant/retrieval infrastructure, coordinator training, post-transplant immunosuppressant drugs for BPL patients, and a 24x7 helpline.

Programme-level performance data flows through the Health Management Information System (HMIS), the NHM's facility-level reporting portal, and structural/infrastructure gaps are tracked annually through Rural Health Statistics, which documents persistent shortfalls in the number of functioning Sub-Centres, PHCs and CHCs against population-based IPHS norms — the standing evidence base for continued investment through schemes like PM-ABHIM (§4).

8. The National Medical Commission and medical-education reform

The National Medical Commission (NMC) Act, 2019 was passed by Parliament and received Presidential assent on 8 August 2019; the Commission itself became operational, replacing the Medical Council of India, on 25 September 2020. It regulates medical education and professionals through four constituent boards — the Under-Graduate Medical Education Board, Post-Graduate Medical Education Board, Medical Assessment and Rating Board, and Ethics and Medical Registration Board.

Among its planned reforms was the National Exit Test (NExT), envisioned under the NMC Act as a single common examination that would simultaneously serve as the licensing exam for MBBS graduates to practise in India, the entrance test for postgraduate admissions (replacing NEET-PG), and a screening test for foreign medical graduates seeking Indian registration. As of late October 2025, however, the NMC confirmed to the Federation of All India Medical Association that NExT "will not be implemented at least for the next three to four years," with the Commission instead running NMC-funded mock tests over that period "to assess feasibility and gather feedback from students and institutions" before any final rollout decision — citing concerns including uneven digital infrastructure across medical colleges, maintaining uniform standards between government and private institutions, and student stress.

Re-checked as of September 2026 (roughly 11 months after the October 2025 deferral statement): no announcement revising the "3-4 years" NExT deferral timeline was found in this pass. 9. Health workforce migration — outflow of doctors and nurses, and the state's regulatory response

India is one of the world's largest source countries for internationally migrating health professionals, and the state's main lever over this outflow is not a health law but an emigration law. Under the Emigration Act, 1983, any Indian passport holder travelling for employment to one of 18 specified countries (concentrated in the Gulf) needs an "Emigration Clearance Required" (ECR) endorsement, cleared by the Protector of Emigrants before departure; a passport without this endorsement is "Emigration Clearance Not Required" (ECNR). Nursing is treated as a special case within this framework: per the Embassy of India, Riyadh's own published guidance, nursing is among the professions notified as requiring emigration clearance "irrespective of their educational qualifications" — unlike most graduate professionals, who become ECNR once they hold a degree, a nurse remains under mandatory clearance regardless of qualification, on the reasoning that overseas nurse recruitment carries a higher risk of exploitative contracts and needs case-by-case protective screening.

On the physician side, the government's most concretely documented intervention concerns doctors deputed to the United States on J-1 exchange-visitor visas, who are ordinarily bound by a two-year home-residency requirement before they can apply for a green card or H-1B status; a Health Ministry "NORI" ("No Objection") certificate was, for a period, a route to a waiver of that requirement. Per the Ministry's own statement, "Steps taken to Stop Brain Drain of Doctors Working under the Central Government Sectors" (9 December 2014), only eleven such certificates were issued in 2011 before the practice was halted altogether; following guidelines finalised in March 2013, "NORI Certificates will not be issued under any circumstance" — a freeze relaxed only later, and only "to applicants who are above the age of 65 years." The same statement lists parallel domestic retention measures for doctors in central government service: enhanced pay and allowances following the Sixth Central Pay Commission, a retirement age raised to 65 for medical faculty, and a revised Assured Career Progression scheme.

Trap. Don't read the 1:834 doctor-population ratio, "better than the WHO norm" (§3), as meaning outflow is a non-issue — the two facts answer different questions: §3's ratio counts doctors registered within India, while this section concerns emigration of the already-scarce, often postgraduate-trained cohort. A Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Health and Family Welfare report (176th Report, "Affordability and Accessibility of Healthcare Facilities in Public and Private Sector," presented 7 August 2026) has recommended a voluntary online portal for NRI/overseas-based Indian doctors to register willingness to serve in India temporarily — a "reverse brain drain" proposal, not yet an operational scheme.
No PIB, MoHFW, NMC or MEA release with a current (2024-2026) count of how many Indian doctors or nurses emigrate annually was located in this pass. A Kerala-focused report (Onmanorama, 22 July 2026), citing immigration-clearance figures without naming the underlying government database, put national nurse emigration-clearance numbers at 16,594 (2023), 14,584 (2024, a 12.1% fall) and 11,581 (2025, a 30.2% fall versus 2023) — attributed to Gulf states prioritising their own citizens and tighter entry rules elsewhere. Treat this trend and these specific figures as secondary-sourced and unconfirmed against a primary emigration-authority release; the exam-safe facts in this section are the ECR/NORI regulatory mechanisms above, not this count.

10. The Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010

The Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010, per the Health Ministry's own clinical-establishments portal, exists "to provide for registration and regulation of all clinical establishments in the country with a view to prescribe the minimum standards of facilities and services provided by them." It covers therapeutic and diagnostic establishments in both the public and private sectors — including single-doctor clinics — with the sole stated exception of establishments run by the Armed Forces. Implementation runs through a National Council for Clinical Establishments at the centre and corresponding State Councils and District Registering Authorities, which register facilities against minimum standards and can penalise unregistered operation.

Because public health is a State List subject, Parliament's 2010 Act could not simply apply itself across India: it took effect automatically only in the Union Territories and in states that had already passed an Article 252 resolution requesting central legislation, and elsewhere it needed each state to separately adopt it. The result, per the Ministry's own current statement, is genuinely non-uniform: "The Act has been adopted in the 19 States/UTs namely, Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Bihar, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Mizoram, Rajasthan, Sikkim, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, and all Union Territories except the NCT of Delhi." States that have not adopted the central Act (including, notably, Andhra Pradesh) either regulate private clinical establishments under their own separate state legislation, or lack a comparable registration law altogether.

Trap. For an Andhra Pradesh/Telangana-focused exam, this is a genuinely high-value nuance: Telangana is on the list of states that have adopted the central Act, while Andhra Pradesh is not — the two post-bifurcation states diverge on this specific point even though the chapter's other sections (NHM, PM-ABHIM, IPHS) apply to both uniformly as centrally-sponsored schemes. Don't assume a national law "regulating private hospitals" applies everywhere just because it exists — check the adoption list before answering a state-specific question.
No source consulted in this pass gave a current national total of clinical establishments registered under the Act, or a state-by-state registration count; this remains open for a future pass.

11. Health insurance penetration — PM-JAY, private cover and the IRDAI

India's insurance regulator, the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI), tracks health financing from the demand side that §1's National Health Accounts data tracks from the supply side. IRDAI defines insurance "penetration" precisely as "gross premiums written for direct life and non-life insurance business as a percentage of GDP" — by that measure, per its own Annual Report 2024-25, India's overall penetration stood at just 3.7% in 2024-25, unchanged from 2023-24 (life insurance penetration slipped from 2.8% to 2.7%; non-life held steady at 1.0%). Within the non-life book, health is now the dominant line: health insurance premium (excluding personal accident and travel cover) reached ₹1,27,417 crore in 2024-25 — 41.42% of total non-life premium, up from 40.29% the year before — though premium growth slowed sharply, to 9.19% year-on-year against 19.50% in 2023-24.

Running alongside this commercially-regulated market is the world's largest tax-funded health assurance scheme, Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (AB PM-JAY), entitling roughly 12 crore poorer households to cashless secondary- and tertiary-care hospitalisation cover of ₹5 lakh per family per year. Per the Health Ministry's April 2026 PIB release, "Insurance for All: Expanding Coverage, Strengthening Social Security," 43.52 crore Ayushman cards had been created as of 28 February 2026; a separate, slightly earlier Ministry document puts cumulative authorised hospital admissions at 10.98 crore and claims paid at ₹1.60 lakh crore (as of 1 December 2025). Coverage was extended on 29 October 2024 to all citizens aged 70 and above, "irrespective of [their] socioeconomic status," under Ayushman Vay Vandana, estimated to bring in a further 4.5 crore families (roughly 6 crore individuals). The same April 2026 release puts private (non-government) health cover at 41.84 crore policies issued in FY 2024-25, and the Employees' State Insurance (ESI) scheme — social insurance for organised-sector workers — at 14.91 crore beneficiaries as of 31 March 2025.

Trap. Do not fold PM-JAY's 43.52-crore-card figure into the "3.7% insurance penetration" statistic — by IRDAI's own definition, penetration is premium collected as a share of GDP, and PM-JAY is a tax-funded entitlement with no premium paid by or on behalf of the beneficiary, so its reach does not enter that ratio at all. A question asking for India's "insurance penetration" wants the IRDAI figure (3.7%); a question asking how many Indians have some form of health cover wants PM-JAY, ESI and private-policy figures added separately — and even then, "cards issued" or "policies issued" overstates lives actually protected, since India's out-of-pocket expenditure share is still 43.4% (§1), the harder test of whether coverage translates into financial protection.

Sources: IRDAI, Annual Report 2024-25; PIB, "Insurance for All: Expanding Coverage, Strengthening Social Security" (Apr 2026); PIB, "Ministry of Health & Family Welfare Initiatives & Achievements-2025" (Jan 2026).

12. Artificial intelligence in Indian healthcare — the SAHI strategy

As of early 2026, India's health ministry is still writing a sector-specific AI policy rather than operating under one. Per a 13 February 2026 PIB release, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare is developing a Strategy for AI in Healthcare for India (SAHI) "through consultation with public and private stakeholders," positioned as the health-sector application layer of the broader, Cabinet-approved IndiaAI Mission (launched March 2024, outlay ₹10,371.92 crore). The stated design principle is "democratisation of technology, ensuring AI tools reach all segments of society including rural and underserved populations," framed explicitly as a contribution to Universal Health Coverage and the "Viksit Bharat by 2047" vision.

Several AI applications are already running inside national programmes ahead of SAHI's formal release, and the same PIB release quantifies some results: AI-assisted chest-X-ray analysis and adverse-outcome prediction under the National TB Elimination Programme is credited with a "27% decline in adverse TB outcomes"; MadhuNetrAI, an AI retinal-triage tool under the National Diabetic Retinopathy Screening programme, had screened "7,100+ patients... across 38 facilities"; eSanjeevani (§7) now folds in AI-assisted differential diagnosis and had supported "282 million consultations" cumulatively; and an AI-based Media Disease Surveillance System had published "4,500+ outbreak alerts" since April 2022 for early outbreak detection. Separately, AI-linked work on Ayurgenomics under the Ayush Grid received WHO recognition in July 2025 as a global model.

Governance currently rests on two non-statutory instruments rather than a dedicated AI-in-health law: ICMR's own "Ethical Guidelines for Application of Artificial Intelligence in Biomedical Research and Healthcare," issued around 27 March 2023 by the DHR-ICMR Artificial Intelligence Cell and explicitly described as "a living document [to] be updated as ethics in AI evolve," covering ethical principles, stakeholder guidance, ethics-review processes, AI governance and informed consent; and the separate, non-health-specific MeitY AI Governance Guidelines. Three health-AI Centres of Excellence — at AIIMS Delhi, PGIMER Chandigarh and AIIMS Rishikesh — were designated in March 2025, and the National Health Authority signed an MoU with IIT Kanpur in October 2024 to build a National Federated Learning Platform, intended to let AI models train across hospitals' data without the data itself leaving each hospital. This sits atop the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission's (ABDM) data backbone, which had issued 799 million digital health IDs, registered over 410,000 healthcare facilities and linked 671 million health records as of August 2025.

Trap. The ICMR document is titled "Ethical Guidelines," not a regulation or an Act — it carries no statutory force and creates no licensing requirement for an AI diagnostic tool. Don't describe it as India's "AI-in-healthcare law"; as of the SAHI strategy still being "developed" per the February 2026 release, India does not yet have a dedicated statutory health-AI framework.

Sources: PIB, "Transforming Healthcare Delivery Through Artificial Intelligence" (13 Feb 2026); ICMR, "Ethical Guidelines for Application of Artificial Intelligence in Biomedical Research and Healthcare" (icmr.gov.in, 2023); The Hitavada, "ICMR comes up with first ethical guidelines for application of AI in healthcare research" (27 Mar 2023, date corroboration).

13. Medical device regulation — CDSCO's separate track for devices

Section 6 covers CDSCO's role in approving drugs; medical devices are regulated by the same body but under a structurally different set of rules, a distinction exam questions frequently blur. Before 2017, only 15 categories of medical device (catheters, cardiac stents and similar items) were regulated at all, and even those were regulated by being notified as "drugs" under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act — an awkward fit, since a stent or an MRI machine is nothing like a chemical formulation. The Medical Devices Rules, 2017, notified by the Health Ministry on 31 January 2017, replaced that arrangement with a dedicated, device-specific regulatory framework.

The 2017 Rules classify every device into one of four risk-based classes — Class A (low risk), Class B (low-moderate risk), Class C (moderate-high risk) and Class D (high risk) — with the compliance burden scaled to the class: a Class A device can be licensed "without prior audit of manufacturing site" through self-certification, with post-approval audits following later, while higher-risk classes face progressively stricter Third-Party Conformity Assessment through CDSCO-accredited "Notified Bodies." Manufacturing sites are expected to align with the international ISO 13485 quality-management standard, applications are processed online against defined timelines, and — unlike a drug manufacturing licence, which requires periodic renewal — a device licence under the 2017 Rules has perpetual validity once granted, subject to ongoing risk-based audits rather than a renewal cycle.

Trap. Two traps in one: (1) don't assume CDSCO regulates devices the way it regulates drugs — the drug pathway (§6) is inspection- and clinical-trial-led and requires periodic renewal, while the device pathway is risk-class-based, leans on third-party (and, for Class A, self-) certification, and grants perpetual licences; (2) a question describing a stent, glucometer or MRI scanner being cleared "as a drug" is describing the pre-2017 position, not the current one.

Source: PIB, "Health Ministry Notifies Medical Devices Rules, 2017" (31 Jan 2017).

14. The National List of Essential Medicines — NLEM's role inside NPPA price control

Section 6 introduced NPPA's price-control machinery in outline; the mechanism's actual trigger is the National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM), and the two are linked by explicit statutory cross-reference rather than by loose policy overlap. The current NLEM 2022 was released on 13 September 2022 by then-Health Minister Dr Mansukh Mandaviya and contains 384 medicines organised into 27 therapeutic categories — the fourth revision of the list since it was first published in 1996 (subsequent editions in 2003, 2011 and 2015), and a net change from NLEM 2015 of 34 medicines added and 26 removed. PIB's own framing of its purpose: NLEM "plays an important role in ensuring accessibility of affordable quality medicines at all levels of healthcare."

That accessibility role is not merely aspirational — NLEM is the legal gateway into price control. Under the Drug (Prices Control) Order, 2013 (DPCO 2013), a "scheduled formulation" is, by definition, a formulation listed in the Order's own Schedule I, and Schedule I is drawn directly from NLEM: since the 2013 Order, "scheduled formulations consist of the 'Essential Medicines' declared so by the Government through its National List of Essential Medicines," with any formulation built on an NLEM-listed drug eligible for price fixation. This is why §6's "roughly 384 scheduled drugs" figure and this section's 384-medicine NLEM 2022 count are the same number by design, not coincidence: NPPA fixes a binding ceiling price (under DPCO paragraph 4) only for formulations that made it onto NLEM/Schedule I in the first place. A medicine that falls off NLEM in a revision — such as the 26 dropped in the 2022 list — correspondingly falls out of this mandatory ceiling-price mechanism, though the government separately retains a residual power, under DPCO paragraph 19, to cap prices of non-scheduled (non-NLEM) formulations only in extraordinary circumstances, subject to a different and looser rule (typically an annual MRP-increase cap rather than a fixed ceiling).

Trap. Don't treat "on NLEM" and "under DPCO price control" as two separate facts to memorise — they are the same fact stated two ways: NLEM membership is what makes a formulation "scheduled" and therefore subject to NPPA's mandatory ceiling price under DPCO 2013 paragraph 4. A non-NLEM medicine is not price-uncontrolled by default, but it sits under the weaker paragraph-19 mechanism (extraordinary-circumstances price capping, not a standing ceiling) rather than the standard route.

Sources: PIB, "Dr Mansukh Mandaviya launches National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM), 2022" (13 Sep 2022); Arthapedia (Indian Economic Service, ies.gov.in), "Drug Price Control Orders (DPCO)"; §6 of this chapter (CDSCO/NPPA), cross-referenced for the DPCO 2013 scheduled-drug count.

15. Health Technology Assessment in India (HTAIn)

Health Technology Assessment in India (HTAIn) was set up under the Department of Health Research (DHR), Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, to fill a gap the 12th Five-Year Plan's Working Group on Health Research had identified: no standing institutional mechanism existed to judge the "appropriateness and cost effectiveness of the available and new Health Technologies in India" before they were adopted into public programmes or reimbursement schedules. HTAIn's stated aim is to "encourage the process and finalize the development of standardized cost effective interventions," with the explicit downstream goals of reducing treatment costs, cutting variation in patient care, lowering out-of-pocket expenditure (the same OOPE metric tracked in §1), and streamlining medical reimbursement — in effect, evidence-based gatekeeping for where public health rupees go, rather than open-ended technology adoption.

HTAIn's work is concrete and ongoing rather than purely advisory in the abstract: its own "Completed HTA Studies" listing includes assessments of HPV DNA testing for cervical-cancer screening and influenza vaccination among the elderly (both 2024), screening methods for COPD at Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (2024, directly bearing on the primary-care tier described in §2), and Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement (TAVR) cost-effectiveness (2024), with assessments of typhoid conjugate vaccination and HPV vaccination for India's immunisation programme dated 2026. HTAIn also convenes an annual public forum, the International Symposium on Health Technology Assessment (ISHTA) — the 2025 edition, held 8-9 March 2025 at Bharat Mandapam under the theme "Bridging Evidence to Policy: Health Technology Assessment for Affordable Healthcare," was framed around evidence-based policymaking, support for innovative technologies, and value-based pricing for resource allocation.

No source consulted in this pass gives a formally constituted "HTA Board" membership list or a current total count of studies completed to date; this chapter states only the specific completed-study examples the DHR-HTAIn site itself lists, and flags the organisational-chart detail as open for a future pass.

16. The Public Health Management Cadre debate

A long-running administrative reform question in Indian health policy is whether public-health management — running a district's disease surveillance, health-programme implementation and facility administration — should be staffed by a dedicated Public Health Management Cadre (PHMC), separate from the clinical medical cadre of practising doctors who currently fill many of these administrative posts by default. National Health Policy 2017 proposed creating a PHMC across all states, and the 13th Central Council of Health and Family Welfare (2019) went further, with member states resolving to establish a PHMC in their own states by 2022. The Health Ministry followed up institutionally, forming a dedicated PHMC expert committee in 2020 — chaired by the Joint Secretary (Policy), with the Ministry's Public Health Administration division as secretariat, and drawing on consultation with NITI Aayog, WHO and the Public Health Foundation of India (PHFI) — which produced a guidance note for states covering "principles, career pathways and organograms" for the proposed cadre, most recently reissued as a revised PHMC booklet in 2023.

Implementation, however, remains markedly uneven more than three years past the CCHFW's own 2022 target. Tamil Nadu is the standing reference case, having run a dedicated public health cadre — separate from its clinical medical service — since shortly after independence, and its strong maternal- and child-health indicators are routinely credited in the policy literature to this administrative separation. Odisha is cited as a state that has since established its own dedicated cadre. Most other states, by contrast, continue to fill senior public-health administrative posts from the general clinical cadre, meaning the reform envisioned in NHP 2017 remains substantially a state-by-state work in progress rather than a nationally completed transition.

Trap. Don't confuse the Public Health Management Cadre debate (an administrative-workforce reform — who manages the health system) with the doctor-population and nurse-population staffing ratios in §3 (a clinical-workforce numbers question) or with the Clinical Establishments Act in §10 (which regulates private facilities, not who staffs government public-health administration). All three are workforce/regulatory topics in this chapter but answer entirely different exam questions.

17. Blood transfusion services — NACO, the National Blood Transfusion Council and blood bank licensing

Blood transfusion safety in India runs on two separate tracks that are easy to conflate: a policy-and-promotion track under health-programme administration, and a hard regulatory-licensing track under drug law. The National Blood Transfusion Council (NBTC) was created in 1996, not by ordinary executive action but following a Supreme Court directive arising from a public-interest litigation (Common Cause v. Union of India), which also mandated corresponding State Blood Transfusion Councils (SBTCs) in every state. NBTC functions as "the policy formulating apex body in relation to all matters pertaining to operation of blood centres" — promoting voluntary (non-remunerated) blood donation, setting quality and infrastructure standards, coordinating human-resource development, and formulating national blood policy. It was housed for over two decades within the National AIDS Control Organisation (NACO), run through NACO's own Blood Transfusion Services Division, before governance transitioned to the Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS) in 2021.

The licensing track is entirely separate and considerably stricter, because Indian law treats blood itself as a regulated product: "human blood is covered under the definition of 'Drug'" under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940, and every blood bank must hold a licence under that Act — mandatory licensing was enforced from 1997. The operative requirements sit in Schedule F Part XII-B (blood banks and blood-component preparation) and Part XII-C (blood-product manufacture) of the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945, with the detailed collection/storage/processing/distribution rules laid out in Part X-B (Rules 122-EA to 122-P). Licensing itself runs through a two-tier structure: a State Licensing Authority conducts the initial inspection and processes the application, while the Central Licence Approving Authority (CLAA), under the Drugs Controller General of India, gives final approval — a structure the DCGI established following a further Supreme Court direction in 1992-93. A licence, once granted or renewed, is valid for five years, after which renewal (with fresh inspection fees) is required — unlike the perpetual-validity regime §13 describes for Class-A medical devices. As of the DGHS's own current figures, 4,263 licensed blood centres operate nationally across the government, NGO/charitable and private sectors.

Trap. NACO/NBTC sets blood-transfusion policy and standards and promotes voluntary donation — it does not itself issue a blood bank's operating licence. That licence is a Drugs and Cosmetics Act instrument, granted by the State Licensing Authority with CLAA/DCGI approval, on the same statutory chassis (though a different Schedule/Part) that CDSCO uses for drugs (§6) and, before 2017, once used for medical devices (§13). A question asking "which body regulates whether a blood bank can legally operate" wants the state drugs-licensing authority and DCGI, not NACO.

Sources: naco.gov.in, "National Blood Transfusion Council (NBTC)"; dghs.mohfw.gov.in, "Blood Transfusion Services"; CDSCO, "Regulatory Requirements of Blood and/or Its Components and Blood Products" (guidance document incorporating GSR 166(E), 11 Mar 2020).

18.3 Mnemonics

Four compact devices for the recurring confusions flagged across this chapter and in §18.1-18.2:

1. Facility tier order — "Sub, Pehle, CHC, District" (S-P-C-D). Sub-Centre → PHC → CHC → District Hospital is the fixed ascending order of both population served and clinical complexity; "Pehle" (Hindi: "first") cues that PHC is the first doctor-staffed tier a patient reaches above the Sub-Centre.

2. CDSCO vs. NPPA — "Clearance before Cost." CDSCO decides whether a drug/device Can be sold at all (safety, efficacy, manufacturing standards); NPPA decides the Number it sells for (price ceilings under DPCO/NLEM). Clearance (C) always logically precedes Cost-control (N) — a drug must clear CDSCO before NPPA's price ceiling is even relevant to it.

3. Doctors "Did it," Nurses "Need it" — the WHO-ratio asymmetry. India's doctor ratio (1:834) already "did" better than the WHO's 1:1,000 target; India's nurse ratio (1:670) still "needs" to close the gap to WHO's 1:300 — same letter (D/N) as the direction of travel (Done vs. Needed), so the two professions' standing relative to WHO is never the same answer.

4. NLEM off the list, price control off the hook — "No List, No Limit." A medicine stays under NPPA's mandatory ceiling price only while it remains on NLEM/DPCO Schedule I; drop off NLEM (as 26 medicines did in the 2015→2022 revision) and the drug falls out of the standing price-ceiling mechanism into the weaker, discretionary paragraph-19 route — "No List [membership], No [standing price] Limit."

19. The constitutional foundation — why a State List subject carries central laws

Two genuine previous-year questions — NDA-I 2009 (Q10) ("The entry 'Public Health and Sanitation' is included in the Constitution of India in..." State List) and CAPF 2019 (Q4) (a pair-matching question confirming, as all three pairs correct, that "State list — Public health and sanitation," "Union list — Citizenship, naturalisation and aliens," and "Concurrent list — Legal, medical and other [professions]" are all correctly matched) — test a fact this chapter had stated loosely in §10 ("because public health is a State List subject") without ever naming the actual constitutional entries. That gap is closed here. Under the Seventh Schedule, "Public health and sanitation; hospitals and dispensaries" is Entry 6 of the State List (List II) — squarely a state subject, which is exactly why Parliament's Clinical Establishments Act, 2010 (§10) could not simply apply itself nationwide and instead needed each state's own adoption (or an Article 252 resolution).

But two closely related subjects sit one list over, in the Concurrent List (List III), where both Parliament and state legislatures can legislate (with central law prevailing on conflict, under Article 254): Entry 26, "Legal, medical and other professions," and Entry 25, "Education, including technical education, medical education and universities... vocational and technical training of labour" — the latter only Concurrent since the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 moved education (along with forests, protection of wild animals and birds, weights and measures, and administration of justice — five subjects in total) out of the State List. This is the specific constitutional mechanism that lets the National Medical Commission Act, 2019 (§8) regulate medical education and the medical profession uniformly across all of India, replacing the Medical Council of India nationwide, in a way the Clinical Establishments Act never could: NMC legislates under Entries 25/26 (Concurrent — profession and medical-education regulation), while a state's hospital-and-clinic registration regime sits under Entry 6 (State — the actual running of health facilities). The same Concurrent-List logic extends to AYUSH: the National Commission for Indian System of Medicine (NCISM) Act, 2020 and the National Commission for Homoeopathy Act, 2020, which replaced the old Central Council of Indian Medicine and Central Council of Homoeopathy on the same NMC-style regulatory model, draw on the identical Entry 25/26 basis.

Trap. Don't let "public health is a State subject" (true, under Entry 6) lead to the false inference that medical education or professional licensing is also purely a state matter — those sit in the Concurrent List (Entries 25 and 26) precisely so a central law (NMC Act, NCISM Act) can bind every state. The practical test a question is likely to pose: "who decides if a clinic can legally operate" is Entry 6/State-List territory (hence §10's patchy national adoption of the Clinical Establishments Act); "who decides if a person can legally call themselves a doctor" is Entry 25/26/Concurrent-List territory (hence the NMC Act's uniform, all-India reach).

20. PM-JAY's eligibility mechanics — SECC 2011, pre-existing disease cover, and the family-size question

A genuine CDS I 2019 (General Studies, Q119) previous-year question asked which of four listed items are "benefits of the Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PMJAY)": free treatment at public and empanelled private hospitals, cashless/paperless access, ₹5,00,000 per family per year, and — the deliberately false fourth option — "pre-existing diseases are not covered." The correct answer excludes that fourth statement, because PM-JAY does cover pre-existing conditions. §11 of this chapter gives PM-JAY's scale (43.52 crore cards, ₹5 lakh/family/year) but never states this specific, exam-tested mechanic, nor how a household qualifies in the first place — both gaps are closed here from NHA's own PM-JAY eligibility page.

Beneficiary identification runs entirely off the Socio-Economic Caste Census (SECC) 2011 database, not an income test. For rural households, qualification requires meeting at least one of several automatic-inclusion/deprivation criteria — among them D1 ("only one room with kucha walls and kucha roof"), D2 (no adult member aged 16-59), D4 (a disabled member with no able-bodied adult in the household), D5 (SC/ST households), and D7 ("landless households deriving a major part of their income from manual casual labour"), alongside destitute persons, manual scavengers, primitive tribal groups and freed bonded labourers, who are automatically included. For urban households, eligibility instead runs off eleven listed occupational categories — ragpickers, domestic workers, street vendors, construction workers, sweepers, sanitation workers, and similar informal-sector occupations among them — rather than any housing-deprivation test.

Three features NHA states explicitly, each a plausible exam distractor: "all pre-existing medical conditions are covered under the scheme" from the day of enrolment, with no waiting period; "no restriction on family size, age or gender" — the ₹5 lakh annual cover operates as a family floater usable by any household member, however large the household; and the benefit is fully portable nationwide — "a beneficiary can visit any empanelled public or private hospital in India to avail cashless treatment," not only a hospital in their home state.

Trap. The "no family-size cap" feature is a deliberate design break from PM-JAY's own predecessor: the Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana (RSBY), launched 1 April 2008 under the Ministry of Labour and Employment (transferred to Health and Family Welfare from 1 April 2015) and offering ₹30,000 cover per family per year, did cap coverage at five members — "head of household, spouse and up to three dependent children or parents." A question contrasting RSBY and PM-JAY is testing exactly this: coverage amount rose from ₹30,000 to ₹5,00,000, and the five-member family cap was removed entirely — don't assume PM-JAY simply "scaled up" RSBY's rules unchanged. (Both schemes, notably, covered pre-existing conditions from day one — that specific feature is not what distinguishes them.)

Sources: nha.gov.in, "About Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM-JAY)" (National Health Authority); synopsisias.com, CDS I 2019 GS Q119 (PYQ compilation with answer key); Wikipedia, "Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana" (RSBY structural facts, used for secondary/structural confirmation only).

21. The Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission's voluntary architecture

A genuine UPSC Civil Services Prelims 2022 question asked, with reference to the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, whether (1) "private and public hospitals must adopt it," (2) "every citizen of India should ultimately be part of it" since it aims at universal health coverage, and (3) "it has seamless portability across the country" — the correct answer is statement 3 only: both 1 and 2 are false, because ABDM participation is voluntary for both providers and citizens. §12 of this chapter already covers ABDM's data-backbone scale (799 million digital health IDs, 410,000-plus facilities, 671 million linked records, as of August 2025) but never states the voluntary-vs-mandatory distinction this PYQ directly tests — that gap is closed here.

The Health Ministry's own position, stated plainly in a PIB release on ABDM's implementation: "Creation of ABHA number is voluntary" — no citizen is required to obtain one, and no hospital is compelled to come onto the ABDM network, though the Mission's stated aim is that both eventually will, by choice rather than mandate. ABDM's three core building blocks are the ABHA (Ayushman Bharat Health Account) number — "a random 14-digit number," issued digitally or as a hardcopy, serving as a portable patient identifier that lets health records follow a person between providers and states; the Healthcare Professionals Registry (HPR), "a comprehensive repository of all healthcare professionals involved in delivery of healthcare services" across both modern and traditional (AYUSH) medicine systems; and the Health Facility Registry (HFR), the parallel repository of public and private health facilities — hospitals, clinics, laboratories and pharmacies — across all systems of medicine. ABDM itself traces to 27 September 2021, when the pilot National Digital Health Mission (running since August 2020 in six Union Territories) was launched nationally under its current name.

Trap. Don't conflate the ABHA number (§21, a voluntary, universal digital-identity instrument anyone can create, with no eligibility test) with the Ayushman (PM-JAY) card (§11/§20, an entitlement restricted to SECC-2011-qualifying households, with no cost to the beneficiary once issued) — both carry the "Ayushman Bharat" brand and both involve a card/ID, but one is a universal health-data identifier and the other is a means-tested insurance entitlement; a question that blends ABDM's "universal," voluntary framing with PM-JAY's restricted, SECC-based eligibility (as the false Statement 2 in the 2022 PYQ does) is testing exactly this distinction.

22. Cross-Chapter Linkages

APPSC Mains examiners reward answers that connect themes across the syllabus rather than treating each topic as a sealed compartment. The three subsections below trace specific, verifiable mechanisms linking this chapter's delivery-system content to three sibling chapters: how Chapter 5's disease-burden figures explain why this chapter's infrastructure and workforce are shaped the way they are; how this chapter's facility tiers and digital/workforce backbone are the concrete machinery through which Chapter 7's named flagship schemes actually reach a patient; and how Chapter 1's climate-risk data explains why PM-ABHIM-era infrastructure investment increasingly carries a climate-resilience design layer. Every cross-chapter fact below is cited to the sibling chapter's own section so it can be checked against that chapter directly.

22.1 Disease burden drives delivery-system design (Chapter 5)

Chapter 5 §1 documents India's epidemiological transition in precise terms: using India State-Level Disease Burden Initiative data, the non-communicable-disease share of DALYs rose from 30% in 1990 to 55% in 2016, while the communicable/maternal/neonatal/nutritional share fell from 61% to 33% over the same period; Chapter 5 §2 separately states that NCDs — cardiovascular disease, cancer, chronic respiratory disease, diabetes — now account for 63% of all deaths per NPCDCS's own framing. This chapter's delivery architecture is the structural answer to that transition, not a coincidental parallel: the Ayushman Arogya Mandir tier (§2) runs population-based NCD screening for everyone aged 30 and above, and Chapter 5 §16 names the exact infrastructure this feeds — 724 District NCD Clinics, 210 District Cardiac Care Units, 326 District Day Care Centres and 6,110 Community Health Centre NCD Clinics, built to manage a diabetes burden the ICMR-INDIAB study puts at 10.1 crore (101 million) people nationally. NRHM was launched in 2005 (§2) against the older communicable-disease burden profile; the screening-age-30+ design of the AAM tier only makes sense against the NCD-majority burden Chapter 5 documents emerging over the following decade.

Chapter 5 §14 quantifies why antimicrobial resistance is treated as a structural health-system priority rather than a narrow clinical concern: the Lancet/GRAM study estimated 1.27 million deaths globally in 2019 directly attributable to bacterial AMR and 4.95 million deaths associated with a drug-resistant infection, with South Asia specifically recording 22 deaths per 100,000 attributable to AMR and 77 per 100,000 associated with it — among the highest regional rates the study found. This chapter's drug-regulatory architecture is the lever available against that burden: CDSCO's manufacturing-standard inspections and clinical-trial gatekeeping (§6) govern whether an antibiotic reaches the market at all, while the NLEM-DPCO price-control chain (§14) governs whether a correctly-prescribed antibiotic stays affordable rather than pushing patients toward cheaper, often substandard or partial-course alternatives that themselves drive resistance; blood-safety licensing under the same Drugs and Cosmetics Act Schedule F framework (§17) closes a parallel infection-control pathway. None of these three sections name AMR directly — Chapter 5 §8/§23 carries the dedicated NAP-AMR policy response — but the regulatory instruments this chapter documents are the ones that response has to work through.

Trap. Don't assume this chapter's "better than WHO norm" doctor-population ratio (1:834, §3) means India's health workforce is adequate across every specialty. Chapter 5 §13 gives a sub-specialty figure that contradicts that impression sharply: India has 0.75 psychiatrists per 100,000 people against a WHO-recommended 3 per 100,000 — a fifth of the benchmark — against a mental-disorder treatment need the same section puts at "nearly 150 million Indians." A question that cites the aggregate doctor ratio as evidence of workforce adequacy, without checking whether it is asking about a specific specialty, is exactly the trap this contrast is built to test.

22.2 This chapter's infrastructure as the delivery backbone for Chapter 7's flagship initiatives

Chapter 7 §1 describes Ayushman Bharat as built on "two inter-related components" — Health and Wellness Centres/Ayushman Arogya Mandirs delivering primary care, and PM-JAY providing hospitalisation cover — and explicitly defers the first component's detail to this chapter. That deferral is literal, not stylistic: the 1,81,873 Ayushman Arogya Mandirs operationalised nationally as of 30 November 2025, with a cumulative footfall of 494.71 crore and 41.93 crore teleconsultations (§2 of this chapter), is Ayushman Bharat's first component — Chapter 7 names the scheme design, this chapter supplies the physical network running it. The same relationship holds for workforce: the ASHA/ANM frontline cadre this chapter defines by role and ratio (§3) is the delivery mechanism for Chapter 7's named catch-up immunisation drive, Intensified Mission Indradhanush 5.0 — run across all districts nationally in three 2023 rounds, vaccinating over 34.7 lakh children and 6.55 lakh pregnant women in its first two rounds alone (Chapter 7 §5) — since IMI's household-level tracing and mobilisation runs through exactly the ASHA-ANM division of labour this chapter sets out.

Digital and tele-health infrastructure shows the same pattern. eSanjeevani's doctor-to-doctor hub-and-spoke service (§7 of this chapter) runs through roughly 50,000 Health and Wellness Centres — the same AAM physical tier counted in §2 — and that shared infrastructure is also the delivery layer for Chapter 7's Tele-MANAS (launched 10 October 2022, Chapter 7 §7) and the U-WIN immunisation registry (Chapter 7 §5): both are service/data layers riding the facility network this chapter maps, not separate physical build-outs. The Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission's building blocks — ABHA, the Healthcare Professionals Registry and the Health Facility Registry, with their voluntary (not mandatory) design confirmed against the 2022 UPSC Prelims PYQ (§21 of this chapter) — are precisely what Chapter 7 §2 calls ABDM's "digital backbone," with this chapter additionally supplying the voluntary/mandatory distinction Chapter 7's own ABDM section does not state. One further concrete link: Chapter 7 §6 names Jan Aushadhi (PMBJP) as the retail channel selling generic medicines "substantially below their branded equivalents" — that pricing gap exists only because NLEM-linked drugs already sit under NPPA's DPCO ceiling price (§14 of this chapter); Jan Aushadhi is a distribution channel operating downstream of the same price-control mechanism this chapter documents, not an independent pricing scheme.

22.3 Climate-resilient health infrastructure (Chapter 1)

Chapter 1 §10 — the CEEW Climate Vulnerability Index (26 October 2021) — finds that 27 of India's 35 states and union territories are highly vulnerable to extreme hydro-meteorological disasters, that more than 80% of India's population lives in such highly vulnerable districts, and, specifically relevant to this exam's geography, that Andhra Pradesh ranks second nationally on the index (after Assam), with 59% of eastern coastal-zone districts facing extreme cyclone vulnerability. Chapter 1 §11 supplies the concrete 2025 instance: Severe Cyclonic Storm Montha made landfall near Kakinada on 28 October 2025, and the Ministry of Home Affairs' own Rajya Sabha reply (3 December 2025) put Andhra Pradesh's total estimated damage at ₹6,355.60 crore. This is precisely the exposure profile that PM-ABHIM's deliverables (§4 of this chapter) are built against: public-health units at 11 seaports and critical-care hospital blocks in every district with population over 5 lakh are infrastructure investments sited exactly where Chapter 1's CVI data says the hazard concentrates — a coastal, cyclone-exposed state carrying the second-highest vulnerability ranking in the country.

The mechanism connecting the two chapters is now also an explicit, named one rather than only a geographic coincidence. The National Centre for Disease Control's own "Guidelines for Green and Climate-Resilient Healthcare Facilities" (first issued February 2023, reissued as a facility guidebook in 2025) states plainly that its facility-assessment checklist "has been developed based on the existing NPCCHH guidelines on green and climate resilient healthcare facilities and the Indian Public Health Standards (IPHS) 2022" — the same four-volume, tier-specific standard this chapter documents in §2 (Sub-Centre/PHC/CHC/District Hospital volumes). The checklist scores each facility across five domains, one of them named "Climate Resilient Elements" covering structural audits, flood resilience, heat preparedness, water/power backup and disaster-preparedness planning, with facilities graded 1 (least vulnerable) to 4 (severely vulnerable). In other words, the IPHS 2022 tier standard this chapter already covers as a staffing-and-services benchmark is the same document NCDC's climate-resilience scoring system is built on top of — the facility that must meet IPHS norms to count as a functioning CHC or District Hospital is now also being scored against the heatwave and flood exposure Chapter 1 quantifies.

Two of Chapter 1's hazard figures map onto specific items in that checklist. The urban heat island effect (Chapter 1 §7) can push city-core temperatures up to 10°C above surrounding areas, against a backdrop in which only about 9% of Indian households have air conditioning — directly relevant to the checklist's "heat preparedness" criterion for facilities that must keep functioning, and keep patients safe, through the heat exposure their own catchment population mostly cannot afford to air-condition away. Separately, Chapter 1 §4's finding that extreme daily rainfall events above 150mm rose by roughly 75% over central India between 1950 and 2015 is the hazard behind the checklist's "flood resilience" and "drain maintenance" criteria — a design response to a documented rainfall-intensity trend, not a generic sustainability add-on.

Cross-chapter pass note. No PIB, MoHFW or PM-ABHIM scheme document found in this pass explicitly states that PM-ABHIM funding itself is conditioned on, or linked to, the NCDC climate-resilience checklist — the connection established above is that both instruments operate on the same IPHS 2022 facility-tier standard, which is a documented fact, not that PM-ABHIM disbursement requires a climate-resilience score. Treat the PM-ABHIM/CVI geographic-targeting link (first paragraph above) as the stronger, more directly evidenced claim, and the IPHS-as-shared-foundation link (second paragraph) as the specific named mechanism — but not as proof that every PM-ABHIM-funded facility has already been retrofitted against the NCDC checklist.
