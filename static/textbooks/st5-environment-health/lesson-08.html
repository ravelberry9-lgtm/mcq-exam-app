1. Dr. NTR Vaidya Seva

Andhra Pradesh's flagship health assurance scheme has changed its name several times, tracking closely with changes of state government — a naming history worth learning precisely because it is exactly the kind of detail an exam question can turn on. The scheme's lineage, as far as could be verified in this pass: it began as Rajiv Aarogyasri (the "Rajiv Aarogyasri Health Insurance Scheme," confirmed effective from 1 April 2007, under the undivided Andhra Pradesh's Congress-led government, named for Rajiv Gandhi). After the 2014 bifurcation of Andhra Pradesh and the change of state government, it was renamed to "Dr. Nandamuri Taraka Rama Rao Aarogya Seva" (per G.O.Ms.No.127, dated 27 September 2014), and then, only a few months later, renamed again — to "Dr. Nandamuri Taraka Rama Rao Vaidya Seva" (commonly "Dr. NTR Vaidya Seva") — by G.O.Ms.No.157, dated 17 December 2014. Following the 2019 change of government, it was renamed "Dr. YSR Aarogyasri" by G.O.Ms.No.59, dated 13 June 2019, with the associated Trust becoming the "Dr. YSR Aarogyasri Health Care Trust." Following the 2024 change of government, it reverted to the name "Dr. NTR Vaidya Seva".

Trap. This topic inventory's own note states the scheme "began as Rajiv Aarogyasri" and was renamed by G.O.Ms.No.157 of 17 Dec 2014 "after bifurcation." What could be directly verified in this pass is more precise: G.O.Ms.No.157 (17 Dec 2014) renamed the scheme from an intermediate name, "Dr. NTR Aarogya Seva" (itself only created a few months earlier, by G.O.Ms.No.127 of 27 Sept 2014), to "Dr. NTR Vaidya Seva" — it did not rename "Rajiv Aarogyasri" directly. The "began as Rajiv Aarogyasri" framing is still very likely correct as the scheme's ultimate origin (2007), but the immediate predecessor name that G.O.157 actually renamed was the short-lived "Dr. NTR Aarogya Seva," not "Rajiv Aarogyasri" itself — worth being precise about if a question asks specifically what G.O.157 renamed.
The claim that the Trust was registered under G.O.Rt.No.404 of 5 August 2015 could not be independently confirmed in this pass — the official GO-listing page (drntrvaidyaseva.ap.gov.in/newgo) and a secondary page specifically about GO 404 (teachernews.in) both failed to load (robots.txt blocks). 2. NTR Vaidya Pariksha — free diagnostics

NTR Vaidya Pariksha is Andhra Pradesh's free-diagnostics scheme, nested under the Union Ministry of Health and Family Welfare's national Free Diagnostics Scheme (launched July 2015). Per WHO India's own reporting, free essential laboratory investigations for all patients visiting public health facilities were launched under the NTR Vaidya Pariksha name in January 2016. Andhra Pradesh was, per WHO's assessment, "the first state to undertake a state-wide rollout of both laboratory and radiology services using a hybrid model" (a public-private partnership approach). WHO India hosted the launch of an evaluation report on the scheme on 4 April 2018, which found savings of ₹228 crore on diagnostic tests during the period January 2016–June 2017.

This topic inventory's specific test-count figures by facility tier — 19 tests at PHCs, 40 at CHCs, 72 at area and district hospitals — could not be confirmed in this pass. 5. AIIMS Mangalagiri

AIIMS Mangalagiri, in Guntur district, is one of the newer All India Institutes of Medical Sciences, established under the Pradhan Mantri Swasthya Suraksha Yojana (PMSSY), which aims to correct regional imbalances in tertiary healthcare and medical education. Its establishment was gazette-notified on 29 January 2018. It began functioning later that year from a temporary campus at Government Siddhartha Medical College, Vijayawada, admitting its first two MBBS batches (50 students each, in 2018 and 2019) there; OPD services began at its permanent Mangalagiri campus in March 2019, and the 2020 MBBS batch (125 students) started at the permanent campus from day one. It was formally dedicated to the nation by Prime Minister Narendra Modi on 25 February 2024, alongside four other AIIMS (Rajkot, Bathinda, Raebareli, and Kalyani) — the five together built at a total cost of ₹6,315.23 crore. AIIMS Mangalagiri itself is a 960-bed hospital complex, built at a cost of ₹1,618.23 crore, spread over 183.11 acres, with a medical college offering 125 MBBS seats.

Source: AIIMS Mangalagiri official "About Us" page (aiimsmangalagiri.edu.in); PIB, "Prime Minister Shri Narendra Modi Dedicates 5 AIIMS... to the nation" (25 February 2024, PRID 2008922).

6. AP MedTech Zone (AMTZ), Visakhapatnam

The Andhra Pradesh MedTech Zone (AMTZ), located in Nadupuru village near Visakhapatnam adjacent to the Visakhapatnam Steel Plant, is described as India's first dedicated medical technology manufacturing park, offering common manufacturing and scientific facilities to medical-device companies. 8. YSR Sampoorna Poshana

YSR Sampoorna Poshana is Andhra Pradesh's state nutrition scheme, aimed at improving the health and nutritional status of children aged roughly 7–72 months (i.e., up to 6 years) and pregnant and lactating mothers, delivered through the Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) framework — the scheme's own page cites 257 ICDS Projects and 55,607 Anganwadi Centres across the state, developed in consultation with the National Institute of Nutrition (NIN). It works by providing supplementary nutrition (described on the scheme's own page as "iron, protein, energy rich nutrition") to bridge the gap between recommended and average caloric/nutrient intake among low-income women and children.

A distinct variant, YSR Sampoorna Poshana Plus, targets Scheduled and Tribal Sub-Plan areas specifically: it is implemented in 77 Scheduled and Tribal Sub Plan mandals (an estimated 3.8 lakh beneficiaries), while the standard YSR Sampoorna Poshana scheme covers the remaining mandals of the state (an estimated 26.36 lakh beneficiaries).

The scheme's launch date, the specific food items/rations provided, the feeding frequency/schedule, and which department administers it (Women and Child Development or Health) were not stated on the official scheme page checked in this pass — that page describes the delivery mechanism (ICDS/Anganwadi) without naming a specific administering department in those exact words.

10. Fluorosis in Rayalaseema

Rayalaseema — comprising the districts of Anantapur, Kurnool, (YSR) Kadapa, and Chittoor, south of the Krishna river — has long-documented groundwater fluoride contamination, a naturally occurring geological phenomenon: fluoride-bearing minerals (fluorapatite) in the region's crystalline rock dissolve into groundwater, with deep borewells (worsened by drought conditions that push communities toward deeper, more fluoride-concentrated sources) particularly implicated. A study of school-going children (aged 12–15) in one mandal of Chittoor district found dental fluorosis in 66.5% of the 1,182 participants studied (786 of 1,182), rising to 75% among children whose households relied on borewell water, with boys showing a markedly higher prevalence than girls. Prolonged excessive fluoride exposure during development can also cause the more severe, skeletal fluorosis, though this particular study assessed only dental fluorosis.

The specific district or mandal most severely affected within Rayalaseema as a whole could not be established from the one study checked in this pass, since its fieldwork was conducted entirely within a single mandal of Chittoor district — its 66.5% figure should not be generalised as a Rayalaseema-wide prevalence rate. No government fluoride-mitigation programme (e.g., a named fluoride-removal or alternate-water-supply scheme) could be confirmed from the source checked; the only mitigation effort mentioned was a non-governmental one (an NGO-run activated-alumina-filter distribution programme in Chittoor), and even that detail came from an AI-summarised portion of the source rather than text read directly, so it should be treated as lower-confidence.

Source: "Prevalence and Correlation between Dental Fluorosis and Drinking Water Fluoride Levels among 12–15-year-old School-going Children of Rayalaseema Region of Andhra Pradesh, India" (PMC).

11. Uddanam CKDu

"Uddanam nephropathy" refers to a cluster of chronic kidney disease of unknown etiology (CKDu) in the Uddanam region of Srikakulam district, Andhra Pradesh — specifically the mandals of Kaviti, Sompeta, Kanchili, Itchapuram, Palasa and Vajrapukotturu. The World Health Organization has characterised it as among the "least understood and least publicized" nephropathies of unknown origin worldwide — its cause is genuinely not established. As of 2015, over 34,000 cases had been recorded in the region, with an estimated 4,500 deaths from the disease over the preceding decade; a Times of India figure cited elsewhere put roughly 21% of the Uddanam-region population as affected by kidney ailments. A 2020 study of risk factors found CKD prevalence 20% higher among farmers than non-farmers, with rice farming (but not other crops such as coconut, cashew, vegetables, or fruit) specifically associated with the disease, and higher prevalence among older people and users of alcohol and chewing tobacco — but explicitly found no association with pesticide or fertiliser use or exposure, and no significant association with gender, hypertension, or diabetes. The disease's clustering among rural agricultural workers doing heavy physical labour in hot conditions parallels similar CKDu clusters elsewhere in the world (Mesoamerican nephropathy, Sri Lankan CKDu), suggesting a possible heat-stress/dehydration-related hypothesis, though this remains one hypothesis among several rather than an established cause.

Audit update (second pass) — gap closed. The Srikakulam-district location and the "Kidney Research Centre and dialysis unit at GGH Palasa" detail are now confirmed via two independent sources. The AP government gave in-principle sanction for a 200-bed Super Specialty Hospital with a Kidney Research Centre and Dialysis Unit at Palasa in September 2019 (Government Order issued by Principal Secretary K.S. 15. Saline intrusion in the Krishna-Godavari delta

Saline water intrusion is a documented problem in parts of the Krishna-Godavari delta region. One specific, recent case study — from Kesanapalli village, Malikipuram mandal, in Dr. B.R. Ambedkar Konaseema district — attributes a serious local saline-intrusion crisis not to the broader causes often assumed for coastal salinity (sea-level rise, upstream damming, groundwater over-extraction, or aquaculture practices — none of which this particular study names) but to a specific, localised infrastructure failure: incomplete dredging, between 2013 and 2017, of a 7.9-kilometre stretch of the Sankaraguptam Major Drain, which created a structural vulnerability allowing tidal saline backwater to intrude into farmland. The study reports roughly one lakh coconut trees and thousands of acres of paddy fields lost across Kesanapalli and at least nine other villages, contamination of both soil and groundwater forcing households to buy purified drinking water, and calls for completing the dredging, constructing sluice gates, soil reclamation, and introducing salt-tolerant crops as remedies, alongside broader governance reform.

This is one specific, localised case study (one drain, one cluster of villages in Konaseema district) rather than a delta-wide assessment — it should not be generalised as describing saline intrusion causes across the whole Krishna-Godavari delta, where other documented mechanisms (such as reduced freshwater flow, aquaculture-linked salinity, or broader groundwater dynamics) may also be relevant but were not confirmed in this pass.

20. The Visakhapatnam (LG Polymers) styrene gas leak, 7 May 2020

Andhra Pradesh's most severe chemical-industrial disaster in recent decades occurred in the early hours of 7 May 2020 (around 3:00 am), when a storage tank at LG Polymers India — a subsidiary of South Korea's LG Chem — leaked styrene monomer vapour at its plant in R.R. Venkatapuram village, Gopalapatnam, Visakhapatnam district. The plant had been shut for weeks under the COVID-19 lockdown, and a failure to maintain the refrigeration needed to keep roughly 1,800–2,000 tonnes of stored styrene stable is understood to have triggered uncontrolled polymerisation and vapour release, which then spread through nearby residential areas. Death-toll figures vary across contemporaneous sources — reputable reports place it at 11, 12, or 13 people killed, including at least one child, with over 1,000 people exposed and several hundred hospitalised (one widely cited figure is 585 people injured). A government-appointed fact-finding committee (five members, headed by former Andhra Pradesh High Court judge Justice B. Seshasayana Reddy) later found "gross safety lapses" and human/procedural failure, including outdated storage tanks lacking temperature sensors, and separately established that the plant had operated for years — reportedly since a 1997 expansion — without valid environmental clearance under the EIA Notification framework.

The National Green Tribunal (NGT) took suo motu cognisance of the disaster and, on 8 May 2020, ordered LG Polymers to deposit ₹50 crore as an interim measure toward environmental restoration and victim compensation, while constituting the fact-finding committee referenced above. On 4 June 2020, a further NGT order held that the company bore "strict and absolute liability" for the loss of life and environmental/public-health damage caused — a standard that fixes liability on an enterprise engaged in a hazardous activity regardless of fault or negligence, and that does not permit the exceptions available under ordinary "strict liability" tort doctrine. The same order barred LG Polymers from restarting operations without fresh statutory clearances and tribunal clearance, and directed the state government to act against the officials who had allowed the plant to run without valid clearance for so long. LG Polymers challenged aspects of the NGT's findings at the Supreme Court, which declined to substantively interfere with the NGT's compensation order (at one point placing it on a short hold pending a fuller hearing, before it proceeded), and separately directed the company to approach the NGT itself. Criminally, an FIR was registered including under IPC Section 304-II (culpable homicide not amounting to murder); twelve accused, including the plant's CEO and two directors, were granted bail by the Andhra Pradesh High Court in August 2020, since all the other offences named were bailable.

Trap. Do not confuse this incident's chemical or its legal doctrine with the 1984 Bhopal gas tragedy: Bhopal involved methyl isocyanate (MIC), while Visakhapatnam involved styrene monomer. On the legal side, "absolute liability" (as opposed to ordinary "strict liability") is itself a post-Bhopal Indian jurisprudential innovation, first articulated by the Supreme Court in the 1986 Oleum Gas Leak case (M.C. Mehta v. Union of India), and the NGT's invocation of that same standard against LG Polymers is a natural link a question could test between the two disasters, even though the chemicals, locations, and decades differ entirely.
Sources materially disagree on the exact death toll (11 vs 12 vs 13 across otherwise reliable reports) — this chapter presents the range rather than a single figure, and a question demanding one number should be checked against whichever source the examiner is drawing from. On the plant's current status: the most recent court development directly traced in this pass (a Supreme Court order permitting LG Polymers restricted access to the still-sealed premises to inventory and sell stock, with sale proceeds held by the District Collector pending security to the AP High Court Registrar General) is undated in the source consulted and appears to be from several years ago rather than 2025/2026 — no reporting from 2024, 2025, or 2026 confirming whether the plant has since reopened, been formally closed, or remains sealed could be located in this pass, despite a dedicated search. Per one 2021–22-era court filing, LG Polymers represented that roughly ₹37.1 crore had by then been disbursed to the families of the deceased and to injured persons, on top of the ₹50 crore NGT deposit — but no more recent, updated compensation total was found. Treat the plant's current operational status as an open gap requiring a fresh check closer to any exam date.

21. Biomedical waste management in Andhra Pradesh

Biomedical waste from healthcare facilities is regulated nationally under the Bio-Medical Waste Management Rules, 2016 (as subsequently amended), which mandate that generators — hospitals, clinics, diagnostic centres, and similar facilities — segregate waste at source into four colour-coded categories (yellow, red, white/translucent, and blue, by waste type and treatment method) and route it to an authorised Common Bio-Medical Waste Treatment Facility (CBWTF) rather than treating or disposing of it themselves, in almost all cases. In Andhra Pradesh, the Andhra Pradesh Pollution Control Board (APPCB) is the prescribed state authority, and — per APPCB's own official page, one of the few AP-government pages that loaded successfully in this research pass, unlike several other state portals in earlier passes — the state currently has 11 Common Bio-Medical Waste Treatment Facilities in operation, giving close to district-level coverage: Rainbow Industries (Laveru) for Srikakulam and Vizianagaram; Maridi Eco Industries and Vasishta Environ Care for Visakhapatnam; EVB Technologies (Rajanagaram) for East Godavari; Safenviron & Associates (Ungutur) for West Godavari; Safenviron Unit-II (Jaggaiahpet) for Krishna; Safenviron (Mangalagiri) for Guntur; the Ongole Medical Waste Treatment Facility for Prakasam; S S Bio Care (Ozili) for SPSR Nellore; AWM Consulting for Chittoor; Sriven Environ Technologies for Anantapur and YSR Kadapa; and Medical Waste Solutions for Kurnool. 22. Andhra Pradesh's forest cover trends and Mission Haritha Andhra Pradesh

Andhra Pradesh's forest cover, as tracked across successive biennial India State of Forest Reports (ISFR) from the Forest Survey of India, showed a period of sustained growth through the late 2010s: ISFR 2019 recorded 29,137.40 sq km of forest cover (17.88% of the state's 1,62,968 sq km geographic area — comprising 1,994.22 sq km Very Dense Forest, 13,938.36 sq km Moderately Dense Forest, and 13,204.82 sq km Open Forest), an increase of 990.40 sq km over the ISFR 2017 assessment — a 3.52 percentage-point gain that was, per contemporaneous reporting, the second-highest positive change of any state, after Kerala. This growth reportedly continued through ISFR 2021.

The more recent ISFR 2023 (India's 18th such report, released December 2024) complicates that growth narrative for Andhra Pradesh specifically. PIB's own release on ISFR 2023 does not name Andhra Pradesh among its highlighted top-performing or largest-forest-cover states, but secondary reporting citing the report's district- and state-level change tables records two specific, less favourable AP data points for this cycle: a decline of 83.47 sq km in forest cover inside Recorded Forest Area (RFA) — the fourth-steepest such RFA decline of any state, after Tripura (116.90 sq km), Telangana (105.87 sq km), and Assam (86.66 sq km) — and a further decline of 55.19 sq km in forest cover outside RFA. Set against this, the same reporting credits Andhra Pradesh with one area of gain: a 13.01 sq km increase in mangrove cover, among the larger state-level mangrove gains nationally (behind only Maharashtra's 12.39 sq km in the same comparison, curiously listed as smaller despite the phrasing — treat the exact mangrove ranking as secondary-sourced).

On the state government's own initiative side, Andhra Pradesh launched Mission Haritha Andhra Pradesh on 5 June 2025 (World Environment Day), jointly by Chief Minister N. Chandrababu Naidu and Deputy Chief Minister Pawan Kalyan, operating under the umbrella of the state's Vana Mahotsav and "End Plastic Pollution" campaigns. The mission's stated aim is to raise the state's total green cover from a current 30.05% to 50% by 2047 — the 30.05% baseline itself broken down by the state government as 22.96% recorded forest area plus 7.09% trees outside forest. Ten million saplings were reportedly planted on the launch day itself, with a target of 55.8 million saplings across 180,000 hectares for 2025, and over 987,000 hectares targeted cumulatively for 2025–2030; progress is to be tracked through geo-tagging, drone surveillance, satellite imagery, and audit-based scoring, with real-time public dashboards.

Trap. Keep three distinct concepts apart: (1) "forest cover" as ISFR measures it — satellite-assessed tree canopy density of at least 10%, over any land parcel of at least one hectare, regardless of legal or ownership status, and which can rise or fall between assessment cycles; (2) "Recorded Forest Area (RFA)" — legally notified forest land (reserved/protected/unclassed forest), a largely stable administrative category whose internal canopy can still degrade, as AP's 83.47 sq km RFA decline in ISFR 2023 shows; and (3) the state government's own "green cover" figure (30.05%, per Mission Haritha AP), which explicitly combines recorded forest area with "trees outside forest" and is not simply a restatement of ISFR's forest-cover percentage. A question asserting that AP's forest cover has been straightforwardly rising every cycle would be misled by the ISFR 2023 RFA decline; equally, quoting the state's 30.05%/50%-by-2047 green-cover figures as if they were ISFR's own current forest-cover percentage would conflate two different metrics.

25.1 Groundwater contaminants beyond fluoride — uranium at Tummalapalle, YSR Kadapa

UPSC Civil Services Preliminary Examination 2013 asked: "Which of the following can be found as pollutants in the drinking water in some parts of India? 1. Arsenic 2. Sorbitol 3. Fluoride 4. Formaldehyde 5. Uranium — Select the correct answer using the codes given below," with the correct answer being 1, 3, and 5 only (arsenic, fluoride, and uranium are real geogenic groundwater contaminants documented in parts of India; sorbitol and formaldehyde are not). This chapter's §10 covers Rayalaseema's fluoride problem in detail but, until now, said nothing about the other two real contaminants the same question tests — and Andhra Pradesh, specifically within Rayalaseema, turns out to have a live, documented, AP-specific example of exactly this broader point: naturally occurring uranium in groundwater.

The clearest case is the Tummalapalle uranium mine, in Vemula mandal, YSR Kadapa district — one of the same four Rayalaseema districts already covered for fluorosis in §10 — operated by the Uranium Corporation of India Limited (UCIL) since commissioning in 2012 (initial investment reported at roughly ₹1,106 crore). It is among India's largest uranium deposits: an in-situ reserve of 2.51 lakh tonnes of uranium oxide was the figure given by Union Minister Jitendra Singh in a written Lok Sabha reply dated 12 March 2025 (a second, much smaller Andhra Pradesh deposit — 2,761 tonnes — exists at Koppunuru, Palnadu/Guntur district), a substantial upward revision from an original estimate of around 15,000 tonnes. Residents of villages near the mine (Tummalapalle, M.C. Palle, Kottala, Kanampalle, Rachakuntapalle, Kota, and Bhumaiahgaripalle) and a 2018 independent fact-finding visit have alleged that an inadequately lined tailings pond has allowed uranium-bearing waste to leach into groundwater — citing a fall in the local water table (reported as dropping from roughly 50–60 feet to 800–2,000 feet), white mineral deposits in borewell water, and one set of 2019 groundwater samples showing uranium concentrations as high as 690–4,000 parts per billion against a permissible limit cited in that reporting as 60 ppb. UCIL has denied responsibility throughout, and the government's official position — restated in that same 12 March 2025 Lok Sabha reply, drawing on hydro-geological studies by APPCB, IIT-Chennai, and the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre — is that "there is no discernible dissolved uranium plume emanating from the tailings pond," and that elevated uranium in the region (including upstream of the mine) reflects naturally occurring mineral deposits rather than mine-caused leaching. This is a live, unresolved, two-sided dispute, not a settled fact in either direction.

More recent and more systematic evidence that fluoride is not Rayalaseema's only geogenic water problem comes from the Central Ground Water Board's 2025 annual report (covering 1,135 wells sampled pre-monsoon 2024), which found the Rayalaseema region to be Andhra Pradesh's worst-affected for groundwater quality generally: elevated electrical conductivity/salinity and fluoride in Anantapur, elevated fluoride in Kadapa, and, notably, uranium specifically detected in Kurnool — a second Rayalaseema district beyond YSR Kadapa with a documented uranium signal — alongside salinity-intrusion issues in SPSR Nellore. Statewide, the same report found roughly 9% of sampled wells exceeding safe fluoride limits and roughly 10% exceeding safe salinity limits, with "sporadic spikes" of uranium beyond 30 ppb specifically in the state's hard-rock (granitic) terrain — the same geology that produces Rayalaseema's fluoride problem in §10, since both fluoride and uranium are mobilised from crystalline/granitic rock under similar hydrogeological conditions.

Trap. The 2013 Prelims question's real test is precision about which substances are documented Indian geogenic groundwater contaminants (arsenic, fluoride, uranium) versus plausible-sounding distractors that are not (sorbitol, formaldehyde). Do not answer a "which of these are real Indian groundwater pollutants" question with fluoride alone just because this chapter's §10 is fluoride-focused — Rayalaseema itself, per the CGWB 2025 data above, is a live example of fluoride and uranium co-occurring in the same region (and, per some reporting, even in overlapping geology), not a single-contaminant case.
The causation dispute at Tummalapalle (naturally occurring geology, per UCIL/the government's 2025 parliamentary position, versus tailings-pond leaching, per residents and a 2018 independent fact-finding report) was not resolved by any source checked in this pass and is presented here as a live, two-sided controversy — the same even-handed caution this chapter already applies to the LG Polymers death toll (§20) and the Kakinada blast toll (§23). 25.2 Seawater/saline intrusion — the national picture behind §15's Konaseema case study

UPSC Civil Services Mains 2025, General Studies Paper III (10 marks, 150 words) asked: "Seawater intrusion in the coastal aquifers is a major concern in India. What are the causes of seawater intrusion and the remedial measures to combat this hazard?" This is a direct national-level counterpart to §15's Krishna-Godavari delta material — but answering it well requires a generic causes-and-remedies framework that §15, by design, deliberately does not supply, since the one case study in §15 (Kesanapalli village, Konaseema district) explicitly attributes its saline-intrusion crisis to a specific, localised infrastructure failure (incomplete drain dredging) rather than to any of the textbook national-level causes. A candidate who only knew §15's Kesanapalli story would be unable to answer the 2025 question, which is the gap this subsection closes.

The generic, national-level causes of coastal-aquifer seawater intrusion — as distinct from, and additional to, the Kesanapalli-specific mechanism in §15 — fall into two groups. Natural causes: rising relative sea levels (climate-change-linked), tidal fluctuation and storm-surge forcing (directly relevant to this chapter's cyclone material in §12), and highly porous coastal sedimentary geology that allows easy saline percolation. Anthropogenic causes: over-extraction of fresh groundwater, which lowers the freshwater table below sea level and reverses the natural seaward hydraulic gradient, allowing saline water to migrate inland (the single most commonly cited driver); land subsidence caused by that same over-extraction, which further lowers aquifer elevation; reduced freshwater river discharge reaching the coast (due to upstream damming/diversion), which weakens the freshwater "wedge" that normally holds back seawater; and expanding brackish-water aquaculture (shrimp farming) in coastal zones, whose saline ponds can themselves leach into adjoining freshwater aquifers. Commonly cited remedial measures include: regulating and reducing groundwater extraction in vulnerable coastal zones; artificial/managed aquifer recharge (check-dams, percolation ponds, recharge wells) to rebuild the freshwater hydraulic head; subsurface physical barriers (underground/sub-surface dams) to physically block the saline wedge; conjunctive use of groundwater with surface water or treated/desalinated water to reduce aquifer drawdown; and, specifically for drainage-infrastructure-driven cases like Kesanapalli, maintaining tidal drains/sluice gates to prevent the kind of backwater intrusion pathway §15 describes.

Trap. Do not conflate the two registers. If an exam question asks generically "what causes seawater intrusion in India's coastal aquifers" (as the 2025 Mains question does), the correct answer is the national causes-and-remedies framework above — over-extraction, sea-level rise, reduced freshwater flow, aquaculture. If a question instead asks specifically about the Konaseema/Kesanapalli case in §15, none of those textbook causes is the documented one there — the study behind §15 explicitly found no local role for sea-level rise, over-extraction, damming, or aquaculture, and attributed the crisis instead to a one-off, localised dredging failure on the Sankaraguptam Major Drain. Citing the generic national causes as the explanation for Kesanapalli specifically would contradict the chapter's own source for that case.

25.3 EIA Notification 2020 (draft) vs 2006 — what the already-cited 2020 Mains question is actually testing, and its link to LG Polymers

§24.1 already correctly identifies UPSC Mains 2020 GS3's question — "How does the draft Environment Impact Assessment (EIA) Notification, 2020 differ from the existing EIA Notification, 2006?" (150 words) — as thematically close to §20's LG Polymers material. What neither §20 nor §24 actually supplies, until now, is the substance: what the EIA Notification 2006 process is, and what the 2020 draft actually proposed to change — the content the question requires to be answered, as opposed to merely cited.

Under the EIA Notification, 2006, projects requiring environmental clearance are sorted into Category A, appraised centrally by the Union Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) via its Expert Appraisal Committee, with no state-level screening stage; and Category B, appraised by the state-level State Environment Impact Assessment Authority (SEIAA), itself split into B1 (which still mandatorily requires a full EIA study) and B2 (exempted from a full EIA study). The standard four-stage process for projects requiring one is screening (categorisation, Category B only) → scoping (defining study terms of reference) → public consultation, including a public hearing (originally a 30-day public notice period under the 2006 notification) → appraisal and final clearance decision.

The draft EIA Notification, 2020 (issued for public comment in March 2020; the comment period was subsequently extended by Delhi High Court order to 11 August 2020, following litigation and public pressure) proposed several changes that attracted sustained criticism: a formal post-facto (ex-post-facto) clearance mechanism, allowing projects that had already started or been built without the environmental clearance the 2006 process required to apply for regularised clearance retroactively, rather than facing closure or prosecution; a shortened public hearing notice period, from 30 days down to 20 days; reduced compliance-reporting frequency, from the 2006 requirement of six-monthly compliance reports to an annual report; and an expanded list of project categories exempted from public consultation altogether, including, per multiple secondary analyses, reclassifying certain oil and gas exploration activities into the lighter-touch B2 category. Critics — including several analyses published in the months immediately following the draft's release — argued the post-facto clearance provision in particular would reward, rather than deter, exactly the pattern this chapter's §20 documents at LG Polymers: a plant operating for over two decades without the valid environmental clearance the 2006 notification required. The draft's public-comment window was still open when the LG Polymers leak occurred on 7 May 2020, and the incident was subsequently cited in several published critiques of the draft as a real-time illustration of what unclearanced, long-unmonitored industrial operation can produce.

Trap. Keep three distinct things separate when this material comes up: (1) the 2006 notification, which is the one LG Polymers is documented (§20) to have operated in defiance of for over two decades; (2) the 2020 draft, which was never notified as a final rule in the form described here and remains, as of the sources checked in this and earlier passes, a draft rather than the operative law — do not describe EIA clearances in India today as governed by "the 2020 notification" as though it has replaced 2006; and (3) the specific post-facto-clearance mechanism within that draft, which is the provision most directly relevant to a §20-style fact pattern and the one most likely to be the actual target of an exam question pairing this chapter's two most-cited sources (§20 and §24.1's UPSC 2020 question).
This pass could not confirm the draft EIA Notification 2020's current legal/procedural status as of the chapter's own September 2026 date (i.e., whether it has since been finalised, substantially revised, or remains pending) — the government sources needed to check this (environmentclearance.nic.in, moef.gov.in) were not successfully fetched in this pass, consistent with this chapter's recurring pattern of blocked official portals. Treat "remains a draft" as accurate only as of the secondary sources' own publication dates (2020–2022), not as independently confirmed current as of 2026.

Source: Drishti IAS, "Environmental Impact Assessment" (EIA 2006 process and category structure); Fox Mandal, "The Draft EIA Notification, 2020: What Went Wrong?" (draft timeline, Delhi High Court extension to 11 Aug 2020, post-facto clearance critique); ThinkWildlife Foundation, "Environment Impact Assessment Bill 2020 in a Nutshell" (itemised 2006-vs-2020 changes: post-facto clearance, 30-to-20-day public hearing notice, six-monthly-to-annual compliance reporting, expanded B2/exempted categories).

26. Cross-Chapter Linkages — AP as the Local Instance of National Frameworks

This chapter exists because it is the book's synthesis point: every other chapter in this sub-topic (Environment and Health) states a national framework, index, or scheme, while this one supplies Andhra Pradesh's own facts, schemes, and events. Those two layers are rarely made to meet explicitly elsewhere in this book — a national climate-vulnerability index is one chapter, AP's cyclone record is a different one; the SDG India Index is one chapter, AP's own score is a stray unresolved figure in this one; national health-financing and scheme architecture sit in three separate chapters, AP's own scheme chronology sits here. This section makes five of those national→AP links explicit, by name and figure, so that a fact already established in §1–§25 above can be correctly located within the national framework a question might actually be testing.

26.1 Climate vulnerability and cyclone exposure — Chapter 1

Chapter 1 §10 covers the Council on Energy, Environment and Water's (CEEW) Climate Vulnerability Index (CVI) (Mapping India's Climate Vulnerability: A District-Level Assessment, 26 October 2021) — the national-level finding that 27 of India's 35 states and union territories are "highly vulnerable" to extreme hydro-meteorological disasters, covering more than 80% of India's population. Within that index's own state-by-state ranking, Andhra Pradesh places second nationally, immediately after Assam, in a top five that reads Assam, Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra, Karnataka, and Bihar (Chapter 1 §10's own trap note flags this second-place ranking specifically, against the generic assumption that only India's east/north-east is most exposed). The same CEEW assessment separately finds 59% of districts in India's eastern coastal zone face extreme cyclone vulnerability, against 41% in the west — the national statistical backdrop against which this chapter's own cyclone record (Cyclone HudHud, 2014, §12; Cyclone Michaung, 2023, §12; Cyclone Montha, 2025, §12; Cyclone Arnab, 2026, §12) is not a run of unrelated weather events but the concrete, repeated realisation of a vulnerability the national index independently ranks AP second-highest on. AP's own State Action Plan on Climate Change and Human Health (SAPCCHH, §18) and the APSDMA Early Warning Dissemination System (§13) are, in this light, AP's institutional response to a risk level the national CVI already quantifies as the country's second-highest.

Chapter 1 §11 also supplies an independently-sourced, official account of Cyclone Montha's damage, worth setting directly against this chapter's own §12 account because the two draw on different source types and do not fully agree. This chapter's §12, sourced to contemporaneous press coverage, gives 38,000 hectares of standing crops destroyed and a further 1.38 lakh hectares of horticultural crops damaged, 3 human deaths, and a state-announced ex-gratia of ₹5 lakh per bereaved family. Chapter 1 §11, sourced to the Ministry of Home Affairs' own written Rajya Sabha reply of 3 December 2025 (restating the Andhra Pradesh government's own damage assessment, cross-checked on the ground by an Inter-Ministerial Central Team visiting 10–11 November 2025), instead gives 1.61 lakh hectares of agricultural area with crop loss exceeding 33%, a further 6,250 hectares of horticulture area affected, and total estimated damage of ₹6,355.60 crore — against which the state sought ₹902 crore from the NDRF, received an SDRF allocation of ₹1,449.60 crore for 2025–26 (₹1,088 crore Central share plus ₹361.60 crore State share), with a first instalment of ₹544 crore released on 27 November 2025. The two totals are broadly comparable in overall scale (roughly 1.7–1.8 lakh hectares either way) but categorise the damage very differently — the MHA figure's horticulture component (6,250 ha) is far smaller than the press figure's (1.38 lakh ha), with most of the MHA total instead booked as generic "agricultural area" crop loss. This is not a case this chapter can resolve from the sources in hand; it is flagged here as a genuine, citable discrepancy between a parliamentary/official damage assessment and contemporaneous press reporting — useful in itself as an illustration of why this chapter's recurring caution about press-only cyclone figures (see the §12 Montha and Arnab .wip notes) is warranted, and a reminder that Chapter 1's MHA-sourced figures, where they exist, are the more authoritative of the two for an exam answer specifically asking for "official"/Parliament-reported damage.

Trap. Do not cite this chapter's own §12 Montha figures as "the official government damage assessment" — they are press-sourced. The actual Government of India-reported figures (via the Ministry of Home Affairs' own Rajya Sabha reply) are in Chapter 1 §11, and differ specifically on the horticulture-damage component. If a question asks for "the Centre's/State's assessed damage," answer with Chapter 1's MHA-sourced ₹6,355.60 crore and SDRF-allocation figures, not this chapter's press-sourced crop-area figures.

26.3 Disease burden and climate-health planning — Chapter 5

This chapter's §18 documents Andhra Pradesh's State Action Plan on Climate Change and Human Health (SAPCCHH, 2022–27) as a named, AP-specific document. Chapter 5 §15 supplies the national mandate that document is the state-level output of: per NCDC's own programme page, a National Action Plan for Climate Change and Human Health (NAPCCHH) "developed in 2019 and revised in 2021" set the national framework, and — in NCDC's own words, as Chapter 5 quotes them — "every state and union territory has since developed its own State Action Plan for Climate Change and Human Health (SAPCCHH), tailored to local risk profiles." This chapter's §18 is therefore not describing an AP-specific innovation; it is describing Andhra Pradesh's compliance instance of a now-universal national requirement, prepared (as §18 itself notes) with technical support from the same National Centre for Disease Control that administers the parent NAPCCHH nationally. The match goes further than the instrument's existence: NAPCCHH's national priority areas (heat, vector-borne disease, extreme weather, air pollution, per Chapter 5 §15) line up one-to-one with the five priority areas §18 lists for AP's own SAPCCHH (air-pollution-related disease, heat-related illness, extreme-weather-event health impacts, vector-borne disease, and water-/food-borne disease).

Chapter 5 §15 also supplies a quantified national mechanism behind one of those five priority areas that this chapter's own §9 and §18 state only qualitatively. On vector-borne disease, the Lancet Countdown's 2025 India data sheet (cited in Chapter 5 §15) finds that "the average R₀ for dengue transmission by Aedes albopictus mosquitoes increased from 0.86 to 1.60 in 2015–2024, crossing from less than to more than 1" — meaning the mosquito vector's climatic suitability for sustaining dengue transmission has newly crossed the epidemic threshold nationally over the decade this chapter's own cyclone and heat-related material (§12, §14) also covers. This chapter's §9 describes AP's vector-borne disease control only as the state-level arm of the national NVBDCP, without being able to confirm any AP-specific surveillance data (its own §9 .wip box records the state's dedicated NVBDCP page as unreachable across every research pass); Chapter 5's national R₀ finding is the closest this chapter can currently get to a quantified reason why §18's SAPCCHH explicitly names vector-borne disease as one of only five state priority areas, rather than treating §9's NVBDCP coverage as a static, climate-independent programme.
