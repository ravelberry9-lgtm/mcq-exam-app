01. HISTORICAL SETTING
The Krishna and Godavari valleys, coastal plains, eastern hill ranges and interior plateau form important settings of Andhra history. River valleys supported settlements and agricultural activity; coastal routes linked settlements with maritime exchange. Amaravati and Nagarjunakonda supply important evidence from the Krishna valley. Andhra history therefore includes relationships between inland settlements, religious centres and coastal trade, rather than only a succession of rulers.

02. ANDHRA, TELUGU AND TENUGU
Andhra is an old designation found in literary and inscriptional records. In later usage, Andhra, Telugu and Tenugu are associated with the people, region and language. Concrete examples are the Andhra people-name in the Aitareya Brahmana, the Andhras in Ashoka's Major Rock Edict XIII, and the region-name Andhrapatha in the Mayidavolu plates. These examples belong to different periods and kinds of records.

03. AITAREYA BRAHMANA
The Aitareya Brahmana belongs to the Shakala tradition of the Rigveda. VII.18 names the Andhras with the Pundras, Shabaras, Pulindas and Mutibas in the narrative concerning Vishvamitra's sons. It is an early literary reference to the Andhras. The examinable connection is Aitareya Brahmana—Rigveda; the source category is literature.

Earlier in the same narrative, Rohita buys Sunahsepa as a sacrificial substitute. Vishvamitra later accepts him as a son, named Devarata.

In VII.17, Vishvamitra asks his sons to accept Devarata's seniority among them.

04. ASHOKA'S ANDHRA REFERENCE
Ashoka's Major Rock Edict XIII, of the third century BCE, names the Andhras in its discussion of dhamma. In Dhammika's translation they appear among peoples within the king's domain. The edict also discusses the suffering caused by the Kalinga conquest and the importance assigned to conquest through dhamma. This is epigraphic evidence, unlike the Aitareya literary reference.

05. TRILINGA AND DAKSHINAPATHA
Dakshinapatha is a designation associated with the southern route and southern regions. Its historical usage varies by source. In the Junagadh inscription, Satakarni is described as lord of Dakshinapatha. Trilinga is traditionally explained through three Shaiva centres—Srisailam, Draksharama and Kaleshwaram. Hanumanth Rao discusses this and other explanations of the name; the three-shrine explanation is a tradition, not a settled linguistic derivation of Telugu.

06. TYPES OF HISTORICAL SOURCES
Literary sources include religious texts, poems, genealogical works, chronicles, travel accounts and local histories. Indian works occur in Sanskrit, Prakrit, Pali, Telugu, Kannada, Persian and other languages. Archaeological evidence includes inscriptions, coins, pottery, tools, buildings, sculpture and paintings. Epigraphy studies inscriptions engraved on materials such as stone and copper plates; numismatics studies coins; palaeography studies the forms and development of handwriting and scripts. A manuscript is a handwritten copy, not necessarily the author's original.

07. EPICS AND PURANIC DYNASTIC TRADITIONS
The Mahabharata contains Andhra references, including the Sabha Parva account of Sahadeva's southern expedition. Puranas preserve dynastic lists. The Vishnu Purana, Book IV, Chapter XXIV, lists Andhra/Andhrabhritya rulers, including Krishna, Satakarni and Hala. Wilson's translation and notes also compare readings in other Puranas. These lists are important for Satavahana historical traditions; their differing names and totals require comparison with inscriptions and coins when constructing a chronology.

In Ganguli's translation, Section XXX narrates Sahadeva's southern expedition. After defeating the king named Pandrya, he proceeds farther south. The later tribute list includes the Andhras alongside other named peoples. Examination links: Sabha Parva — southern expedition — Sahadeva — Andhra reference.

The Ramayana's Kishkindha Kanda, Sarga 41, describes Sugriva sending a party south to search for Sita. Verse 12 names Andhra alongside Pundra, Chola, Pandya and Kerala. The relevant source link is Ramayana—Kishkindha Kanda—southern search—Sugriva's instructions.

08. BUDDHIST AND JAIN LITERATURE
The Suttanipata's opening to the Parayana section places the teacher Bavari on the Godavari in the Assaka–Mulaka setting. This supplies a named literary example for the early Deccan. Buddhist literature also includes Jataka narratives, teachings and accounts of monastic life. Jain literary traditions include Pampa's Kannada Adipurana and Ranna's Ajitapurana. Their religious subjects and literary settings contribute to the study of Jain culture; the relevant author–work pairs should be kept distinct.

Pampa's Kannada Adipurana narrates Rishabhanatha (Adinatha), the first Jain Tirthankara, and the story of Bharata and Bahubali. Ranna's Ajitapurana concerns Ajitanatha, the second Tirthankara. Keep these religious works distinct from their Mahabharata-based works: Pampa's Vikramarjunavijaya and Ranna's Gadayuddha.

Anguttara Nikaya 3.70 includes Assaka in a list of sixteen great countries. This is a specific Buddhist literary reference to a Deccan polity.

09. NAGARJUNA'S WORKS
Suhrllekha, or Letter to a Friend, contains 123 verses of Buddhist advice addressed to a South Indian king. It combines daily conduct with the path to enlightenment. Ratnavali, or Precious Garland, comprises 500 verses and connects Buddhist teachings with questions of governance. Both are important examples of Buddhist counsel addressed to a ruler.

10. GATHASAPTASHATI AND KAMASUTRA
Gathasaptashati is a Prakrit anthology traditionally associated with Hala. Its poems portray love, marriage and village life, including agricultural settings. Kamasutra is a Sanskrit work by Vatsyayana. The Doniger–Kakar translation places it within ancient Indian social thought and discussions of human relationships. These works broaden social-cultural study beyond royal campaigns. Their subject matter differs: an anthology of poems and a systematic treatise are not the same literary form.

11. REGIONAL LITERATURE AND PATRONAGE
Pampa wrote Vikramarjunavijaya, or Pampa Bharata, in Kannada under the patronage of Arikesari II of the Vemulavada Chalukyas. Ranna wrote Gadayuddha, also called Sahasabhimavijaya. In Telugu, Nannaya undertook the Mahabharata at Rajaraja Narendra's request in the eleventh century. Tikkana, associated with Manumasiddhi of Nellore in the thirteenth century, continued the tradition. These works connect literary activity with regional courts. Genealogical compositions such as Velugotivari Vamsavali are another source category discussed by Hanumanth Rao.

12. PERSIAN CHRONICLES
Important pairings are Amir Khusrau—Khazain-ul-Futuh; Isami—Futuh-us-Salatin; and Ziauddin Barani—Tarikh-i-Firoz Shahi. Khusrau's prose work concerns Alauddin Khalji; Isami's historical poem was completed in 1349–50; Barani's chronicle dates to 1357. Firishta's Tarikh-i-Firishta, also called Gulshan-i-Ibrahimi, includes the Bahmani kingdom and Deccan successor states, including Golconda. Together they supply literary evidence for Sultanate and Deccan political history.

13. KAIFIYATS AND THE MACKENZIE COLLECTION
Kaifiyats preserve local histories and accounts of villages, including economic, social, religious and cultural information. Colin Mackenzie's collection depended on Indian scholarly collaborators. The National Mission for Manuscripts catalogue credits the Kavali brothers: Borraiah, and subsequently Ramaswamy and Lakshmaiah. These records bring village institutions and local traditions into Andhra historical study, alongside royal records. The catalogue uses village names as entry titles.

14. MEGASTHENES, ARRIAN AND PLINY
Megasthenes wrote Indica. The original work has not survived intact; extracts were preserved by later Greek and Roman writers. Arrian also wrote an Indica, drawing on earlier accounts. McCrindle's 1877 volume brings together translated fragments of Megasthenes and the first part of Arrian's work. For the specific Andhra reference, Pliny's Natural History VI.67 describes the Andarae as possessing thirty fortified towns, 100,000 infantry, 2,000 cavalry and 1,000 elephants. These figures should be remembered as Pliny's report, not as a modern census.

15. PERIPLUS AND PTOLEMY
The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea is an anonymous first-century CE account of maritime commerce. Section 62 describes Masalia as a coastal region producing fine cotton cloth. Ptolemy's Geography is another source for ancient place names. Schoff's commentary relates Ptolemy's Maisolia to the Periplus's Masalia and associates it with the Krishna coastal region. The ancient names and their modern geographical identifications are separate parts of the evidence.

Section 50 calls the coastal region beyond Barygaza Dachinabades and explains its name through the word for south.

16. CHINESE BUDDHIST ACCOUNTS
Faxian's account describes his journey to India and Ceylon in 399–414 CE, especially Buddhist texts and institutions. Xuanzang travelled to India in the seventh century, departing in 629 and returning in 645. His Record of the Western Regions was compiled after his return. Hanumanth Rao includes these accounts, along with Yijing's writings, among sources for Buddhist history. Their strongest use here is the history of Buddhist learning, monasteries and religious practice. Xuanzang's separate Andhra and Dhanakataka descriptions are explained in sections 32–33.

17. MARCO POLO
Marco Polo's account of Mutfili describes fine textiles, diamonds and a queen praised for just government. Yule and Cordier's editorial notes identify Mutfili with Motupalli and connect the account with the Kakatiya kingdom. This is useful evidence for trade and a foreign observer's description of the region. Remember the textual name Mutfili and the identification Motupalli together.

18. VIJAYANAGARA ACCOUNTS
Niccolo de' Conti belongs to the fifteenth-century accounts. Abd al-Razzaq visited Vijayanagara in 1443 during Deva Raya II's reign; his description includes markets and court life. Domingo Paes describes the kingdom under Krishnadevaraya around 1520. Fernao Nuniz's chronicle belongs to the 1530s, under Achyutaraya. Sewell's A Forgotten Empire publishes the Portuguese accounts in translation. These observers belong to different periods, not one king's reign.

Paes describes the nine-day Mahanavami celebrations at Vijayanagara.

19. EUROPEAN COMPANY RECORDS
Hanumanth Rao includes Portuguese, Dutch, French and British records among sources for later Andhra history. Commercial correspondence, factory records and administrative documents extend the source base beyond travel narratives. Their detailed use—trade centres, Company administration and colonial changes—belongs to Unit 3. Here the distinction is between a traveller's account and records produced through continuing commercial or administrative activity.

20. TYPES OF INSCRIPTIONS
Inscriptions occur on rocks, pillars, copper plates and relic caskets. Their purposes include royal orders, grants, religious donations and praise of rulers. A land grant may name the ruler, recipient, village and privileges. A donation record may identify the donor and the institution supported. Language and script are different: Prakrit or Sanskrit is a language, while Brahmi is a script. Bhattiprolu caskets and Mayidavolu copper plates illustrate different materials and purposes.

21. ASHOKAN SITES AND BHATTIPROLU
Yerragudi and Rajula Mandagiri are important Ashokan inscription sites in the Andhra region. The published Rajula Mandagiri record contains Minor Rock Edict material in Prakrit and Brahmi. Bhattiprolu supplies a different kind of evidence: inscribed stone caskets associated with Buddhist relic deposits. The Chennai Museum guide records three such caskets found in the 1892 excavation. Their Brahmi lettering has distinctive features. Remember the contrast: Ashokan edicts convey royal messages; the Bhattiprolu records belong to Buddhist relic deposits.

Erragudi preserves Ashoka's Major Rock Edicts I–XIV and Minor Rock Edicts I–II. Major Rock Edict XIII therefore also occurs at an Andhra Pradesh site.

22. SATAVAHANA AND COMPARATIVE RECORDS
Naneghat is associated with Queen Naganika and Sri Satakarni; its record includes Vedic sacrifices and donations. Gautami Balasri's Nasik inscription praises Gautamiputra Satakarni and belongs to Vasisthiputra Pulumavi's nineteenth regnal year. These records reveal royal women as sponsors of inscriptions and religious giving. Other important pairings are Hathigumpha—Kharavela and Junagadh—Rudradaman. Rudradaman's Junagadh record is Sanskrit prose. It calls Satakarni lord of Dakshinapatha and claims that Rudradaman defeated him twice but spared him because of their close relationship. This passage supplies comparative evidence for Satavahana political history.

23. MAYIDAVOLU COPPER PLATES
The Mayidavolu grant is a Prakrit record of the Pallava prince Shivaskandavarman. Issued from Kanchipura, it addresses the official at Dhannakada. It grants Viripara in Andhrapatha to two Brahmins and specifies exemptions and privileges. Its value is concrete: a regional name, administrative communication and a land grant occur together in one document. Brahmadeya here denotes the grant made to Brahmin recipients.

24. NAGARJUNAKONDA INSCRIPTIONS
Nagarjunakonda inscriptions illuminate Ikshvaku-period religious patronage. The museum describes third–fourth-century records, mainly in Prakrit with some Sanskrit, in Brahmi. Its collection includes memorial and ayaka-pillar inscriptions. A separate museum record identifies a Sanskrit pillar inscription from the Pushpabhadraswami temple. Thus the site's evidence includes both Buddhist establishments and a Brahmanical temple; it is not a collection of only one religious or linguistic type.

The museum identifies Pushpabhadrasvamin as Shiva. The pillar records temple construction in Ehuvala Chamtamula's sixteenth regnal year and a village endowment for its maintenance.

25. COIN-MAKING AND OVERSTRIKING
Punch-marked coins receive individual symbols from separate punches. Cast coins are formed in moulds. Die-struck coins receive designs from engraved dies. Overstriking places a new design on an existing coin. CSMVS preserves a silver coin of Nahapana overstruck by Gautamiputra Satakarni. Such an object joins monetary history with political succession: the earlier issue was reused under another ruler.

26. WHAT COINS REVEAL
Coins supply the issuing ruler's name where a legend is present, along with metal, weight and symbols. The British Museum's Satakarni coin 1889,0105.938 is copper alloy and carries an elephant and other symbols, including a tree within a railing. A Vishnukundin coin in the National Mission on Monuments and Antiquities catalogue is recorded as copper. These are documented objects, not evidence that every coin of either dynasty used the same metal. Coin study supports comparisons of royal authority and monetary practice.

27. BUDDHIST BUILDINGS AND SCULPTURE
A stupa is a Buddhist sacred mound or monument, often associated with relics. A chaitya hall is a worship space; a vihara is a monastic residence. Sculpture adds narrative evidence to buildings. The Chennai Museum guide identifies Amaravati panels depicting Jataka stories, including Vessantara. The Metropolitan Museum holds an Ikshvaku-period Nagarjunakonda relief of the Buddha's descent from Trayastrimsa heaven. Such named scenes help connect art with Buddhist traditions.

The Met catalogue identifies this panel as limestone from Nagarjunakonda Stupa Site 6, dated to the late third century CE.

28. MEDIEVAL MONUMENTS
Temples and forts extend material evidence into medieval history. Ramappa, formally Rudreshwara Temple at Palampet, belongs to the Kakatiya period. UNESCO records that construction began in 1213 CE. Its carved pillars, brackets and sculptural decoration support the study of temple architecture and artistic skill. The detailed patronage and architectural discussion belongs in the Kakatiya chapter; here it is a named example of a monument as historical evidence.

The five Pancharama shrines are Amararama at Amaravati, Draksharama at Draksharamam, Somarama at Bhimavaram, Ksheerarama at Palakollu and Kumararama at Samalkota. Gandikota is a fort on the Penna (Pennar) river. These are named monument-and-place examples; their detailed patronage belongs in the relevant dynastic chapters.

29. TELUGU LANGUAGE MILESTONES
Telugu belongs to the South-Central branch of the Dravidian language family in Bh. Krishnamurti's classification. The Kalamalla inscription of Renati Chola Dhananjaya, assigned to about 575 CE, is an early complete Telugu record. Pandaranga's ninth-century Addanki inscription contains taruvoja verse. Pandaranga served the Eastern Chalukya ruler Gunaga Vijayaditya III. Yuddhamalla's Bezawada pillar records a temple and matha associated with Kumarasvamin. Its verse is discussed as Madhyakkara in the published edition, which also explains the related Dore-Akkara form. The Addanki verse demonstrates Telugu poetry before Nannaya. Nannaya, Tikkana and Errana are the Kavitraya associated with the Telugu Mahabharata.

30. TWO OTHER FOREIGN WORKS
Cosmas's Christian Topography is a sixth-century work containing descriptions of India and maritime commerce. Ibn Battuta's travel narrative is commonly called the Rihla. The Library of Congress catalogue explains that he dictated his travels to Ibn Juzayy after returning to Morocco. These author–work pairs belong to the wider foreign-source inventory.

Ibn Battuta served as a judge under Muhammad bin Tughluq in Delhi.

31. NAGARJUNAKONDA SALVAGE EXCAVATIONS
Before the dam submerged the valley, ASI conducted salvage excavations at Nagarjunakonda in 1954–60 under R. Subrahmanyam. Selected structures were reconstructed on the island and near Anupu, while excavated antiquities entered the museum. Excavation and preservation together made these remains available for historical study.

32. XUANZANG'S ANDHRA ACCOUNT
Book X of Xuanzang's record describes An-ta-lo, Andhra, separately from Dhanakataka. Beal's translation reports fertile land, cereal cultivation, twenty monasteries and about 3,000 Buddhist monks in Andhra. It also records thirty Deva temples. The capital appears as P'ing-k'i-lo; Beal tentatively identifies it with Vengi. The account therefore supplies information on agriculture and on the presence of both Buddhist monasteries and temples.

33. XUANZANG'S DHANAKATAKA ACCOUNT
The following section describes Dhanakataka. Beal's translation reports many ruined or deserted monasteries, about twenty still preserved, and roughly 1,000 monks studying Mahayana Buddhism. It also mentions one hundred Deva temples and the eastern and western monasteries called Purvasila and Avarasila. These are reported conditions of this region, distinct from the preceding Andhra section's figures. The contrast gives concrete evidence for the changing condition of Buddhist institutions alongside other religious establishments.

34. YIJING'S MONASTIC RECORD
Yijing left China in 671 CE for India and Southeast Asia. His Buddhist Monastic Traditions of Southern Asia, also known as A Record of the Buddhist Religion as Practised in India and the Malay Archipelago, describes monastic discipline and everyday life in monasteries. It adds a source on religious practice to the accounts describing places and institutions.

35. KATHASARITSAGARA: AUTHOR AND LANGUAGE
Somadeva is the author of the Sanskrit Kathasaritsagara. The GRETIL Sanskrit corpus identifies the work under his name. For source identification, retain this author–work–language combination: Somadeva—Kathasaritsagara—Sanskrit.

36. SATAVAHANA LEAD COINS
Lead was widely used in Satavahana coinage. Chennai Government Museum describes lead coins as forming a large part of its Satavahana material and also notes copper and rare silver coins. Many examples show animals or a chaitya on one face and the Ujjain symbol—a cross ending in four circles—on the other. The metals and symbols provide specific evidence for studying Satavahana coinage.

37. SATAVAHANA INSCRIPTION LANGUAGE
Most Satavahana inscriptions are conventionally described as Prakrit inscriptions. Prakrit identifies the language; Brahmi identifies the script.

38. GUNTUPALLI RECORD
The Guntupalli inscription identifies Mahameghavahana Siri Sada with the title lord of Kalinga and Mahisaka. It provides a named example of royal titles in inscriptional evidence.

39. PRASHASTI AND INSCRIPTION MATERIAL
Prashasti denotes a eulogistic composition praising a ruler or other subject. Gautami Balasri's Nasik record is a named royal example. Tamrashasana refers to a copper-plate inscription; silashasana to an inscription on stone. These terms do not all classify the same feature: praise concerns content, whereas copper and stone describe material. A record's material and purpose can be described together.

40. MANUSMRITI'S ANDHRA REFERENCE
Manusmriti 10.48 names the Andhras alongside the Medas, Chunchus and Madgus when assigning occupations. In Ganganath Jha's translation, the activity assigned to these groups is hunting wild animals. This is a specific literary reference in a Dharmashastra text.

41. NANNAYA'S MAHABHARATA CONTRIBUTION
Nannaya composed the Adi and Sabha Parvas and part of the Aranya Parva of the Telugu Mahabharata. He worked under the patronage of Eastern Chalukya Rajaraja Narendra in the eleventh century. The unfinished work was continued by later poets.

42. UNDAVALLI AND ROCK-CUT ARCHITECTURE
The Undavalli caves near Vijayawada are an example of rock-cut architecture: spaces and architectural features were carved into the rock. They provide material evidence for studying architecture, distinct from a literary account or a copper-plate grant.

43. ANDHRA PLACE-NAMES IN JATAKA NARRATIVES
In Chalmers's translation of the Bhimasena Jataka (No.80), the Little Bowman travels from Takkasila to the Andhra country for practical experience. The Serivanija Jataka (No.3) describes two travelling traders crossing the Telavaha river and entering Andhapura. These are distinct story references: Bhimasena—Andhra country; Serivanija—Andhapura and Telavaha.

44. AMUKTAMALYADA: AUTHOR AND SPEAKER
Krishnadevaraya wrote the Telugu Amuktamalyada. Its introductory dream narrative presents Andhra Vishnu of Srikakula speaking to the king and requesting a Telugu poem. The celebrated praise of Telugu belongs to the god's speech in that narrative. Distinguish the work's author, Krishnadevaraya, from its speaker, Andhra Vishnu.

45. PRAYAGA PRASHASTI AND ANDHRA HISTORY
The Allahabad (Prayaga) pillar prashasti praises Samudragupta in Sanskrit. Harisena composed it. Its southern-ruler list names Hastivarman of Vengi and Vishnugopa of Kanchi. The Vengi reference makes this record relevant to Andhra political history as well as Gupta history.

46. GUNADHYA AND SARVAVARMAN IN LITERARY TRADITION
The Kathasaritsagara's eighth chapter presents Gunadhya composing the Brihatkatha in Paisachi. Its seventh chapter links Sarvavarman with the concise Sanskrit grammar Katantra. Both occur in stories about a king called Satavahana. These are literary traditions about authors and works.

47. AL-BIRUNI'S INDIA
Al-Biruni wrote Kitab tahqiq ma li-l-Hind, commonly called Kitab-ul-Hind or India. It discusses Indian learning and religious ideas. Its fourteenth chapter surveys astronomical, mathematical and other literature. It belongs to the wider foreign-source inventory, rather than being a dedicated Andhra chronicle.

48. PUNCH-MARKED COINS AND THE MAURYAN SERIES
Punch-marked coins carry symbols impressed with separate punches. Many are silver. RBI's museum distinguishes early janapada issues from an imperial Mauryan series. The technique and the issuing period are separate facts: punch-marked coins were not confined to the Mauryas.

49. JAYAMANGALA: COMMENTARY, NOT AUTHOR
Yashodhara wrote the Sanskrit Jayamangala commentary on Vatsyayana's Kamasutra. The Doniger–Kakar translation includes extracts from this commentary. Remember three separate identities: Kamasutra—original work; Vatsyayana—its author; Jayamangala—Yashodhara's commentary.

50. TIKKANA: PATRON, CONTRIBUTION AND DEDICATION
Tikkana served Manumasiddhi of Nellore. He composed fifteen Mahabharata parvas in Telugu. His Mahabharata is dedicated to Hariharanatha. Remember the distinction: Manumasiddhi—royal association; Hariharanatha—Mahabharata dedication.
