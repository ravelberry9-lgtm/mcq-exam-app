G1-01. SOURCES AND THE RANGE OF ANDHRA HISTORY
Andhra history is not only a succession of rulers. The source types in this chapter reveal different activities. Poems bring village life and relationships into view; grants show land and administrative privileges; travel accounts describe goods and markets; sculptures represent religious narratives. Taken together, they allow a social-cultural history alongside political chronology. For example, Hala's anthology and a copper-plate grant address very different aspects of society, even though both belong to the written record.

G1-02. RELIGIOUS GIVING AND ROYAL WOMEN
Naganika's Naneghat record and Gautami Balasri's Nasik record place royal women within the public record of patronage. Sacrifice, donation and royal praise appear as connected activities. Religious giving could commemorate a ruler while supporting an institution. These examples make women's role visible through identifiable records rather than a general statement that ancient women had one uniform status.

G1-03. LAND GRANTS AND GOVERNANCE
A grant is both a transfer of resources and an administrative act. Mayidavolu illustrates the sequence: an issuing authority, an addressed official, named recipients, a village and specified privileges. This permits discussion of how royal decisions were communicated and put into effect. The document's religious beneficiary does not exhaust its historical value: its place names and instructions also illuminate administration.

G1-04. TRADE AND REGIONAL CONNECTIONS
The Periplus's textile-producing coast and Marco Polo's textile and diamond descriptions place the region within wider commercial narratives in different periods. The continuity is the importance of traded goods; the political settings and observers differ. For social-cultural history, this opens discussion of production and exchange, not just the arrival of a foreign traveller. A coin's reuse under a new ruler adds a different connection: circulating objects could survive a change of political authority.

G1-05. LANGUAGE AND COURT CULTURE
The Mayidavolu grant uses Prakrit for a royal administrative act. Nagarjunakonda's records include Prakrit Buddhist donations and Sanskrit temple records. Pampa's Kannada Adipurana, supported by the Vemulavada Chalukya Arikesari II, joins a Jain subject to regional court literature. Telugu inscriptions add another setting: Kalamalla records Telugu in the sixth century, while the ninth-century Addanki record contains Taruvoja verse. These inscriptional examples precede Nannaya's Mahabharata under Rajaraja Narendra. Later, Tikkana's association with Manumasiddhi links the continuing Mahabharata tradition with the Nellore court. Religious institutions, royal administration and courts thus supported different written practices; the evidence shows several languages and literary forms operating within Andhra's wider cultural history.

G1-06. LOCAL HISTORY AND CULTURAL MEMORY
Royal records organise history around rulers and grants. Kaifiyats bring villages and local institutions into the narrative. Monuments and sculpture add material settings and visual traditions. Combining these categories makes regional history richer: an institution can be studied through its written references and surviving material form, while village accounts retain a different scale of historical experience.

G1-07. SEVENTH-CENTURY RELIGIOUS INSTITUTIONS
Xuanzang's two regional descriptions distinguish an active monastic population from institutions that had become deserted. They also describe temples. The evidence supports a region-specific discussion of religious institutions, rather than one uniform picture of Buddhism across all Andhra sites.

G1-08. COURTS, RELIGIOUS WRITING AND VILLAGE RECORDS
Pampa's patron Arikesari II belonged to the Vemulavada Chalukyas. Pampa's Adipurana combines a Jain religious subject with Kannada literary expression. Firishta's history includes Golconda's Qutb Shahis among the Deccan dynasties. Kaifiyats shift attention from dynasties to villages: local Karanams prepared accounts gathered for Mackenzie's collection. These examples show three settings in which historical information was preserved—court-supported religious literature, political chronicles and village records.
