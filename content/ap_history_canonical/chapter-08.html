Group 2 narrative

The Vengi country between the Krishna and Godavari rivers possessed fertile delta lands, access to coastal ports, and routes linking the Deccan interior with the sea. It therefore had considerable political value. When the Badami Chalukya ruler Pulakeshin II extended his power eastward, he entrusted the government of this region to his brother Kubja Vishnuvardhana. The year 624 CE is conventionally used for the foundation of the Eastern Chalukya line. Political change, however, was gradual: Vishnuvardhana first functioned within his brother’s imperial order, while the weakening of Badami power later enabled a distinct dynasty to become established in Vengi. The kingdom was therefore the outcome of eastern imperial expansion gradually developing into regional sovereignty, not a state created instantaneously in a single year.

The Eastern Chalukyas influenced the politics of Vengi for nearly four centuries. Their charters describe the family through such identities as the Manavya gotra, Haritiputras, and the boar emblem. These belong to a language of dynastic praise through which rulers asserted antiquity, divine favour, and royal dignity. Ancient Vengipura, in the Pedavegi area, was an early centre; Vijayawada and Rajamahendravaram acquired importance in later political, donative, and temple networks. Vengi’s history becomes clearer when these are treated as changing centres of authority rather than as one permanent capital throughout the dynasty.

Group 1 analysis

The formation of the Eastern Chalukya state illustrates how an imperial province could develop into a regional kingdom. An administration initially attached to the Badami centre became a durable local monarchy amid the political changes that followed Pulakeshin’s death. Agricultural surplus in the Krishna–Godavari delta, grants to Brahmins, the expansion of temples and religious establishments, and traffic between the interior and the coast supplied a stable material base. The eulogistic portions of charters gave the dynasty a mythic pedigree, whereas their operative portions identify villages, territorial divisions, exemptions, and officials. A critical reconstruction uses both bodies of evidence while preserving the distinction between royal self-representation and administrative information.

Group 2 narrative

Political instability continued under Rajaraja Narendra, yet his court acquired a distinctive place in Telugu literary history. With his patronage, Nannayya Bhattaraka began to recreate the Sanskrit Mahabharata as Telugu poetry. Literary tradition credits him with the Adi and Sabha books and part of the Aranya book. His work was not a word-for-word translation: it reshaped the Sanskrit epic through Telugu syntax and idiom while drawing upon Sanskrit poetic technique. An inscription remembers the assistance of Narayanabhatta, placing the undertaking within an intellectual world formed by royal patronage, scholarly collaboration, and multilingual learning.

Surviving evidence establishes that Telugu was used in inscriptional prose and verse before Nannayya. Renati Chola prose records, the Addanki taruvoja, sisa verses at Kandukur and Dharmavaram, and the Vijayawada madhyakkara show an earlier inscriptional and poetic tradition. Nannayya’s distinction lies not in inventing Telugu writing but in creating the earliest extensive and artistically influential Telugu epic that survives. This foundational achievement explains his later honour as *Adikavi*. Centuries later Tikkana composed fifteen books beginning with Virata, while Errana completed the unfinished portion of Aranya; the work of all three poets formed the Andhra Mahabharata.

Group 1 analysis

Nannayya’s work united linguistic development, politics, and literary transformation. At a time of strong Chola connections, a Telugu recreation of the Sanskrit great epic gave cultural prestige to the Vengi court. His language drew extensively on Sanskrit vocabulary but integrated it with Telugu inflection, verbs, and idiom. The combination of prose and verse, narrative movement, description, and ornament offered a model for later poets. There is insufficient evidence for claims that Nannayya definitively fixed Telugu grammar or that every grammatical text attributed to him is certainly his. His most securely established contribution remains the Mahabharata composition itself.

The Renati Cholas were a regional dynasty whose core territory was Renadu, centred largely in the present Kadapa region. Although they claimed Chola descent and traced their lineage to Karikala, they were distinct from the later imperial Cholas of the Kaveri valley. The exceptional importance of the Renati Cholas lies in their Telugu epigraphic tradition. The Kalamalla record directly states that Erikal Mutturaju Dhananjaya was ruling Renadu. Several lines are damaged, so the full details of the transaction cannot be recovered, but the surviving text records the ruler, local persons and villagers in Telugu. Palaeography conventionally places it in the later sixth century, often around 575 CE; that year is not explicitly written in the inscription. Contemporary records such as Erragudipadu similarly represent an early phase in which government, grants and village relations were recorded in Telugu. They establish that Telugu functioned as a language of public inscription centuries before Nannayya.

Group 1 analysis

Reducing Kalamalla to the label “the first Telugu inscription” obscures much of its historical value. The record is damaged, and its date is assigned through palaeography rather than an internal calendrical statement. Even so, it moves beyond the occasional appearance of local words within Sanskrit records: it preserves a political and donative act in Telugu syntax. It therefore does not mark a moment when Telugu suddenly came into existence. It is surviving evidence for a stage at which an already spoken regional language entered the documentary practices of government.

The Renati inscriptions illuminate village history as well as linguistic history. They name not only rulers but also persons carrying out commands, village communities, grants, boundaries, taxes and service relationships. Inscription gave public durability to transactions linking king, official and village. Early Telugu epigraphy was not imperfect literature before Nannayya, but a documentary register with vocabulary and sentence patterns suited to local administration.

Group 2 narrative

The Renati Chola succession was not a simple, unbroken line ruling from a single capital. Read together, the Malepadu plates, the Kalamalla and Erragudipadu records and other Renadu inscriptions preserve names such as Nandivarman, his sons Simhavishnu, Sundarananda and Dhananjaya, and the later Punyakumara. Connections with places such as Nidugallu and Chirchuli suggest that branches of the same broad family exercised authority from different local centres. Dhananjaya’s early Telugu records arose within this branched political setting.

Punyakumara, a son of Mahendravikramavarman, was a prominent seventh-century Renati ruler. The Malepadu plates preserve his genealogy and its asserted link with Karikala, while other records describe him through titles including Erikal Mutturaju. Later inscriptions name rulers such as Vikramaditya, Sasikumara and Satyaditya. Pallava–Chalukya rivalry, Nolamba and Vaidumba expansion and Rashtrakuta pressure gradually altered Renati political autonomy. Chola-identified families subsequently appear at Pottapi, Konidena and Nellore, but the evidence does not permit every such house to be treated as one direct and uninterrupted branch of the Renati line.

Group 1 analysis

Renati genealogies contain two distinct levels of evidence: eulogistic ancestry and contemporary administrative information. Descent from Karikala bestowed antiquity and prestige on the dynasty; it is not a continuous generation-by-generation pedigree from the Sangam Cholas. The appearance of names and titles familiar from Pallava royalty may indicate political imitation, subordinate association or appropriation of prestige. Similarity of names alone does not establish biological descent.

The spread of dynastic branches reveals the structure of regional state formation. Power in a tract such as Renadu rested not on one capital alone but on agrarian villages, forts, routes and communities of donees. Under an expanding empire, a local ruler could continue as a subordinate; when central authority weakened, the same family might adopt more sovereign titles. Renati history was a reorganisation of branched local power under changing overlordships, not a bare succession list.

Group 2 narrative

The collective labels “Andhra Cholas” and “Telugu Chodas” do not denote one dynasty ruling a single kingdom in one period. The Renati Cholas rose in early-medieval Renadu, while the Pottapi, Konidena and Nellore Choda houses developed in different regional settings during later centuries. Several claimed descent from Karikala Chola. That claim enhanced dynastic prestige but does not prove an uninterrupted biological link among all the houses. Their actual territories are reconstructed chiefly from the villages, grants, sovereign titles and interstate relations recorded in their own inscriptions.

These Telugu Choda houses did not operate in isolation. The political world spanning Renadu, Pakanadu, Velanadu and Nellore also included the Banas, Nolambas, Vaidumbas, the Vemulavada and Mudigonda Chalukyas, the Velanati Chodas and the Kona Haihayas. Some served the Pallavas, others the Rashtrakutas, Eastern or Western Chalukyas, or imperial Cholas, and alliances changed with political circumstances. A regional chief could date records in an overlord’s reign and supply tribute or troops while retaining substantial authority over local grants, warfare and succession.

Group 1 analysis

The Andhra politics of the eleventh and twelfth centuries was more complex than a two-level model of emperor and powerless vassal. A ruler such as Velanati Gonka could profess loyalty to a Chola sovereign while leading armies, gathering other chiefs and extending authority across the Krishna–Godavari coast. The Konidena Chodas, Kona Haihayas, Kolani chiefs and Pithapuram Chalukyas secured positions in this network through warfare, marriage, grants and military service. Overlordship did not abolish local power; it recognised that power in return for allegiance.

The expression “age of mandalikas” is sometimes used for this order, but it did not mean the complete absence of central rule. Royal titles, imperial regnal years and military appointments reveal superior authority, while village grants, temple patronage, local taxes and control of forts reveal regional capacity. Cases in which the same chief acknowledged different overlords at different moments show that allegiance often followed the balance of power more than permanent dynastic sentiment.

Group 2 narrative

The Palnadu conflict arose within the regional rivalries of the twelfth-century Velanati political order. The Palnati Haihayas, centred on Gurazala and Macherla, belonged to the wider sphere of Velanati Choda power. When succession within the ruling family became disputed, ministers, local chiefs and neighbouring houses aligned with competing sides. The final battle is associated with Karempudi and is conventionally placed in the later twelfth century. Severe losses on both sides weakened not only Palnati leadership but also the wider political strength of the Velanati Chodas.

The celebrated story of Nalagama, Malideva, Nayakuralu Nagamma, Brahmanaidu and Balachandra reaches us chiefly through the *Palnati Viracharitra* and oral heroic traditions. Dramatic episodes such as the cockfight, seven-year exile, personal vows and battlefield speeches are not contemporary historical documents. They preserve the social memory through which later generations understood the war. Inscriptions can establish the presence of Haihaya rulers, temples and regional warfare in Palnadu, but they do not independently authenticate every episode in the epic narrative.

Group 1 analysis

Scholars differ over the exact year of the Palnadu battle, with dates proposed across the 1170s and 1180s. Neither 1178 nor 1182 is finally established; the evidence supports only the broader placement of the battle in the later twelfth century. Contemporary inscriptions permit reconstruction of ruling families, grants and political relations; the complete war narrative comes from later literary formation.

Although it is not a complete contemporary war narrative, the story has distinct historical value as social memory. Its succession dispute, ministerial power, Saiva–Vaishnava identities, participation of occupational communities and female political leadership reveal the values through which later Telugu society interpreted medieval politics. They are not a verbatim event list, but they illuminate ideas of justice, allegiance, caste position and heroic death. Inscriptions reconstruct rulers, grants and political relations, while the epic preserves the moral and social meanings that later society gave the war; the two bodies of evidence answer different historical questions.

Group 2 narrative

Palnadu narrative tradition presents Nalagama and Malideva as heirs born to Anuguraja through different queens. It places Nayakuralu Nagamma as Nalagama’s minister at Gurazala and Brahmanaidu as Malideva’s principal supporter at Macherla after a division of the kingdom. In this telling, the conflict became more than a succession dispute between princes: it set two political centres, ministerial groups and their warrior followings against one another. The family structure, motives and speeches, however, belong explicitly to the *Palnati Viracharitra* tradition.

The narrative links Malideva’s marriage to a Kalachuri alliance and assigns support from other regional powers to Nalagama’s side. It associates Brahmanaidu with Vaishnava devotion and social inclusion, while portraying Nagamma through administrative intelligence and political strategy. These characterisations gave later ethical meaning to a historical struggle for authority. Reducing Nagamma to a scheming villain or Brahmanaidu to a single-dimensional reformer flattens the varied forms of the tradition.

Group 1 analysis

The celebrated account of Brahmanaidu’s *chapakudu* describes a common meal across caste boundaries as an act of social equality. It has a powerful place in popular memory, but it was not a policy independently certified by a contemporary inscription. Figures such as Kannamadasu similarly suggest the incorporation of lower-status communities into military and devotional mobilisation, yet their biographies were also shaped by literary tradition. These are memories of a continuing Telugu debate over equality and inherited order, rather than transparent reports of one documented event.

Nagamma is equally important to historical analysis. Her place in the story as minister, strategist and protector of authority reveals the social possibility of imagining female political capacity within a male-dominated order. Versions that turn her into a villain may preserve the memory of the opposing camp. Palnadu tradition therefore answers “how did later society interpret the conflict?” more securely than it answers every part of “exactly what happened?”

Group 2 narrative

Nellore, called Vikramasimhapura in inscriptions, emerged as a major Telugu Choda centre in the thirteenth century. Choda Tikka intervened in the political crises of the Kaveri Cholas and extended his activity towards Tondaimandalam. Titles such as Chola-sthapanacharya, Abhinava Bhoja and Kavi-sarvabhauma expressed military service, royal patronage and learned prestige. During the reign of his son Manumasiddhi II, Nellore faced intense pressure from local rivals, the Pandyas, the Kakatiyas and the politics of the southern Chola country.

Kakatiya Ganapatideva’s military intervention was decisive during the crisis in which Manumasiddhi lost authority. Evidence including the Nayanipalli inscription confirms a political setting in which Kakatiya forces defeated the Nellore rivals Tikkana and Bayyana and restored the ruler. The further detail that poet Tikkana personally travelled as envoy to Orugallu and persuaded Ganapatideva is preserved chiefly in literary-biographical tradition. Kakatiya intervention is thus inscriptionally grounded; the poet’s embassy is a strong tradition, but not a fact recorded with equal directness in the inscription.

Group 1 analysis

Three similar names create a recurrent historical confusion. Choda Tikka was a dynastic ruler of Nellore; Tikkana Somayaji was the poet-minister associated with Manumasiddhi; and the rival Tikkana named in the Nayanipalli political conflict was another person. Unless they are separated, military actions of Choda Tikka may be assigned to the poet, or the rebel’s role may be attached to the king. Identification depends on the combined inscriptional profile supplied by title, parentage, regnal context and place.

Manumasiddhi’s restoration also shows that Kakatiya aid was not a cost-free alliance. By restoring a local ruler, Ganapatideva expanded Kakatiya influence in southern Andhra. Manumasiddhi recovered his kingdom, but Nellore entered a closer relationship with Kakatiya military power. “Restoration” was therefore not an exact return to the previous order; it produced a new political arrangement in which the ruler returned while the structure of overlordship changed.

Group 2 narrative

Tikkana Somayaji occupies a major place in thirteenth-century Telugu literature. His family belonged to a learned administrative milieu connected with the Nellore kingdom; names such as his father Kommana Amatya and grandfather Bhaskara occur in literary prefatory traditions. Tikkana’s association with Manumasiddhi’s court reflects a medieval literary world in which poetry, ministerial service and royal patronage overlapped. The dedication of his *Nirvachanottara Ramayanamu* to Manumasiddhi is direct literary evidence for that courtly relationship.

After Nannayya rendered the Adi and Sabha parvas and part of the Aranya parva, the Telugu Mahabharata remained incomplete. Tikkana left the unfinished Aranya section aside and composed the fifteen parvas from Virata through Svargarohana. Errana later completed the remainder of Aranya, bringing the combined work of Nannayya, Tikkana and Errana—the Kavitrayam—to completion. Tikkana’s work was not a word-for-word transfer; it recreated the Sanskrit epic through Telugu metre, dramatic dialogue and an idiom suited to regional literary hearing.

Group 1 analysis

Tikkana’s Mahabharata interweaves statecraft, dharma, war and human character. Rapid dialogue, syntactic patterns that expose inner conflict, and a style joining Sanskritic vocabulary with natural Telugu expression gave his poetry its distinctive force. The literary vision associated with his *Harihara-advaita* presents Saiva and Vaishnava divinity through reconciliation rather than opposition. This reconciliation was not equivalent to modern political secularism; it was the religious-literary language through which Tikkana’s age expressed the relationship between Saiva and Vaishnava traditions.

The embassy tradition expands Tikkana’s persona from poet to skilled statesman. Although it is strongly connected with the crisis of Manumasiddhi, every detail of the poet’s journey is not recorded in a contemporary inscription. By contrast, prefaces and dedications in his works provide direct evidence for court relationship, family setting and literary purpose. Tikkana’s biography therefore rests on three distinct levels of evidence: literary self-testimony, later biographical narrative, and contemporary political inscriptions.

Group 2 narrative

Under the Eastern Chalukyas, Chalukya-Cholas and Telugu Chodas, the kingdom was not a single-layered system in which the court directly administered every village. Terms such as *vishaya*, *nadu* and *sthala* denoted territorial divisions of different scales, though their meaning was not identical in every record. The numbers in names such as Vengi Fourteen-thousand, Pakanadu Twenty-one-thousand, Velanadu or Aruvelanadu Six-thousand and Sabbinadu Two-thousand were not exact population totals or a uniform count of villages. They functioned as inherited territorial and revenue designations.

At village level, officers such as the gramani, karanam and talari performed administrative work. In agraharas, mahajanas or Brahmin councils appear in the receipt of grants, land management and dispute settlement; in other villages, residents, assemblies and occupational groups also participated in recorded transactions. Although a grant might issue under royal order, local people knew its boundaries, implemented tax exemptions and witnessed the transfer. Village institutions were therefore not alternatives to kingship; they were intermediaries that converted royal decisions into locally operative arrangements.

Group 1 analysis

A land-grant inscription praised royal generosity, but it was also a document of administration. Land had to be measured; boundaries identified through trees, tanks, canals and fields; taxes or compulsory services remitted; and existing stakeholders informed. A phrase such as *sarva-kara-parihara* did not create a sovereign territory free of every obligation. It normally denoted exemption from specified royal dues on the granted land. Variation in exemption lists from one charter to another reveals the diversity of local revenue systems.

Regional chiefs formed a crucial intermediate layer. They supplied tribute and troops to a superior king while controlling forts, grants and officials within their territories. When they changed sides in a succession struggle, the effective power of the central king also changed. Medieval Andhra administration cannot therefore be reduced to either centralisation or decentralisation. It operated through distributed rights and obligations among king, mandalika, temple, Brahmin council, village officers and cultivators.

Group 2 narrative

Agriculture was the principal support of both royal revenue and ordinary livelihood under the Eastern Chalukyas. Although the fertile Krishna–Godavari delta favoured wet-rice cultivation, the economy of Vengi was more complex than delta fertility alone. In drier tracts such as Renadu, the construction and repair of tanks and the protection of cattle were crucial to village life. Paddy, sugarcane, sorghum and cotton, together with mango, coconut, betel and areca gardens, formed parts of cultivation in different local settings. Tanks supplied drinking water as well as irrigation, making their maintenance a continuing responsibility of rural society.

Land also served as a means by which rulers rewarded service. Alongside grants to Brahmins, temples and Jain establishments, assignments to officials and military servants created claims upon village revenue. Charters recorded the village, boundaries, recipient and exemptions, and therefore reveal how royal authority over agrarian resources was exercised. A grant by itself, however, did not keep cultivation functioning: cultivators' labour, cattle, water and the cooperation of local officers remained indispensable. Relations among the king, beneficiaries, officials and cultivators converted agricultural surplus into revenue.

An inscriptional land grant was not automatically equivalent to modern absolute private ownership. Whether the soil itself, a fiscal claim, or specified revenue rights were transferred depends on the vocabulary, exemptions and boundary clauses of each charter. Service grants indicate a redistribution of rural surplus through officers, warriors and religious institutions, but they do not by themselves prove a single, simultaneous process of “feudalisation” across every locality. Evidence for tanks, cattle conflicts, salt-producing centres and gardens shows instead a varied economy in which delta agriculture, dry-zone water management, pastoral wealth and coastal resources complemented one another.

Group 2 narrative

Agricultural surplus did not remain confined to the village. Grain, salt, garden produce and manufactured goods moved from local markets to urban centres and beyond. Merchants organised trade through associations; inscriptions contain institutional names such as nakara and leadership titles such as sresthi. Pekkandru is associated with itinerant merchant bodies, but each occurrence belongs to its own chronological and regional setting rather than automatically to one uniform organisation. Monetary terms such as dramma, gadyana, mada, ruka and chinnam illuminate exchange, taxation and gifts, though their metal and relative value did not necessarily remain constant.

The Cheruvu Madhavaram copper plates provide a concrete example of merchant participation. Issued under Vishnuvardhana V, the charter connects land obtained for a Saiva foundation at Vijayavata with a merchant body. Merchants therefore took part not only in buying and selling goods but also in property transactions, religious gifts and the making of urban institutions. Land supplied produce or revenue to the foundation, while merchant participation gave economic and social support to the transfer. Commerce, landed resources and religious patronage thus met within the same local system.

Group 1 analysis

The appearance of a monetary term in an inscription does not by itself prove a fully monetised economy. A tax or fine expressed in a monetary unit might in practice have been discharged in coins, grain or other commodities. Likewise, nakara and pekkandru indicate organised commerce, but their membership, jurisdiction and interregional reach could vary. The value of the Cheruvu Madhavaram charter lies in demonstrating a specific relationship between merchants and a Saiva establishment; it does not establish a general claim that temples controlled all trade in Vengi.

Group 2 narrative

Eastern Chalukya charters clearly reveal the relationship among land, administration and religious institutions. When a ruler granted a village or plot to a Brahmin, temple or religious community, the charter recorded boundaries, fiscal rights, exemptions and the officials expected to implement the order. Royal commands required officers at territorial and village levels, headmen and boundary witnesses before they could operate upon local resources. A charter was therefore not merely a eulogy of royal generosity; it was also an administrative instrument.

Temples were more than places of worship. Land, cattle and income assigned for lamps, offerings and ritual connected priests, attendants, suppliers and cultivators in a continuing service system. Queens, officials, merchants and local donors joined kings in supporting such establishments. Yet temples did not all possess equal resources or exercise identical authority over villages; the rights and services in each record belonged to its own local setting.

Group 1 analysis

The society visible in charters is largely the society recorded from the standpoint of donors, beneficiaries and officials. Cultivators seldom speak directly, although their labour was indispensable to the income conveyed by a grant. Boundary clauses, exemptions and warnings to future rulers show that claims over land required definition and protection. A temple could simultaneously be a sacred centre, a property-holder, an organiser of service and a repository of local memory, but the strength of each role varied with time, locality and endowment.

The religious history of the Eastern Chalukyas cannot be told solely as a history of Siva temples. Vishnuvardhana III's Musinikunda charter supplied village revenue to a Jain establishment at Vijayavata, while copper plates of Amma II also record courtly support for Jain basadis. Group 2 narrative

Siva worship in Vengi expanded through ascetics, teachers, disciples and priests as well as through temple construction. Names such as Pasupata, Kalamukha and Kapalika designate distinct currents within Saivism, but all three were not necessarily present in every temple. A local association becomes secure when an inscription identifies a particular lineage or teacher. Land, lamp oil, flowers, offerings, remuneration and service rights were necessary for worship to continue. A Saiva establishment therefore functioned as a living institution joining donors, teachers, priests, artisans, suppliers and cultivators around the deity.

The growth of Saiva and Vaishnava institutions did not cause Jain establishments to disappear at once. Large gifts to Siva temples, royal grants to Jain basadis and support for Vishnu worship could coexist. Competition may have occurred, but every gift is not evidence of religious conflict. Donors expressed devotion, family memory, political status and local relationships through endowment. Vengi's religious history was therefore a process in which expansion, coexistence and competition for patronage operated together, not a simple replacement of one religion by another.

Group 1 analysis

Descriptions of Saiva sects in later texts do not apply unchanged to earlier inscriptional settings. While commemorating public acts, gifts or reputation, these records used regional metrical forms in the inscriptional medium. They demonstrate an established metrical and public use of Telugu poetry before Nannayya.

The tenth-century Gangadhara–Bommalagutta record of Jinavallabha extends this development. Its multilingual Sanskrit, Kannada and Telugu setting includes kanda verses in Telugu, generally treated as the earliest presently known Telugu examples of that metre. Since Jinavallabha was the brother of the Kannada poet Pampa, the inscription connects Telugu expression with a Jain and multilingual Deccan literary world. The tradition that identifies Malliya Rechana as the author of *Kavijanasrayam* is also important, though debates over the work's authorship and date remain under scholarly discussion.

Group 1 analysis

The expression “first Telugu verse” is relative to the presently recoverable and datable evidence. The earliest presently recoverable, datable inscriptional example does not mark the birth of the metre itself; an earlier oral or manuscript tradition may have existed. Records from Addanki to Gangadhara reveal stages by which regional metres entered public inscriptional language. Nannayya was therefore not the absolute beginning of Telugu poetry but a decisive moment at which a prior tradition acquired the scale of a court-supported epic. The Gangadhara inscription also demonstrates that Telugu, Kannada and Sanskrit operated together within the same scholarly environment.

Group 2 narrative

Learning in the Eastern Chalukya age was not concentrated in one uniform school system. Personal study under teachers, Brahmanical centres, ghatikas and scholarly circles attached to temples and Jain institutions provided different channels of transmission. Land grants supported learned recipients and helped sustain the study of the Vedas, grammar, logic and literature. Narayanabhatta, who assisted Nannayya in the Andhra Mahabharata project, was praised for multilingual learning. Their collaboration places the Telugu epic within a world shaped by Rajaraja Narendra's patronage, scholarly cooperation and contact between Sanskrit and Telugu, rather than treating it as the isolated labour of one poet.

The *Sarasangraha Ganitamu* associated with Pavuluri Mallana is an important instance of scientific writing in Telugu. It is related to the Jain mathematician Mahaviracharya's Sanskrit *Ganitasarasangraha*, although calling Mallana's work a merely literal translation understates its adaptive character. By expressing calculation, measurement and practical problems in Telugu verse, it extended literary usage beyond myth, religion and royal praise into scientific knowledge. Mallana's identity and date remain debated; the evidence places the tradition only within the broad Eastern Chalukya–Chola cultural milieu and does not establish him as a securely dated contemporary of Rajaraja Narendra.

Group 1 analysis

The appearance of the word ghatika does not establish an identical university, curriculum and administration at every site. Charters usually record endowment, learned recipients and lineages of teachers, not classroom practice. Narayanabhatta's role is evidence for the linguistic and scholarly collaboration required by the epic, not a replacement for Nannayya's authorship. Pavuluri's mathematical tradition reveals the movement of knowledge across languages and the adaptation of a technical work to regional verse, while the chronological gap between surviving manuscripts and the presumed original leaves dating and authorship unresolved.

Group 2 narrative

Court culture was made not only by poets and scholars but also by musicians, singers, dancers and actors. A grant tradition associated with Chalukya Bhima I names the musician Chellavva. The comparison of her father Mallappa's musical ability with that of the mythical musician Tumburu belongs to the language of praise; it indicates the family's artistic reputation rather than offering a literal historical measurement. The tradition that Chellavva received landed support is especially important because it records a woman performer as a recognised beneficiary of royal patronage.

Support for performance extended beyond a single personal reward. Courts supplied settings for ceremony, while temple worship and festivals created recurring occasions for music and dance. Endowed land or revenue could sustain performers, singers and service personnel. Support given to individual artists and resources assigned to temple service together indicate a wider institutional setting for performance. Although inscriptions do not provide a complete history of specific performance forms, they clearly reveal an institutional connection among art, religious service and landed income.

Group 1 analysis

A land assignment to a performer does not automatically establish absolute private property in the modern sense. The original wording determines whether it conveyed personal enjoyment, remuneration for service, family maintenance or a share of revenue. Nevertheless, the recording of Chellavva brings women into history not only as royal donors but also as professional artists recognised for skill. Temple festivals provided settings for performance, but the surviving evidence does not establish a specific dance form or service community for this period without a direct record.
