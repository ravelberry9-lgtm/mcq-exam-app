C07-01

దక్కన్ రాజవంశం, ఆంధ్ర ప్రాంతం

A Deccan dynasty and the Andhra country

గ్రూప్ 2 పాఠ్య వివరణ

క్రీ.శ. ఐదవ–ఆరవ శతాబ్దాల్లో తూర్పు దక్కన్‌ రాజకీయ చరిత్రలో విష్ణుకుండినులు ప్రముఖ శక్తిగా ఎదిగారు. వారి తొలి అధికార పరిధి నేటి తెలంగాణ అంతర్భాగంతో సంబంధం కలిగి ఉండగా, తరువాతి శాసనాలు కృష్ణా–గోదావరి ప్రాంతాల వరకూ వారి అధికారం, దానవ్యవస్థ విస్తరించినట్లు తెలియజేస్తాయి. అందువల్ల విష్ణుకుండినుల చరిత్ర ఒకేచోట స్థిరంగా ఉన్న రాజ్య కథ కాదు; అంతర్భాగపు కేంద్రాల నుంచి తీరాంధ్ర వైపు విస్తరించిన రాజకీయ ప్రక్రియగా కనిపిస్తుంది. ‘విష్ణుకుండిన’ అనే వంశనామాన్ని వినుకొండతో అనుసంధానించే అభిప్రాయం ఉన్నప్పటికీ అది నిర్ధారిత వ్యుత్పత్తి కాదు. అలాగే అమరపురం, ఇంద్రపురం, శ్రీపర్వతం వంటి శాసన స్థలనామాలను ఆధునిక ప్రదేశాలతో సరిపోల్చడంలో పండితుల మధ్య ఏకాభిప్రాయం లేదు. శాసనం లభించిన ప్రదేశం, శాసనంలో పేర్కొన్న రాజకేంద్రం, రాజ్యపు మొత్తం భూభాగం—ఇవి మూడు వేర్వేరు చారిత్రక ప్రశ్నలు.

గ్రూప్ 1 విశ్లేషణ

విష్ణుకుండినుల కాలక్రమాన్ని నిర్మించేటప్పుడు చరిత్రకారులు శాసనాల్లోని రాజ్యవర్షాలు, వంశావళులు, వివాహ సంబంధాలు, సమకాలీన రాజవంశాల ప్రస్తావనలు, లిపిశైలి వంటి ఆధారాలను పరస్పరం కలుపుతారు. అయితే ఒకే పేరుగల రాజులు పలుమార్లు రావడం, కొన్ని వంశక్రమాలు మధ్య తరాలను మాత్రమే ఇవ్వడం, అన్ని శాసనాలకూ స్వతంత్రంగా నిర్ధారించగల శక సంవత్సరం లేకపోవడం వల్ల ఖచ్చిత తేదీల విషయంలో భిన్న పునర్నిర్మాణాలు ఏర్పడ్డాయి. తుమ్మలగూడెం వంటి తెలంగాణ అంతర్భాగపు శాసనాలు, ఈపూరు–పొలమూరు వంటి ఆంధ్ర ప్రాంతపు శాసనాలు కలిసి రాజవంశపు విస్తృత కార్యక్షేత్రాన్ని చూపుతాయి; కాని వాటి లభ్యస్థానాలను వరుస రాజధానులుగా ప్రకటించడానికి అవే సరిపోవు. ఇదే జాగ్రత్త శ్రీపర్వతస్వామి, ఇంద్రపురం వంటి పేర్లకూ వర్తిస్తుంది: అవి రాజాధికారానికి, మతపోషణకు ముఖ్యమైన సూచనలు అయినప్పటికీ వాటి భౌగోళిక గుర్తింపుకు స్వతంత్ర పురావస్తు, స్థలనామ ఆధారాలు అవసరమవుతాయి.

Group 2 narrative

The Vishnukundins emerged as a major power in the political history of the eastern Deccan during the fifth and sixth centuries CE. Their earliest sphere of authority was connected with the Telangana interior, while later inscriptions reveal the extension of their power and endowment network towards the Krishna–Godavari region. Their history is therefore not that of a kingdom fixed at a single centre, but of a political formation that expanded from inland centres towards coastal Andhra. Some scholars have connected the dynastic name “Vishnukundin” with Vinukonda, but this is not a proven etymology. Nor is there scholarly agreement on the modern identification of inscriptional place-names such as Amarapura, Indrapura and Sriparvata. The place where an inscription was found, the royal centre named in it and the entire territory of the kingdom are consequently three different historical questions.

Group 1 analysis

The Vishnukundin chronology is reconstructed by correlating regnal years, genealogies, marriage alliances, references to contemporary dynasties and palaeography. Exact dates nevertheless vary among modern reconstructions because royal names recur, some genealogies preserve only selected generations and not every charter contains an independently fixed era date. Inscriptions from the Telangana interior, such as Tummalagudem, and those from Andhra localities, such as Ipur and Polamuru, together demonstrate a wide field of royal activity; their findspots alone do not prove a succession of capitals. The same caution applies to names such as Sriparvatasvamin and Indrapura. They are important evidence for royal legitimacy and religious patronage, but their geographical identification requires independent archaeological and toponymic support.

C07-02

రాగి శాసనాలు, వంశావళి చర్చ

Copper plates and the disputed genealogy

గ్రూప్ 2 పాఠ్య వివరణ

విష్ణుకుండినుల రాజకీయ చరిత్ర ప్రధానంగా రాగిరేకు దానశాసనాల్లో భద్రపడింది. తుమ్మలగూడెం, ఈపూరు, పొలమూరు, రామతీర్థం, చిక్కుళ్ల శాసనాలు దానాల వివరాలతో పాటు వంశంలోని వేర్వేరు తరాలను తెలియజేస్తాయి. తుమ్మలగూడెం మొదటి శాసనంలో ఇంద్రవర్మను గోవిందవర్మకు తాతగా, మాధవవర్మను తండ్రిగా పేర్కొన్నారు. తుమ్మలగూడెం రెండో శాసనం మరింత విస్తృతమైన క్రమాన్ని ఇస్తుంది: గోవిందవర్మ తరువాత పదకొండు అశ్వమేధాలు చేసిన మాధవవర్మ, వాకాటక మహాదేవికి జన్మించిన విక్రమేంద్రవర్మ, అతని కుమారుడు ఇంద్రభట్టారకవర్మ, చివరగా శాసనాన్ని జారీ చేసిన విక్రమేంద్రభట్టారకవర్మ కనిపిస్తారు. మాధవవర్మ, విక్రమేంద్రవర్మ వంటి పేర్లు వంశంలో మళ్లీ రావడంతో ఆధునిక చరిత్రకారులు వారిని మొదటి, రెండో అని సంఖ్యలతో వేరు చేశారు; ఆ సంఖ్యలు అసలు శాసనాల్లోని రాజనామాల్లో భాగం కావు.

ఈ శాసనాల వంశావళులు కుటుంబ సభ్యుల జాబితాలు మాత్రమే కావు; ప్రతి రాజు అధికారానికి చట్టబద్ధత కల్పించే విధంగా ఎంపిక చేసి రూపొందించిన రాజప్రశస్తులు. గోవిందవర్మను బౌద్ధ ధర్మంపై విశ్వాసం కలవాడిగా, అనేక మహావిహారాల స్థాపకుడిగా రెండో తుమ్మలగూడెం శాసనం కీర్తిస్తుంది. అతని కుమారుడు మాధవవర్మను పదకొండు అశ్వమేధాలు సహా అనేక వైదిక యజ్ఞాలు చేసిన సార్వభౌముడిగా చూపుతుంది. మాధవవర్మ కుమారుడైన విక్రమేంద్రవర్మ వాకాటక మహాదేవికి జన్మించినవాడనీ, మహాకవీ, పరమసౌగతుడనీ పేర్కొంటుంది; తరువాత ఇంద్రభట్టారకవర్మ సైనిక విజయాలను, బాల్యంలోనే మంత్రిమండలి రాజ్యభారం అప్పగించిన విక్రమేంద్రభట్టారకవర్మ ధర్మాధారిత రాజత్వాన్ని ప్రశంసిస్తుంది. ఈ వర్ణనలు వంశంలో బౌద్ధపోషణ, వైదిక రాజయజ్ఞాలు, వాకాటక వివాహబంధం, సైనిక విజయం అనే వేర్వేరు చట్టబద్ధతా మూలాలను ఒకే కుటుంబ స్మృతిలో కలుపుతాయి. అయితే ప్రశస్తిలోని దిగ్విజయాలు, సార్వభౌమత్వపు వాదనలు రాజకీర్తి భాషలో ఉన్నందున వాటిని స్వతంత్ర ఆధారాలతో పోల్చి మాత్రమే రాజకీయ భూభాగంగా పునర్నిర్మించాలి.

Group 2 narrative

The political history of the Vishnukundins is preserved chiefly in copper-plate land grants. The Tummalagudem, Ipur, Polamuru, Ramatirtham and Chikkulla records provide details of donations while preserving different generations of the royal family. The first Tummalagudem charter names Endravarman as Govindavarman’s grandfather and Madhavavarman as his father. The second Tummalagudem charter supplies a longer sequence: Govindavarman was followed by Madhavavarman, credited with eleven Asvamedhas; then came Vikramendravarman, born to a Vakataka great queen; his son Indrabhattarakavarman; and finally the issuing king Vikramendrabhaṭṭārakavarman. Because names such as Madhavavarman and Vikramendravarman recur, modern historians distinguish their bearers by adding numerals such as I and II. Those numerals are editorial conventions and are not part of the royal names in the inscriptions.

Group 1 analysis

These genealogies are not neutral family lists; they are royal eulogies selectively composed to legitimise each ruler. The second Tummalagudem charter praises Govindavarman for his faith in the Buddhist teaching and for founding numerous great monasteries. It presents his son Madhavavarman as a universal sovereign who performed many Vedic rites, including eleven Asvamedhas. Madhavavarman’s son Vikramendravarman is described as born to a Vakataka great queen, a great poet and a devoted Buddhist; the record then celebrates the military victories of Indrabhattarakavarman and the dharmic kingship of Vikramendrabhaṭṭārakavarman, to whom the council of ministers entrusted power while he was still a child. Buddhist patronage, Vedic royal sacrifice, a Vakataka marriage alliance and military success are thus combined as distinct sources of legitimacy within one dynastic memory. Claims of universal conquest and sovereignty in a eulogy must, however, be compared with independent evidence before they are converted into a map of actual political control.

C07-03

గోవిందవర్మ, విహార పోషణకు దానం

Govindavarman and a monastery endowment

Group 2 narrative

Govindavarman’s Tummalagudem charter, issued on the full-moon day of Vaisakha in his thirty-seventh regnal year, records the donation of the two villages Embudala and Penkapara to the monastery of his chief queen. The grant was intended to provide lamps, incense, perfume, flowers, banners, drink, food, beds, seats and medicine for the sick, as well as the repair of damaged parts of the establishment. This was therefore not a single act of constructing a building and abandoning it; it assigned resources for the continuing worship, daily life, medical care and physical maintenance of the monastic community. Fiscal privileges and exemptions attached to the villages supplied the economic foundation for that support.

Group 1 analysis

The grant transferred more than cultivated land. The beneficiary institution received treasures above and below the ground, the royal share of produce, periodical dues, income from punitive taxes and rights connected with corvée labour. By prohibiting entry into the two villages by spies, mercenaries, envoys, courtiers and king’s men, the charter limited the intervention of the ordinary royal apparatus. Royal authority did not disappear, however: future kings of the Vishnukundin lineage were instructed to protect and augment the endowment, and religious imprecations equated its violation with the five great sins. The charter thus combined a transfer of revenue, administrative immunity and religious merit to give the monastery long-term institutional support.

The second Tummalagudem charter states that Paramabhattarika Mahadevi was born in the Prithvimula royal family and that her marriage into the Vishnukundin house established a dynastic alliance between the two ruling families. Group 1 analysis

The later king’s grant to the monastery founded by Paramabhattarika shows that the queen’s patronage outlived her and became part of the dynasty’s official memory. The eulogy’s emphasis on her birth in the Prithvimula family and her marriage connection with the Vishnukundins suggests a political relationship through which the prestige of the two houses reinforced one another. The inscriptional genealogy presents the reign of Madhavavarman as a decisive phase in Vishnukundin political expansion. The second Tummalagudem copper-plate charter praises him as the performer of eleven Ashvamedhas, Bahusuvarna and Paundarika rites, a thousand kratus, the Sarvamedha, Rajasuya and Purushamedha. Rites such as the Ashvamedha and Rajasuya portrayed the king as a paramount ruler entitled to claim authority over an extensive realm; the same eulogy’s claim that he ruled the land from the Reva to the eastern, southern and western seas belongs to this political idiom. The dynasty connected military success with an ideal of universal rule, but such praise does not define fixed borders on a modern map. The Vakataka marriage functioned within the same construction of royal prestige by providing an honoured maternal connection. The charter’s further description of Vikramendravarman as a great poet and a devout Buddhist shows marriage alliance, courtly learning and Buddhist affiliation converging in the representation of a single prince.

Group 2 narrative

After Madhavavarman, the genealogy proceeds through Vikramendravarman, Indrabhattarakavarman and Vikramendravarman II. The Tummalagudem charter praises Indrabhattaraka as a warrior who obtained imperial dominion through numerous elephant combats; this is a royal eulogy expressing his military reputation. The same record explicitly states that his son Vikramendrabhattarakavarman was entrusted with the burden of kingship by the council of ministers while still a child. Succession therefore rested not only on hereditary right but also on the support of ministers and leading members of the court who could sustain a young ruler’s authority. The king later issued the charter after weakening a Pallava ruler named Simha and returning first to Sakrapura. The beneficiary, however, was the Paramabhattarika Mahavihara at Indrapura, Sakrapura is therefore the place to which the king returned, whereas Indrapura is the location of the beneficiary monastery; the charter uses them as distinct place-names. Identifying this Pallava Simha with a particular numbered Pallava king is likewise a later reconstruction, not an explicit statement of the charter.

Group 1 analysis

The account of Vikramendravarman II’s accession reveals the role of the prakriti-mandala in Vishnukundin government. The expression was broader than a modern cabinet; it denotes the body of ministers, political notables and supporters who upheld the king. Entrusting sovereignty to a child preserved dynastic legitimacy, while the later victory over a Pallava gave the ruler a personal reputation for martial success. Read together with the village grant to the Indrapura monastery, these details show royal authority resting on three supports: hereditary entitlement, political backing at court, and royal prestige constructed through victory and benefaction. The charter’s closing identification of Srimularaja as the officer responsible for the order, praised for restoring his overlord’s declining fortune through policy and valour, further demonstrates the importance of senior functionaries in sustaining the kingdom.

Group 2 narrative

Vishnukundin charters declare that the rulers obtained authority to protect their subjects through devotion at the feet of Sriparvatasvamin. The designation itself is explicit, but scholars have not agreed on either the deity intended or the location of Sriparvata. Some connect it with the Buddhist sacred landscape of Nagarjunakonda-Sriparvata, while others propose a Shaiva identification; neither should therefore be presented as settled fact. The inscriptions do show, however, that the dynasty’s religious life was not confined to one tradition. Madhavavarman is celebrated for major Vedic sacrifices, whereas Govindavarman is described as devoted to the Buddha’s teaching and as the founder of many great monasteries; Vikramendravarman is likewise called a devout Buddhist. A later king renewed support for the monastery founded by Queen Paramabhattarika. Vedic ritual, Buddhist institutional patronage and the personal devotions of royal family members thus coexisted within the history of the same dynasty.

Group 1 analysis

This plurality cannot be understood fully through a simple division in which one king was Buddhist and another Brahmanical. A ruler’s personal devotion, the sacrificial language that legitimated sovereignty, and the institutions receiving state-supported endowments operated at different levels. Govindavarman’s charter opens with praise of the Buddha and grants villages to the monastic community, yet the same dynasty incorporated protection of social orders and Vedic sacrifice into its royal image. The second Tummalagudem charter records a gift to a Buddhist monastery while describing protection of the donation as the duty of kings who follow shruti, smriti and established conduct. Religious traditions therefore did not function as sealed, mutually exclusive worlds; they intersected in royal patronage, family memory and the construction of political legitimacy.

Its attention to both syllabic and moraic forms reveals different ways in which rhythm was measured in Sanskrit prosodic analysis. Yet forms described as regional or current do not by themselves prove the existence of an already independent Telugu literary corpus. The surviving text, its commentary, manuscript history and inscriptions bearing royal titles provide separate bodies of evidence; only their convergence can support firm conclusions about date, authorship and regional literary affiliation.

The political field of the eastern Deccan did not become empty at a single moment when Vishnukundin power weakened. Regional houses, including the Ranadurjaya family centred on Pishtapura, expanded their authority; the Tandivada and Gollavalli records illuminate the rule of Prithvimaharaja of this line. Group 1 analysis

The nature of the evidence is central to interpreting this transfer of power. Rock-cut sanctuaries around the Krishna preserve the region’s religious art in material form. At Undavalli, halls, pillars and chambers were excavated from the living rock. The large reclining Vishnu image defines a principal sacred space within the complex. In rock-cut construction, pillars, walls and ceilings are not separately assembled components but interconnected parts of a single mass; sculpture, architecture and worship therefore meet within one spatial arrangement. Official promotional pages, however, give conflicting accounts of the date of Undavalli’s phases and of the original religious affiliation of each level. The conflicting accounts do not provide a firm basis for assigning the entire complex to one ruler or one century.

The Mogalrajapuram caves at Vijayawada provide clear evidence of Shaiva sculptural practice. The official district account identifies images of Ardhanarishvara, Nataraja and Vinayaka at the site. Ardhanarishvara integrates Shiva and Parvati within one bodily form, while Nataraja presents Shiva as lord of dance. These images give the surviving sanctuary a definite Shaiva character, but any claim that one is the “earliest” example in South India requires comparative art-historical, stylistic and chronological evidence. The caves are clear evidence of the evolving rock-cut tradition of the Krishna valley, but the surviving evidence does not assign every one of them categorically to Vishnukundin patronage.

The two sites did not follow one architectural programme. At Undavalli, superimposed halls, pillars and chambers, together with the large reclining Vishnu, formed a complex setting for Vaishnava worship. These contrasting architectural and iconographic programmes show that the Krishna valley’s rock-cut tradition was neither uniform nor confined to a single religious form. Physical details such as four levels, five sanctuaries and surviving images establish the character of the sites, but they do not supply a precise date or a named Vishnukundin patron for every building phase. The caves are therefore important evidence for the artistic environment of the Vishnukundin age, while a claim that every component was commissioned by a Vishnukundin ruler would exceed the surviving evidence.
