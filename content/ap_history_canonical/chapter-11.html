Group 2 narrative

At the beginning of the fourteenth century, the political order of southern India changed rapidly. Older kingdoms such as the Kakatiyas, Hoysalas and Pandyas weakened, while campaigns of the Delhi Sultans disturbed the balance of power across the Deccan and the south. It was within this crisis that Harihara and Bukka built a new centre of authority on the Tungabhadra. Their kingdom did not appear fully formed in a single event. It expanded into the Vijayanagara Empire by gradually bringing forts, cultivated zones, trade routes and military leaders into a wider political system. The capital site survives today as the archaeological landscape of Hampi in Karnataka's Vijayanagara district; the modern district boundary must not be confused with the historical empire's extent. The year 1336 is conventionally given as its foundation date. Even before Hampi became an imperial capital, the locality possessed sacred importance as the landscape of Pampa and Virupaksha; the emerging kingship could therefore associate itself with an older holy centre.

Group 1 narrative

The available traditions do not give one uncontested account of Harihara and Bukka’s earlier political lives. One tradition discussed by Hanumantha Rao connects them first with Prataparudra at Warangal and then with the kingdom of Kampili, whereas another places them within the establishment of the Hoysala ruler Ballala III. The well-known tradition that Vidyaranya inspired the foundation also acquired an important place in later historical memory. These accounts help us understand how the founders’ background was remembered, but a single contemporary record does not establish all of them as a sequence of proven appointments and events. Vijayanagara’s emergence was not the sudden result of one person’s command, but a historical process made possible by political crisis, local military power, a sacred centre and the defensible geography of the Tungabhadra region.

No single text provides a complete history of Vijayanagara. Group 2 narrative

Krishnadevaraya was succeeded in 1529 by his brother Achyutadevaraya. The empire did not collapse immediately under Achyuta; military action continued on its southern and Andhra frontiers. Competition among royal relatives, the Salakam chiefs and Aliya Rama Raya nevertheless destabilised the court. After Achyuta’s death in 1542, his son Venkata was briefly placed on the throne, but Rama Raya’s faction prevailed in the succession struggle. Group 1 narrative

While Sadasiva remained the formally recognised ruler, Aliya Rama Raya—the son-in-law of Krishnadevaraya—gained decisive control over the army, appointments and diplomacy. Rama Raya intervened in wars among the Deccan Sultanates of Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda and their neighbours through frequently changing alliances. For a time, this policy used their divisions to extend Vijayanagara influence. Repeated intervention, however, turned former partners into enemies and increased the possibility of a broad coalition against Vijayanagara. The distinction between Sadasiva as the crowned king and Rama Raya as the effective holder of power is essential to understanding this period.

Group 2 narrative

The battle fought at Rakshasi-Tangadi in 1565 is also commonly called the Battle of Talikota. A coalition of the Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda and Bidar Sultanates confronted the Vijayanagara army led by Rama Raya. The conflict had no single cause. Rama Raya’s interventions in Deccan politics, rivalry over the Raichur region, shifting alliances and remembered political humiliations together formed the background to the coalition. Rama Raya was captured and killed during the battle, causing the command of the Vijayanagara army to collapse. Hampi was then subjected to plunder and destruction that gravely damaged the court, markets, residential areas and many monuments.

The destruction of Hampi did not, however, abolish the Vijayanagara state on the same day. Rama Raya’s brother Tirumala withdrew towards the south-east with the surviving royal family, treasury and forces, and eventually made Penukonda a new centre of power. Aravidu rulers later attempted to continue imperial authority from Andhra centres such as Penukonda and Chandragiri. The loss of Hampi severely damaged central institutions and networks of revenue and exchange, while also giving distant nayakas greater freedom. The year 1565 marked the fall of a metropolis and a decisive acceleration of imperial fragmentation, not the instant extinction of the dynasty.

Group 1 narrative

The Raichur doab, the western-coast horse trade and military assignments to amara-nayakas were not separate elements of Vijayanagara power. Revenue from productive land sustained cavalry and the forces of commanders; imported horses increased capacity in open warfare; and control of forts protected both agrarian income and commercial routes. Rama Raya’s Deccan policy operated within this same competition for resources. The defeat of 1565 was neither simply the victory of a religious coalition nor the result of one commander’s error. It resulted from the interaction of military resources, frontier politics and a reorganisation of alliances.

The continuation of Aravidu kingship after 1565 demonstrates that a capital, a dynasty and effective imperial control were not identical. Destruction of Hampi’s royal enclosure, ceremonial system and great markets weakened both the prestige of central authority and its ability to concentrate resources. Rulers could nevertheless claim rights over territory, taxation and military service from centres such as Penukonda, Chandragiri and Vellore. Their ability to enforce those claims gradually declined because of increasingly autonomous regional commanders, expansion by Bijapur and Golconda, and succession disputes. Vijayanagara’s decline was thus not a moment completed by one battle, but a prolonged political process in which the centre moved, obligations weakened and regional states acquired independence.

Group 2 narrative

Land revenue was the principal income of the Vijayanagara state, but taxation was not confined to agriculture. Hanumantha Rao names pullari on grazing, maggari and pinjasiddhaya connected with looms and textile work, ganugari on oil presses, illari on houses, and chakrakanike associated with pottery. Commercial dues such as sthala-adaya at market places and marga-adaya on the movement of goods along routes drew revenue from exchange. These names do not constitute an empire-wide schedule imposed at one uniform rate. Demands varied with local production, occupation, agreements and relations of authority.

Taxpayers did not always accept government demands silently. Merchants and occupational communities could object collectively to excessive dues. Hanumantha Rao associates Krishnadevaraya with the removal of marriage-related levies called gudikalyanam and kalyanakanike, and Aliya Rama Raya with remitting certain barber taxes after a petition by a barber named Kondoju. Initial concessions to newly cultivated settlements reveal the state’s interest in receiving revenue after production had been allowed to grow. Petitions, collective resistance, negotiation and remission therefore formed part of the history of taxation alongside coercive collection.

Women did not share one uniform experience in Vijayanagara society. Queens and other royal women donated villages, ornaments and support for ritual services, thereby asserting a place within temple and political institutions. Gangadevi gave literary form to military victory in the *Madhuravijayam*, while writers such as Tirumalamba demonstrate women’s participation in courtly learning. Hampi expanded in a planned manner among the Tungabhadra River, granite hills and natural boulder formations. Hills and lines of boulders provided natural defence, while connected walls, gateways and watch posts controlled entry. The extensive capital was not a compact settlement enclosed by one wall; several defended zones linked sacred centres, the royal enclosure, residential quarters, cultivated land and suburban settlements. River and rainwater were directed through channels, tanks, wells and stepped reservoirs for drinking, gardens, rice fields and royal use. The integration of natural landscape with defence, water control and agriculture formed the basis of Hampi’s metropolitan stability.

The city’s sacred, commercial and royal centres were distinct but interconnected. Long bazaar streets before the Virupaksha and Vitthala temples show that pilgrims, merchants and festival chariots shared the same spaces. During ceremonies at the Mahanavami platform in the royal enclosure, armies, regional commanders, envoys and tribute were displayed before the ruler. Reliefs at the Hazara Rama temple combine Ramayana narratives with elephants, horses, soldiers and ceremonial processions. These structures were not merely beautiful monuments: they transformed imperial devotion, economic wealth, social rank and political authority into experiences visible to participants.

If Hampi’s architecture is treated only as an expression of royal taste, the city’s political economy disappears from view. Group 2 narrative

During the sixteenth century, Telugu prabandha adopted inherited stories from the Puranas and epics and developed them through poetic invention, elaborate description, characterisation and the culture of the court. In the *Manucharitramu*, Allasani Peddana transformed the story of Svarochisha Manu from the Markandeya Purana into an extensive poem. Nandi Timmana reworked the story of Krishna, Satyabhama and Rukmini in the *Parijatapaharanamu* through the languages of love, devotion and courtly relationship. Dhurjati’s *Srikalahastimahatmyamu* joined a sacred-place tradition with Telugu prabandha composition. Although the underlying stories were already known, each poet established individuality through the manner of expansion, emphasis on particular characters and description of scenes.

Group 1 narrative

Telugu literary memory firmly preserves a tradition that groups Peddana, Timmana, Dhurjati, Madayagari Mallana, Ayyalaraju Ramabhadrudu, Pingali Surana, Tenali Ramakrishna and Ramarajabhushana as the “Ashtadiggajas.” Their lifetimes, dedications and patrons, however, make it difficult to establish that all eight sat together at one time in Krishnadevaraya’s Bhuvanavijaya assembly. Peddana and Timmana are closely and clearly connected with Krishnadevaraya’s court, whereas poets such as Surana and Ramarajabhushana also belonged to the world of later sixteenth-century regional patrons. The Ashtadiggajas are therefore best understood not as an exact attendance list for one day at court, but as a literary tradition symbolising the achievement of Telugu prabandha in the Vijayanagara age.

The development of Telugu prabandha did not end with the works of Peddana and Timmana. Pingali Surana extended Telugu narrative form and verbal technique in remarkable ways. Group 2 narrative

The Vijayanagara period had no single state school system offering one curriculum to everyone. In learned households, pupils studied grammar, Vedic texts, philosophy and poetry with teachers; monasteries transmitted their religious and philosophical traditions; and temples supported knowledge required for recitation, music, accounts and ritual. Kings, regional chiefs and other donors supplied scholars with land, money and honours that sustained their work. Texts passed between generations through copying palm-leaf manuscripts, preserving them and reading them with a teacher’s commentary. Access to learning depended strongly on social status, family tradition and connection with an institution, yet knowledge was produced in many centres from the royal court to village agraharas.

Group 1 narrative

Music, dance and instrumental performance formed part of daily temple service as well as royal festivals. Singers, musicians, dancers and organisers of ritual performance could receive support in land, grain, cash or shares of offerings. Annamacharya’s Telugu compositions gave sung form to devotion at Tirumala, while the Kannada Haridasa tradition associated with Purandaradasa and Kanakadasa carried devotional and philosophical ideas to broad audiences through accessible songs. Temple paintings and sculptures presented related sacred narratives visually. Writing, recitation, music, dance and painting were distinct arts, but during festivals they worked together to transform sacred narrative into a shared public experience.

Supplementary historical context

After Venkata II’s death, dynastic succession conflict culminated in the battle of Toppur, where Yachama Nayaka supported the line represented by Rama Deva. Venkata III and Sriranga III later faced expanding pressure from Bijapur and Golconda. The year 1646 may be treated as a major territorial crisis for Vijayanagara political power, but it did not instantly extinguish every dynastic claimant. A hereditary claim continued for some time even as the capacity to enforce it across distant regions steadily disappeared.

Supplementary historical context

The cultural system of sixteenth-century Vijayanagara continued in new forms within the southern Nayaka kingdoms. At seventeenth-century Thanjavur, the courts of Raghunatha Nayaka and Vijayaraghava supported Telugu and Sanskrit literature, music and dance. Learned women and authors such as Ramabhadramba and Madhuravani belonged to this courtly world. This was not an unchanged copy of Hampi’s culture; migrant Telugu households, local Tamil traditions and new royal patronage combined to reconstruct Vijayanagara legacies in a regional form.
