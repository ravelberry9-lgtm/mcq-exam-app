Group 2 narrative

The conquest of Orugallu by the armies of the Delhi Sultanate in 1323 ended Kakatiya dynastic rule. Capturing a capital, however, was not the same as establishing durable government throughout its former territories. Distance from Delhi, the burden of maintaining armies in the Deccan, resistance by local chiefs and the wider rebellions of Muhammad bin Tughluq’s reign weakened the new authority. Leaders, landed families and fort commanders connected with the Kakatiya military-administrative order consequently built centres of power in regions where they possessed local resources. The Musunuris emerged in the forested Godavari uplands, the Reddys in coastal Andhra, and the Recherlas around the fortified zones of Telangana.

These kingdoms were not the product of one permanently united liberation movement. Local chiefs sometimes cooperated against Sultanate authority, yet also fought one another for forts, fertile land and commercial routes. The formation of the Bahmani kingdom in 1347 introduced another major power into Telangana politics, while Vijayanagara expansion and later Gajapati intervention repeatedly altered regional alliances. The post-Kakatiya age was therefore not a political vacuum; it was a period in which older relations of power fragmented and were reorganised into new regional states.

Group 1 analysis

The fall of the Kakatiya state can be understood as a dispersal of sovereignty across several levels rather than merely a change of dynasty. Sultanate representatives could claim towns, forts and revenue centres, but the collection of agrarian income, protection of routes and mobilisation of troops still required regional cooperation. Chiefs whose power already rested on land, forts, military followers and kinship networks used this weakness to establish new states. These formations did not completely break with the older order; they recombined resources, offices and relationships created within the Kakatiya political world under new dynastic identities.

Records of the successor states often praise their rulers as restorers of *dharma*. Such language recalls the disruption caused by conquest while presenting the new ruler as capable of restoring grants, Brahmanical institutions and temple service. A eulogy, however, also constructs political legitimacy. “Restoration” therefore contains both administrative action and an argument presenting the donor’s authority as worthy of social recognition.

Group 2 narrative

Musunuri Prolaya Nayaka built his authority in the forested and hilly tract near the Godavari, with Rekapalle remembered as his political centre. His Vilasa grant portrays him as a ruler who restored peace and Brahmanical grant rights disrupted by invasion. The record does more than register a village donation: it supplies a moral justification for his newly established power. Although contemporary information about his reign is limited, the named grant provides firm evidence for Musunuri authority in the Godavari region.

Kapaya Nayaka subsequently extended Musunuri power into inland Telangana and recovered Orugallu during the 1330s. His titles announced authority over Andhra and its fortresses, but they do not measure uninterrupted direct control over every region. Relations initially shaped by accommodation with the Bahmanis later turned into warfare, while regional competitors such as the Recherlas grew stronger. Kapaya’s defeat and death in the struggle with Recherla Anapota near Bhimavaram around 1368 ended the principal Musunuri power centred on Orugallu.

Group 1 analysis

The careers of Prolaya and Kapaya do not form a simple linear dynastic history. Prolaya’s power rested on the Godavari landscape, forest routes and local followers; Kapaya’s recovery of Orugallu allowed him to appropriate the political legacy of the former Kakatiya capital. Titles such as “lord of Andhra” and “Sultan of Andhra” were assertions of regional sovereignty, not evidence of uniform government over the territory of the modern state.

Kapaya’s changing relations with the Bahmanis reveal the contingent character of medieval alliances. An accommodation formed against one danger could become a conflict over forts and frontiers in another phase. Bahmani pressure alone did not cause Kapaya’s decline: the rise of the Recherlas, the independent interests of regional chiefs and the military cost of sustaining an enlarged domain interacted. Musunuri rule was therefore a bounded regional attempt to reconstruct sovereignty after conquest, not merely a heroic liberation narrative.

Group 2 narrative

Anitalli’s Kaluvacheru record of 1423 is an important retrospective source for the political changes after the Kakatiyas. It praises Prolaya and Kapaya as leaders who restored the Telugu country and associates Kapaya with seventy-five nayakas. The record was produced nearly a century after the events it describes. It is therefore not an eyewitness report of the 1320s, but a document showing how a fifteenth-century ruling family remembered the past and connected its own position with that history.

Anitalli’s appearance as the donor is equally significant. Her genealogy, donation and remembrance of earlier rulers occur within the same document, showing that women shaped dynastic history not only through marriage but also by making grants and recording political memory. The figure of seventy-five nayakas is not a fully verified contemporary list; it conveys a later memory of a broad chiefly following around Kapaya.

Group 1 analysis

The historical value of Kaluvacheru lies not in eliminating its chronological distance but in explaining the political memory created by that distance. Fifteenth-century successor states used a narrative of Kakatiya collapse, local resistance and restoration when explaining their origins. It praised earlier leaders while giving later ruling families a legitimate historical inheritance. Its sequence of events becomes clearer only when compared with earlier grants such as Vilasa, securely dated inscriptions and records produced by competing dynasties.

Anitalli’s role also reveals the gendered construction of political memory. She appears not merely as the wife of a male ruler but as the person issuing a donation. Although her donor voice was framed through scribes, scholars and dynastic convention, the document formally recognised the property transfer while preserving public memory of her lineage. Female patronage could therefore unite religious merit, property, family honour and political history.

Group 2 narrative

The Recherla chiefs, described in historical writing as Padmanayakas or Velamas, rose from the Kakatiya military-political world. After the end of Kakatiya rule, they became an independent power based on Telangana’s forts, routes and agrarian zones. Singama Nayaka appears as an early major figure in this formation, while Rachakonda and Devarakonda developed into key centres under his successors. Hills, natural defences and constructed fortifications gave them military value; revenue from surrounding villages and control over routes sustained their political power.

Recherla history was not confined to warfare. The Rachakonda court also supported Sanskrit scholarship, drama and poetics. The *Rasarnavasudhakara* associated with Singabhupala and the activities of scholars such as Vishveshvara and Shakalya Malla indicate that a fortress-capital could also function as an intellectual centre. Revenue needed for war and literary patronage used to display political prestige belonged to the same courtly order.

Group 1 analysis

Rachakonda and Devarakonda reveal the importance of geography to a regional state. A fort was not merely a defensive wall; it housed troops, stored grain and weapons, accommodated officials and served as a centre for collecting income from surrounding villages. Branches of one family operating from two forts indicate divided responsibility, but common dynastic interests and military cooperation linked them. This arrangement could preserve lineage power through one centre when the other came under pressure.

Placing the Recherlas within a fixed religious-political bloc oversimplifies their history. They cooperated or fought with Bahmani and Vijayanagara powers as circumstances changed and competed with the Reddys and other Telangana chiefs. Defence of forts, succession, territory and temporary advantage often mattered more than a permanent religious alignment. Court literature meanwhile represented the ruler as learned connoisseur, warrior and donor, adding cultural honour to military authority.

Group 2 narrative

After Singama Nayaka died in the conflict around Jallipalli, his sons Anapota and Mada reorganised Recherla power. Anapota developed Rachakonda into a strong fortress-centre, while Mada’s branch operated from Devarakonda. Anapota’s defeat of Kapaya near Bhimavaram in the later 1360s ended Musunuri supremacy at Orugallu and enlarged Recherla influence. Although responsibilities were divided between two centres, military cooperation continued when external enemies threatened the family’s position.

Recherla alliances did not remain fixed in later generations. Cooperation with the Bahmanis appears under Kumara Singama, alongside competition with Vijayanagara and the Reddys. When Bahmani support went to a rival claimant in a Kondavidu succession dispute under Anapota II, the Recherlas sought Vijayanagara cooperation. Mada’s intervention again brought Bahmani pressure. By the period of Sarvajna Rao Singama and Linga Nayaka of Devarakonda, Gajapati intervention and Bahmani campaigns had narrowed independent Recherla territory. This was not uninterrupted rule over all Telangana, but an effort to preserve fortress-based authority across a changing frontier.

Group 1 analysis

The existence of two fortress-centres was both a strength and a vulnerability. Rachakonda and Devarakonda mobilised resources from different regions and supplied a wider military base, but coordination could weaken if the interests of collateral branches diverged. Stability depended not only on a ruler’s ability but also on the distribution of responsibility within the family, the loyalty of regional commanders and the movement of information and supplies between forts.

Cooperation with Bahmani or Vijayanagara power did not represent permanent allegiance. A power that served as an ally in one phase could become an enemy when it supported a rival claimant in another. Defence of forts, succession disputes in neighbouring states such as Kondavidu, and control of routes between Telangana and coastal Andhra shaped these alignments. Recherla decline was consequently not the result of one invasion; frontier pressure from stronger neighbours, internal succession arrangements and the continuing cost of warfare gradually restricted their independence.

Group 2 narrative

The Reddy kingdom became a major regional power in coastal Andhra after the fall of the Kakatiyas. Prolaya Vema Reddy established authority during the 1320s with Addanki as an early centre; Kondavidu subsequently developed into the principal fortress. Strongholds such as Kondavidu were well placed to control routes between the coastal plains and the interior. Agrarian income, military followers and relations with local chiefs formed the foundations of the new state. It was not the automatic successor to the entire Kakatiya realm, but one among several regional powers that consolidated a particular territory.

Vema Reddy associated his rule with the restoration of order and *dharma* as well as military success. Support for Brahmanical institutions, temple services and approaches to pilgrimage centres such as Srisailam contributed to this public image. Errana, who completed the remaining portion of the *Aranyaparva* and composed the *Harivamsamu* and *Nrisimhapuranamu*, was connected with his patronage. Forts supplied physical security, while endowment and literary patronage supplied cultural legitimacy.

Group 1 analysis

Geography, revenue and political memory interacted in Vema Reddy’s state-building. Addanki stood near agrarian zones and routes linking coast and interior, whereas elevated Kondavidu offered military security. Possession of a fort alone was insufficient: revenue from surrounding villages and exchange through markets supplied its troops with provisions, pay and weapons. A wide structure of rural production therefore sustained the fortress-state.

Errana’s patronage was more than a ruler’s private literary taste. Continuing the Telugu Mahabharata tradition begun by Nannaya expanded a language of *dharma*, polity and dynastic honour. The representation of Vema as donor, protector of pilgrimage and patron of learning presented the new kingdom as ordered authority after post-Kakatiya disruption. Although eulogy and everyday government were not identical, the image shows how the state explained itself to society.

Group 2 narrative

When Anapota Reddy succeeded Prolaya Vema, the kingdom had to protect the wealth of the coast while responding to pressure from neighbouring powers. The renewal of the Motupalli charter associated with his reign demonstrates concern for merchant access, the safety of cargo and customs. Protecting a port was not merely a concession to traders; it helped the state collect duties and assert sovereignty over the coast. Coastal agriculture, maritime exchange and guarded routes towards Kondavidu and Addanki formed an interdependent economy.

Under Anavema Reddy, influence expanded towards the Krishna-Godavari region. New territory supplied additional revenue, forts and routes, but demanded greater military resources. The ruler governed this enlarged domain by delegating responsibility to relatives, commanders and local chiefs. Delegation assisted expansion, yet the growth of independent military and fiscal resources among regional officers also created conditions for later succession conflicts.

Group 1 analysis

The Motupalli charter was not a modern free-trade policy. By regulating practices that allowed the seizure of property from ships in distress and by announcing specified duties, the ruler reassured merchants while strengthening his own fiscal and judicial authority over the port. Security, customs and sovereignty were inseparable. The commodities named in the record establish long-distance exchange but do not independently identify the precise origin of every item or the total volume of trade.

Anavema’s expansion illuminates relations between centre and region. Victories were recorded in the ruler’s name, but authority depended on the troops who conducted campaigns, officers who held forts and intermediaries who mobilised local revenue. Delegation gave the state territorial reach; it also allowed collateral branches and commanders to acquire interests that could weaken the centre during a disputed succession.

Group 2 narrative

Kataya Vema became a leading commander and administrative partner under Kumaragiri Reddy. Their campaigns extended Reddy influence along the northeastern coast, and Rajamahendravaram developed into an important centre. Dynastic marriages, grants and the distribution of offices connected the new region with the kingdom. Expansion did not, however, give every branch of the ruling family identical interests. Claims to forts, revenue and succession intensified competition.

As Pedakomati Vema strengthened his position at Kondavidu, a distinct branch associated with Kataya Vema formed around Rajamahendravaram. Allada Reddy and later Virabhadra became important in this eastern succession. Both centres invoked Reddy political tradition but did not always obey the same authority. The Kondavidu line ended under Rachavema in the 1420s, whereas the Rajamahendravaram line continued amid changing alliances into the middle of the fifteenth century.

Group 1 analysis

The relationship between Kumaragiri and Kataya Vema reveals the double character of delegated power. Entrusting troops, territory and administrative responsibility to an able commander enabled rapid expansion. Success, however, gave that commander local followers, revenue and marriage ties that could sustain a separate regional authority. When central power weakened, delegated command could become a hereditary branch.

The emergence of Rajamahendravaram was more than a family quarrel. Agriculture in the Godavari delta, coastal routes and political connections towards Kalinga gave the centre its own strategic value. Division consequently arose from resources, distance and regional military needs as well as personal ambition. Despite a common dynastic name, Kondavidu and Rajamahendravaram formed different alliances and developed into competing political systems.

Group 2 narrative

Pedakomati Vema tried to consolidate authority at Kondavidu while competing with the Rajamahendravaram branch. Continuing warfare burdened state resources, yet his court became known for scholarship and for its patronage of Srinatha. These activities were not contradictory: a ruler seeking martial prestige also fashioned an image as hero, donor and connoisseur through literature. Fiscal pressure, rivalry with the eastern branch and intervention by neighbouring powers including Vijayanagara nevertheless weakened Kondavidu.

Rachavema consequently inherited a kingdom in crisis. The Kondavidu kaifiyat states that burdensome levies, including a “birth tax,” made him unpopular and that Savaram Ellayya killed him. A kaifiyat is a later compilation of local history, and every detail in this account lacks contemporary inscriptional confirmation. The birth-tax episode is valuable as political memory but not as a securely preserved administrative order. What is clear is that Kondavidu Reddy power fragmented during the 1420s and its territories became objects of external competition.

Group 1 analysis

Pedakomati Vema’s reign shows that war and literary patronage drew upon the same political economy. Revenue maintained armies and forts while also supporting poets, scholars and religious institutions. Patronage did not remove the crisis, but it legitimised the ruler and gave the court regional distinction. Srinatha’s mobility further demonstrates that poets were not necessarily permanent employees of one king; intellectuals moved among competing courts in search of honour and support.

The kaifiyat turns Rachavema’s fall into a moral story in which oppressive taxation produces popular vengeance. It may preserve a memory of economic discontent, while also reflecting the way a later community justified a ruler’s fall. Royal titles and grants in inscriptions present one image, whereas local memory in the kaifiyat presents another. Their divergence is itself evidence for a critical reconstruction of Kondavidu’s final phase.

Group 2 narrative

The post-Kakatiya political map contained smaller centres of authority alongside the better-known Musunuri, Reddy and Recherla states. Records of the Koppula and Manchikonda chiefs in the Godavari region reveal power based on local forts, village revenue and family followers. Because the frontiers of larger states remained unstable, these chiefs sometimes asserted independence and at other times entered subordinate relations with a stronger neighbour. Their histories demonstrate that regional politics did not operate from capitals alone.

The Kandukur branch of the Reddys formed its own centre in southern coastal Andhra. Like the larger Reddy courts, its rulers supported religious and literary activity; Shivalinga Reddy is associated with the commentary tradition of the *Tattvaprakashika*. Regional branches were therefore more than names in a genealogy. Through grants, learned connections and temple patronage, smaller states fashioned cultural identities for their authority.

Group 1 analysis

The limited survival of evidence for minor chiefdoms does not indicate limited historical importance. Inscriptions of major kingdoms foreground conquest, while a local record may preserve only a village grant, temple service or family succession. Together, such fragments show that relatively small geographical resources—a river crossing, fort or tract of cultivated land—could sustain regional power.

The Koppula, Manchikonda and Kandukur houses were not merely occupants of gaps between large kingdoms, but intermediate powers that negotiated with them. A change of allegiance was not necessarily simple betrayal; it could be an adjustment intended to preserve military protection, dynastic succession and local revenue. Because of these intermediate powers, the reach of Kondavidu or Rajamahendravaram authority varied from region to region.

Group 2 narrative

Agriculture supplied the principal economic foundation for the military and cultural activity of the Reddy states. River water supported the coastal plains of the Krishna-Godavari region, while tanks and canals extended cultivation inland. Surplus from grain and commercial crops reached the state, local officers and religious institutions through taxes and customary dues. It maintained fort garrisons, royal households and administrative personnel. The upkeep of tank embankments, canals, roads and pilgrimage routes connected local production with markets, forts and ports.

Ports such as Motupalli and urban and rural markets enabled the exchange of agrarian surplus, textiles, craft goods and imports. Customs supplied state income, while merchants required secure routes and protection for cargo. Grants to temples and Brahmans assigned the revenue of specified villages to new beneficiaries. Landholders, cultivators, pastoralists, artisans, merchants, service groups and religious specialists depended upon one another within this system; the court was only one powerful part of a wider economic world.

Group 1 analysis

A ruler did not create land when making a grant; he reorganised claims over the revenue of an inhabited village with cultivators, water resources, taxes and services. When a donation carried exemption or revenue transfer, a share formerly claimed by the state passed to a temple, agrahara or individual beneficiary. The donor gained merit and prestige, while the institution acquired a continuing resource; the authority claiming a share of cultivators’ production could nevertheless change. Grant inscriptions are therefore sources for the distribution of surplus as well as for religious history.

Agriculture and commerce did not form separate worlds. Rural production supplied markets, while urban merchants and ports brought salt, metals, aromatics and luxury goods inland. Customs might be recorded in money even while agrarian payments continued in grain, goods or service. Coin, barter and payment in kind coexisted. Economic growth did not distribute benefits equally; when warfare, famine or additional taxation intervened, producers and service groups were likely to carry a disproportionate burden.

Group 2 narrative

Reddy patronage was not confined to one shrine or religious tradition. Srisailam, Ahobilam, Simhachalam and other centres received donations, services and facilities for pilgrims. Rulers often expressed Shaiva devotion, yet Vaishnava shrines and teacher lineages also obtained support. The religious associations of rulers and commanders such as Kumaragiri and Kataya Vema reflect this plural patronage. A grant expressed devotion while also gaining recognition for royal authority within the society connected to a shrine.

Temples and monasteries were more than places of worship. They mobilised land, grain, money and specialised labour for ritual, recitation, teaching, feeding, festivals and accommodation. Donors, priests, merchants, artisans, musicians and dancers came together during festivals, but not as social equals. Differences founded on occupation, caste, property and access to royal patronage continued within temple and village society.

Group 1 analysis

Teacher lineages transmitted religious knowledge while building durable relations among donors, disciples and institutions. Revenue assigned to a teacher or monastery could support instruction, worship and resident disciples; association with a respected teacher gave a ruler religious prestige and access to local followers. Contemporary inscriptions and later literary memories concerning lineages such as the Ghodaraya tradition are not equivalent evidence, yet together they reveal the networked character of patronage.

Support for Shaiva and Vaishnava institutions by the same political elite was neither perfect religious harmony nor merely cynical performance. Personal devotion, merit, family tradition, regional support and control of pilgrimage routes could all shape a grant. Because religious institutions administered land, income, employment and status as well as spiritual authority, they formed part of state formation.

Group 2 narrative

The Telugu Mahabharata was not composed in a single period. Nannaya left the *Aranyaparva* incomplete in the eleventh century; Tikkana composed fifteen later books beginning with the *Virataparva* in the thirteenth; and Errana completed the remaining *Aranyaparva* portion in the fourteenth. The three poets were not contemporaries in one court. Their work, produced in three different political worlds, came together as the Telugu Mahabharata tradition. Errana’s *Harivamsamu* and *Nrisimhapuranamu* also belonged to the religious-literary setting of the early Reddy rulers.

Srinatha formed relationships with several regional patrons rather than remaining within a single court. The *Shringara Naishadhamu* displays courtly style, the *Haravilasamu* develops Shaiva narrative, and the *Bhimeshvara Puranamu* celebrates a regional shrine. Pothana’s Telugu *Bhagavatamu* belongs to the wider devotional world of the fifteenth century, while Gaurana’s dvipada narratives demonstrate the importance of storytelling, devotion and ethics outside ornate court poetry. Telugu literature was produced across shrines, smaller courts, merchant patronage and networks of scholars.

Group 1 analysis

The three Mahabharata poets exhibit linguistic continuity as well as historical change. None simply reproduced the inherited Sanskrit epic word for word; selection, expansion and compression responded to Telugu metres, audiences and the literary methods of each period. “Translation” was therefore creative reconstruction. Errana’s status under the Reddys marks a phase in which Telugu had become a prestigious medium for epic, Puranic and political knowledge.

Treating Srinatha, Pothana and Gaurana as one courtly list obscures their different social locations. Srinatha’s career reveals a scholar moving through a competitive world of patrons; Pothana represents another conjunction of devotion and poetry; and Gaurana employed the rhythm of dvipada, closer to oral reception. Their different forms show that the community for Telugu literature extended beyond royal courts.

Group 2 narrative

Srinatha’s career demonstrates the breadth of fifteenth-century patronage. The *Shringara Naishadhamu* was dedicated to Mamidi Singana, and Srinatha held a learned-administrative connection with Pedakomati Vema’s court. Merchant patrons such as Avachi Tippaya and other regional chiefs also supported him. The *Haravilasamu*, *Kashikhandamu*, *Bhimakhandamu* and *Shivaratri Mahatmyamu* connected different shrines, sacred narratives and patron groups through Telugu literature. A poet was not simply an employee bound to one king, but an intellectual who moved among courts, shrines and wealthy donors through learning and reputation.

Vinnakota Peddana discussed Telugu poetics in the *Kavyalankara Chudamani*, while Anantamatya’s *Bhojarajiyamu* joined narrative with ethical instruction. Gaurana expanded dvipada storytelling through the *Harishchandropakhyanamu* and *Navanatha Charitra*. Authorship of the urban *Kridabhiramamu* remains disputed between Vallabhamatya and Srinatha. Even without settled authorship, its streets, arts and entertainments remain valuable as a literary memory shaped by ornament and exaggeration.

Group 1 analysis

The poet-patron relationship was more complex than a salaried king and a praising poet. Dedication could give the author security, gifts and courtly access, while granting the patron a reputation for generosity, learning and political distinction. Non-royal patrons such as the merchant Avachi Tippaya show commercial wealth entering literary production. Mobility among competing courts enlarged a poet’s audience, although the political language of praise was not a direct report of historical fact.

Disputed authorship does not make a literary source useless; it defines the limits of its use. Manuscript history, colophons and stylistic comparison may yield different answers about the *Kridabhiramamu*. Its occupations, performances and social encounters nevertheless reveal an imagined and experienced city. They are not census data or an administrative report, but evidence for the literary construction of urban life.

Group 2 narrative

Sanskrit scholarship retained prestige beside Telugu literature in Reddy and Recherla courts. Tradition associates Kumaragiri with the dance treatise *Vasantarajiyamu* and Kataya Vema with the *Kumaragiri Rajiyamu*, a commentary on plays of Kalidasa. Rulers such as Pedakomati Vema and Singabhupala of Rachakonda were remembered not only as patrons but as learned authors. These attributions do not all possess equal contemporary confirmation, yet they show that the ideal ruler was expected to value poetry, drama, music and dance alongside warfare.

Spring festivals turned courtly splendour into public performance. Poets, musicians, dancers and other artists participated while the ruler appeared as donor and connoisseur. Lakumadevi became famous in later literary and artistic memory as an accomplished dancer associated with Kumaragiri. Although every biographical detail about her is not equally contemporary, the tradition reflects a historical setting in which women performers could gain recognition and patronage at court.

Group 1 analysis

A treatise on dance was not a code that completely governed performance. It classified bodily movement, aesthetic emotion, music, stage practice and known dance forms in learned language. A gap could exist between the categories of a text and every performance at court. Festivals, patronage of artists and textual attributions nevertheless show interaction between scholarly theory and living performance.

Remembering a ruler as author or learned theorist also formed part of political representation. A knowledgeable king could be portrayed as one who understood *dharma*, protected art and supplied cultivated government. Establishing authorship is therefore one historical problem, while the tradition associating a work with a ruler is separate evidence for the ideal of kingship.

Group 2 narrative

The eulogy in the Chimakurti plates associated with Vema Reddy connects religious patronage with material facilities for pilgrims. It credits him with steps on approaches to Sriparvata and Ahobila, feeding houses at sacred centres, and grants to agraharas along the Gundlakamma, Krishna and Godavari. Pilgrims travelling through hills, forests and river routes needed steps, secure paths and food. Religious endowment thus extended beyond the sanctum to the infrastructure through which a shrine was reached.

Such works brought merit and fame to the ruler while linking distant parts of the kingdom with major sacred centres. A feeding house required grain, cooks, water and continuing income; steps required local maintenance after construction. The eulogy celebrates the donor and the work but does not independently establish how long every establishment operated or how many people it served. It nevertheless demonstrates that facilitating pilgrimage was understood as a royal religious responsibility.

Group 1 analysis

A pilgrimage route was an economic and political route as well as a sacred landscape. Merchants, monastic disciples, performers and carriers of information could use the same roads as pilgrims. Secure paths, river access and feeding stations could increase movement towards a shrine and stimulate markets. Patronage encouraged this circulation while making royal authority visible in distant places.

Agraharas and feeding houses appear as free charity, but rested on transfers of rural surplus. When a portion of village tax or grain was assigned to a feeding house, cultivators’ production became food for pilgrims. Institutional managers collected the resource and converted it into a service. Religious merit, agrarian revenue and administration consequently met within one public work.

Group 2 narrative

According to Hanumantha Rao, judicial business in the Reddy–Nayaka states was heard in *dharmasanas*, with temporary tribunals formed for some particular disputes. Dharmashastra tradition, local custom, testimony and official decision influenced the process. Drawing on contemporary literary descriptions, he also mentions ordeals, corporal penalties, banishment and execution. These references should not be treated as one uniformly applied penal code; the offence, social status and regional custom may have affected a judgment.

Land assessment was a major source of state revenue. The Vilasa inscription cited by Hanumantha Rao mentions one-sixth of the produce as assessment. Villages under royal control could be leased to individuals for payments called *kavulu*, which he says were generally paid in money. Temple and Brahman grants received tax exemptions. Holders of a *dasabandha-manya* were required to contribute one-tenth of the produce towards repairs to water resources. Customs and occupational levies supplied further revenue. Land income, water maintenance and local administration were therefore closely connected.

Group 1 analysis

A dharmasana was not equivalent to a modern independent court. Royal or regional authority supplied jurisdiction, learned advisers interpreted normative tradition, and local elders contributed knowledge of custom. Ordeal reflected a legal culture seeking divine judgment where material proof was limited, not a modern evidentiary procedure. Justice was not necessarily independent of social status.

*Dasabandha-manya* illustrates the union of right and obligation. It joined enjoyment of grant land to a payment of one-tenth of the produce for repairs to water resources, thereby attempting to provide a regular basis for irrigation maintenance. The stated rule alone does not prove that payment and repair occurred consistently or that water was distributed equally. A tax exemption for a temple or Brahman grant likewise did not remove the state’s need for revenue; other taxes or taxable lands may have carried that need.

Group 2 narrative

In Hanumantha Rao’s reconstruction of the succession, the child Kumaragiri II was installed at Rajamahendravaram after Kataya Vema’s death, while Allada Reddy governed as guardian. When the prince died around 1416, Anitalli—Kataya Vema’s daughter and Allada’s daughter-in-law—became the heir. Her husband Virabhadra Reddy occupied the throne, Allada continued to direct affairs, and Virabhadra’s elder brother Vema later carried major responsibilities. Inscriptions cited by Hanumantha Rao describe both brothers as rulers.

Authority did not pass simply from one individual to another. Anitalli’s hereditary claim, the marriage joining two families, Allada’s guardianship and the shared activity of Virabhadra and Vema sustained government at Rajamahendravaram. A woman’s inheritance stood at the centre of legitimacy, although everyday military and administrative authority appears largely in the hands of male relatives.

Group 1 analysis

Reducing Anitalli to a ruler’s wife obscures her place in succession. As Kataya Vema’s daughter, she transmitted the eastern branch’s hereditary claim, even though evidence for decisions personally exercised in her name remains limited. Her case reveals a gendered structure in which female descent supplied legitimacy while male guardian, husband and brother-in-law dominated executive power.

Descriptions of both brothers as kings do not necessarily indicate modern co-rule with equal powers. Title, grant authority, military command and presence at the capital could be distributed differently. The issuing person, place and purpose of each inscription define the relationship more precisely. Shared family authority nevertheless provided a political solution that preserved the eastern state through crisis.

Group 2 narrative

The Kandukur line associated with Malla Reddy and his descendants became a distinct Reddy centre in southern coastal Andhra. Hanumantha Rao describes Malla as Prolaya Vema’s commander-in-chief, his representative in the Boyavihara region and the commander who captured Motupalli. The same account credits Malla’s grandson Srigiri with patronage of music and literature and with excavating tanks for agricultural development. On this evidence, Kandukur developed as an agrarian and cultural centre as well as a military base.

According to Hanumantha Rao, Komati Reddy II used conflict within Kondavidu to expand towards the coast and Udayagiri. Pedakomati Vema recovered the coastal territory, while the Vijayanagara ruler Devaraya brought the Udayagiri region under his control. In the time of Komati Reddy III, the Kandukur Reddys became Vijayanagara subordinates. A line that had risen independently thus continued its regional existence as a subordinate state under a stronger neighbouring empire.

Group 1 analysis

Kandukur cannot be treated merely as an appendage of the principal Reddy kingdom. Proximity to Motupalli, southern coastal routes and territory towards Udayagiri gave it strategic value. Hanumantha Rao’s statements that Srigiri excavated tanks and patronised music and literature suggest an effort to strengthen both agrarian resources and cultural prestige. The evidence thus presents Kandukur not only as a fort but as a subregional centre that consolidated its own authority.

Subordination to Vijayanagara did not necessarily extinguish all autonomy. A Kandukur ruler could retain local revenue and fort administration while supplying tribute or military service to an overlord. Such a relationship could result from defeat, but could also preserve lineage and local rights through political accommodation.

Group 2 narrative

Rachavema succeeded Pedakomati Vema at Kondavidu. According to the Aminabad inscription cited by Hanumantha Rao, Rachavema excavated the Jaganobbaganda canal to supply Santana Sagara, the reservoir built by his mother Surambika. The inscription thus records a specific irrigation work of his reign. By adding a canal to Surambika’s reservoir, mother and son contributed across two generations to the same water system.

The Kondavidu kaifiyat preserves a different memory. It says that Rachavema imposed oppressive levies including a “birth tax” and was killed by the Balija leader Savaram Ellayya. This is a later local tradition, not a narrative whose every element has contemporary epigraphic confirmation. Kondavidu weakened and most of its territory passed under Vijayanagara in the 1420s. Gajapati expansion affected coastal Andhra only in the later phase and should not be projected back as a contemporary cause of Rachavema’s fall.

Group 1 analysis

The Aminabad inscription and Kondavidu kaifiyat portray the same ruler differently. In the inscription Rachavema undertakes irrigation; in the kaifiyat he provokes hostility through taxation. The first officially records a specific construction, while the second shows how a later community gave moral meaning to a ruler’s fall. Neither source cancels the other; the evidentiary character of each determines its historical use.

The birth-tax story may preserve a memory of an actual levy or may have developed as a symbol of oppressive rule. Without a contemporary order or independent record, it cannot be asserted as a securely documented kingdom-wide tax. It nevertheless reveals local political ideas about fiscal justice, royal popularity and social vengeance.

Group 2 narrative

Successor states did not acquire legitimacy through military victory alone. The Vilasa grant represented Prolaya as restoring a disrupted social-religious order. Reddy rulers fashioned reputations as donors and protectors through support for shrines, routes, scholars and poets. Recherla rulers combined fortress victory with Sanskrit learning and performance patronage. A grant was both a transfer of resources and a political statement that rule benefited recognised institutions.

Alliances did not follow an unchanging religious divide. The Recherlas cooperated at different times with the Bahmanis and Vijayanagara, while rival Reddy branches sought different external supporters. Security, forts, revenue territory and succession could outweigh shared religious identity in immediate decisions. The period cannot therefore be reduced either to permanent Hindu unity or uninterrupted religious war.

Group 1 analysis

Legitimacy operated at two levels. Force supplied effective command of a fort; donation, genealogy, restoration rhetoric and literary praise created a language in which that command appeared rightful. By supporting temples, agraharas, monasteries and poets, a ruler claimed inheritance of recognised social values. Eulogistic restoration did not, however, prove equal justice for every group.

Changing alliances reveal political pragmatism. Religion shaped devotion, patronage and the language of conflict, but did not alone determine policy. Regional states used settlement, marriage, tribute and transfer of forts to survive among stronger neighbours. Political calculation and cultural legitimacy operated simultaneously.

Group 2 narrative

Srinatha’s career shows literature moving among politically competing courts. Kondavidu rulers, ministers, merchants such as Avachi Tippaya and monastic leaders supported him in different settings. Gifts, dedication, learned debate and pilgrimage carried a poet beyond one dynasty’s frontier. Sanskrit drama and poetics at Rachakonda, Telugu-Sanskrit writing at Kondavidu and patronage at Rajamahendravaram were not isolated islands, but an interconnected intellectual world through which scholars, manuscripts and narratives circulated.

Kumaragiri’s dance patronage and the memory of Lakumadevi reveal interaction between textual theory and living performance. A spring festival required trained performers, musicians, costumes, a stage and material resources. Treatises supplied learned language for movement, emotion and form, while performers animated these through local traditions and engagement with audiences. Political fragmentation could create competing patronage and opportunities for specialised cultural production.

Group 1 analysis

Poetic mobility was more than changing employment. A poet carried a patron’s fame elsewhere, introduced forms, vocabulary and shrine narratives, participated in debate and enlarged courtly prestige. The patron supplied resources, while the poet supplied literary memory lasting beyond the patron’s lifetime. Such reciprocity sustained intellectual connection despite political hostility.

Competing courts offered reward, title and festival opportunity to attract performers. This enabled artistic specialisation but could leave livelihoods unstable when patrons fell, wars intervened or travel became dangerous. Cultural production depended on royal support while also reflecting the agency of poets, dancers, musicians, copyists and merchant donors.
